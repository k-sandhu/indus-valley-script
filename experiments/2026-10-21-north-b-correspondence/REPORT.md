# Does northern *b-* answer Tamil *m-*? Report of NORTH-B-CORRESPONDENCE-2026-10-21

Across the whole *Dravidian Etymological Dictionary* (DEDR), Kurukh and Malto *b-* answers Tamil *m-* in one entry besides 4876, so prediction P5 of conjecture [C-0011](../../knowledge/conjectures/C-0011-proto-dravidian-min-fish-and-star.md) is unresolved under its frozen rule and the credence stays at 0.20. The count itself points one way. Where Tamil begins a word with *m-*, Kurukh and Malto begin it with *m-* in 83 of 88 comparisons and with *b-* in two, both in the word for "crocodile" (entry 4952). Where Tamil has *v-*, the northern languages have *b-* in 50 of 55. In the setting P5 proposes, a Tamil *m-* followed by a long front vowel, all six northern reflexes keep *m-*, among them Malto *mínu* "fish", the very word the pun needs. Malto keeps "fish" (*mínu*) and "star" (*bínḍke*) apart by their initials, and its star word has the initial and vowel of its word for "sky" (*biṉye*, entry 5396). We propose lowering C-0011 to 0.15 after review.

## Question and design

Parpola reads the Indus fish sign as "star" through the Dravidian pun *mīn* "fish" and *mīn* "star". DEDR records the star sense in entry 4876, headed by Tamil *miṉ* "to flash, glitter", and two of its witnesses are the North Dravidian star words Kurukh *bīnkō* and Malto *bínḍke*. On the dated tree of Kolipakam and colleagues (2018) these two words carry "star" to the North Dravidian node with probability 0.681 ([root-meanings report](../2026-10-21-dravidian-root-meanings/REPORT.md)). Both begin with *b-*, which in the entries that study coded answers Tamil *v-*, and DEDR closes 4876 with a cross-reference to 5396, Tamil *viṇ* "sky". P5 asks whether northern *b-* continues Proto-Dravidian *m-* anywhere else. It passes if at least two entries other than 4876 show undoubted Kurukh or Malto *b-* for Tamil *m-* (credence 0.30), and fails if none shows any such form (credence 0.10).

We counted, for every DEDR entry with a Tamil line whose first form begins with *m-* or *v-* and with a Kurukh or Malto form, the initial of the first undoubted Kurukh and Malto form: *m*, *b*, *v* (v or w) or another letter. The unit is one language in one entry part (an entry with lettered sub-entries has several parts). Forms DEDR marks as borrowed ("<", "borrowed", "loan") were dropped. Forms it marks as doubtful with "?" were counted separately. The vowel after the initial and whether a nasal follows it were recorded, along with Brahui as a secondary count and the Telugu, Kannada and Gondi initials of each crossing entry. The [protocol](PROTOCOL.md) fixed these rules and the pass rule at 18:41 UTC on 21 October, before any entry was read for the study. We then wrote the parser and checked it against the flattened source text label by label. Five parse corrections, none of which changes a counting rule, are recorded in [PARSE-CHECK.md](PARSE-CHECK.md); the most consequential recovers lines whose labels DSAL set without italics and keeps apart two different entries that DSAL prints under the number 5282. The code was frozen by hash at 18:57 ([FREEZE.json](FREEZE.json)), passed its 24 synthetic checks ([selftest](selftest-v1/selftest.json)), and computed the counts once ([results.json](results-v1/results.json)). A second implementation written from the protocol alone reproduces the outcome and every entry list, and corrects one cell of the table (see Verification).

DEDR is the only witness. Its DSAL edition is all rights reserved, so the 514 pages, fetched on 21 October by REBUS-BASE-RATE-2026-10-21 and checked here against their logged hashes, and every parsed line stay in `data/raw/`. Tracked files hold counts, entry numbers and classes. A Kurukh or Malto word that DEDR did not record, or placed in an entry without a Tamil line, is outside the count.

## Verification

A subagent wrote a second implementation from the protocol and [PARSE-CHECK.md](PARSE-CHECK.md) without seeing our code or results ([verify.py](verify/verify.py), [verification-v1.json](verify/verification-v1.json), [notes](verify/VERIFY-NOTES.md)). It gives the same outcome and the same three entry lists, the same population of 63 and 44 entries, and the same counts in every cell but one. It finds the Kurukh line of entry 4886 doubtful, as DEDR marks it with a "?" before the label. Our frozen parser lost that mark because an unclosed bold tag earlier in the entry made it read the preceding gloss, "?" included, as a form. The corrected Tamil *m-* row for Kurukh is 45 *m-*, 1 *b-* and 2 other, with six units that have only doubtful forms. The frozen file keeps the original value, and no *b-* or *m-* count changes. Under seven alternative readings of points the protocol leaves open, the second implementation's lists and outcome stay the same. It also notes two facts about the "crocodile" entry. After the comparison slash, DEDR cites Sanskrit lexical *mācala-* "crocodile" and *musali-* "house-lizard; alligator" and Prakrit *muduga-*. And its Kurukh form comes from a single source marked (BB), a mark that opens no other Kurukh line in the dictionary. The protocol's borrowing rule does not read the comparisons after the slash.

## What the result means

The count asks whether Kurukh *bīnkō* and Malto *bínḍke* can continue a Proto-Dravidian **mīn-* "star" by a regular sound change. They cannot be shown to. The northern languages answer Tamil *m-* with *m-* almost without exception, and in the long front vowel of *mīn* they do so in all six cases the dictionary offers. The decisive pair is in Malto itself: *mínu* "fish" (entry 4885) and *bínḍke* "star" (4876) share the vowel and the following nasal, so any change that turned **m-* into *b-* before *īn* would have changed the fish word as well. The one other entry with *b-* for Tamil *m-*, "crocodile", has a back vowel and no nasal, so it gives P5 no support in P5's own setting. It is also a word that DEDR compares with Sanskrit lexical forms, which makes spread by contact a live possibility. Northern *b-* is the regular reflex of Tamil *v-*, and Malto *biṉye* "sky", in the entry DEDR cross-refers from 4876, shows it before the same *i* and nasal as the star word.

Two observations keep the alternative open. Gondi *mīnkō*, in the same entry 4876, has the same ending as Kurukh *bīnkō*, and DEDR places the northern words beside it under *miṉ* "to shine". And before a nasal the correspondence is less fixed than elsewhere. Kurukh *mennā* and Malto *mene* "to hear" have *m-* where Tamil, Telugu, Gondi and Brahui point to **w-* (5516), so a northern **w-* could become *m-* before a nasal. That is the mirror image of what P5 needs, and it widens rather than closes the gap between the star word and the fish word.

Brahui shows a *b-* for Tamil *m-* before front vowels three times, in words where Kurukh and Malto keep *m-*. That is a Brahui development, and Brahui has no form in entry 4876, so it does not bear on the star words.

## Credence

The frozen rule leaves C-0011 at 0.20: one entry outside 4876 qualifies, so P5 neither passes nor fails. The protocol allows review to lower the credence. We propose 0.15. When the root-meanings study set 0.20, the northern star words were still open between *miṉ* "to shine" and *viṇ* "sky". The count now weighs against *miṉ*: the change P5 proposes is absent in its own setting (zero of six), Malto separates star from fish by the initial, and the only regular source of northern *b-* is the *v-* of *viṇ*. Treating the Kurukh and Malto star words as belonging to *viṇ* lowers the root probability of "star" in entry 4876 to between 0.34 and 0.47 (the root-meanings study's exploratory reconstruction, missing or coded 0). The star sense of "to shine" then reaches beyond South I only through Gondi, and the homophone with "fish" is a South Dravidian pun. The kill condition still fails, because Gondi is a South II language with the star word in *m-*. The proposed record entry is in [CARD-PROPOSAL.md](CARD-PROPOSAL.md).

## Boldest reading and the test that decides it

North Dravidian named the star after the sky. Kurukh *bīnkō* and Malto *bínḍke* are **wiṇ-* "sky" with a suffix in *-k-*, as Malto *biṉye* is the sky word itself. The star word of the north was never homophonous with *mīn* "fish", which Malto keeps as *mínu*. The fish–star pun belongs to the South Dravidian languages and Gondi, where the star is "the shining one", *mīn*. If Harappan writers drew a fish to write "star", their language was of that southern group, and an early South Dravidian language was spoken in the Indus valley in the third millennium BCE.

The test that decides it is the morphology of the northern star words in sources independent of DEDR. If a Malto grammar or dictionary (Droese 1884, Mahapatra 1979) segments *bínḍke* as the sky base *biṉ-* with a productive suffix, or a Kurukh dictionary (Hahn 1903, Grignard 1924) has a base *bīn-* "sky" or "heaven", the northern star is a sky word and the reading stands. If neither language has such a base, and the *-kō* of Kurukh *bīnkō* is the plural or formative that Gondi *mīnkō* shows, the star word is one inherited formation in **mīn-k-* whose northern *b-* is an irregularity shared by Kurukh and Malto. In that case P4 of C-0011, a *mīn*-form for "star" in a North or Central language recorded outside DEDR, is the datum that decides the claim.
