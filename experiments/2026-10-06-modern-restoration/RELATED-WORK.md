# Restoration precedents and comparable outcomes

The published Indus and ancient-text studies establish n-gram restoration, recurrent restoration, transformer restoration, duplicate controls and transfer-learning tests. This package compares specified estimators on the repository's preserved 8,009 single-code masks. Its numbers are measurements on that population; the publications below use other target alphabets, splits or mask distributions. Exact bibliographic records, opened-source status and source locations are in [sources.json](sources.json).

Yadav, Joglekar, Rao, Vahia, Adhikari and Mahadevan's 2010 *Statistical Analysis of the Indus Script Using n-Grams*, *PLoS ONE* 5(3): e9506, retains one copy of each repeated text in the 1,548-text EBUDS corpus. Five 80:20 folds repeat random deletions 100 times. Its 74% sensitivity credits the set of signs within 90% cumulative predicted probability, as stated in the cross-validation section and Figure 14. This package's 39.7178% n-gram top-one and 68.7601% top-ten use one and ten candidates respectively. Neither is the published 90%-mass event, so the figures do not establish a performance ranking. [Publisher full text](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506).

Rao, Yadav, Vahia, Joglekar, Adhikari and Mahadevan's 2009 *A Markov model of the Indus script*, *PNAS* 106(33): 13685–13690, already predicts artificially deleted and unclear signs. PDF page 4 cites the approximately 74% cross-validation result whose event is detailed in the 2010 paper. The current first-order comparator uses unigram-prior smoothing, while this package implements continuation counts and separate modified Kneser–Ney discounts with a finite-vocabulary floor. The outputs compare estimators on the repository's fixed benchmark. [Author PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/PNASIndus.pdf).

Venkatesh and Farghaly's 2023 *Statistical models for identifying missing and unclear signs of the Indus script*, *Journal of Emerging Investigators* 6, uses 2,223 unique ICIT texts and an 80:20 train/test split. Its body reports about 35% exact sign match, 57% top-five and 63% top-ten on repeated random deletions. The abstract's approximately 63% therefore needs its top-ten definition. The results section discusses choosing n-gram orders on the test dataset. The current experiment masks every benchmark position once and selects configurations on three inner component folds. Its scores describe a different population and selection procedure. [Article and full PDF](https://emerginginvestigators.org/articles/22-256), PDF pages 2, 4–6 and Table 2.

Tanishk Tiwari's 2026 *Statistical Structure in Indus Sign Sequences*, NLP4DH, pp. 314–319, reports 6,579 inscriptions, an object-type-stratified 80:20 split, and a BiLSTM next-token assay. Table 4 gives real-test perplexity 2.60 and comparisons to synthetic sequence alterations. The opened methods do not specify an artifact/whole-string transitive split or this package's masked-slot query law. Its perplexity cannot be compared numerically with the conditional bucket losses here. [ACL paper](https://aclanthology.org/2026.nlp4dh-1.28/), Sections 4.1, 4.7 and 5.4.

Assael and colleagues' 2022 *Restoring and attributing ancient texts using deep neural networks*, *Nature* 603: 280–283, introduces Ithaca. It removes 9,441 duplicate texts from I.PHI, which contains 78,608 Greek inscriptions, and partitions by inscription identifiers. Eight transformer blocks combine character and word embeddings. Table 1 reports 61.8% top-one restoration and 26.3% character error rate for artificially hidden spans of 1–10 characters. These known-language spans, longer contexts and word vocabulary differ from single graphic-code masks. The duplicate and split controls are prior practice. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8907065/), Evaluation and Methods.

Assael and colleagues' 2025 *Contextualizing ancient texts with generative neural networks*, *Nature* 645: 141–147, introduces Aeneas. Its Latin corpus removes shared Trismegistos identities and more than 90% fuzzy text matches, then partitions by identifier endings. Table 2 reports 40.5% character error rate and 46.5% top-twenty restoration for known-length gaps; it also evaluates unknown-length gaps. Image inputs and retrieval of epigraphic parallels add observations absent from the small networks fitted here. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09292-5), [author PDF](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/191253/1/s41586-025-09292-5.pdf), PDF pages 5 and 8.

Papavassileiou and Kosmopoulos's 2024 *Restoring Mycenaean Linear B ‘A&B’ series tablets using supervised and transfer learning*, ML4AL, pp. 115–129, is a close small-data recurrent precedent. A bidirectional model on 426 A&B sequences gives 30.28% top-one, 50.23% top-five and 57.75% top-ten under leave-one-out synthetic-gap evaluation. Transfer from the larger D series gives 25.65% or 24.71% top-one and no aggregate improvement in that assay. This motivates testing additional text separately from model capacity; success of transfer depends on the available data and its domain. [ACL paper](https://aclanthology.org/2024.ml4al-1.13/), Tables 3–4 and Section 5.3.

Chen and Goodman's 1998 *An Empirical Study of Smoothing Techniques for Language Modeling*, Harvard technical report TR-10-98, supplies the modified Kneser–Ney discount and continuation-count equations. Section 3, equation 26, distinguishes counts one, two and at least three. The implementation here adds sparse-count fallbacks, clips each discount below its count class, searches a multiplier of 0.75 or 1, and adds 0.5 to bottom continuation counts. These choices are recorded in the [pre-fit protocol](PROTOCOL.md) and each inner fit. [Primary technical report](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-10-98.pdf).
