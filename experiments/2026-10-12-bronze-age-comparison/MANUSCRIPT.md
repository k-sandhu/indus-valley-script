# Short records in four Bronze Age writing traditions: known-category controls and comparisons with the Indus inscriptions

Research manuscript, version 1, 12 October 2026 UTC. Authorship and venue are unassigned. Acquisition, analysis and writing used AI assistance. This is an exploratory comparison with preserved source repairs and numerical endpoints.

## Abstract

Sumerian, Akkadian, Egyptian and Mycenaean Greek provide different documentary comparisons for the Indus inscriptions. We acquire primary digital editions, preserve complete edited objects and natural textual subdivisions as separate populations, and test structural and category-recovery methods against source annotations. The Indus population contains 1,593 distinct complete faces of length 3–12. All eleven testable structural cohorts depart from their within-record position-permutation references after multiplicity adjustment. Known word categories can be predicted on held-out source groups, with substantial contributions from repeated lexical identities. A later sign-level control conceals word boundaries and preserves both successful and insufficient-data cases. Equality-pattern compatibility with 200-entry source spelling inventories covers 91.16–97.13% of the selected Indus faces on average, usually with multiple candidates and independently chosen per-face correspondences. The study supplies source-backed controls for record organization and identifies the observations needed for a more discriminating interpretation. It yields no historical language ranking or authenticated Indus reading. [Claims and exact evidence](CLAIM-EVIDENCE.json).

## 1. Match the work performed by a record

A short inscription can depend on an extensive body of shared knowledge. A writer may record a person, a commodity, an amount or an authorization because the object and its handling supply the remaining context. The useful comparison therefore begins with the activity and carrier, alongside the age of the writing tradition. We use established documentary conventions and specialist administration to make the user's proposed comparison by maturity concrete.

The four traditions serve different roles. Ur III Sumerian accounts and Egyptian Old Kingdom/First Intermediate Period vessel inscriptions provide late third-millennium observations. The available Akkadian editions here belong to Old Babylonian administration and correspondence. Mycenaean Greek provides a later Bronze Age control with economic records and known writing conventions. These collections do not constitute a uniform chronological sample. Their language labels also describe documentary cohorts: Akkadian accounts can contain Sumerian formulae.

Earlier statistical comparisons already included Sumerian. Rao and colleagues' 2009 study compared conditional entropy across IVS, Sumerian, Old Tamil, Vedic Sanskrit and other systems. Its supplementary Sumerian source comprises selected literary compositions. The subsequent debate examined how statistical structure should be interpreted and how comparisons should be chosen. The present investigation changes the observation task to short edited records and source-annotated category calibration. It does not claim first use of cross-language comparison. [Rao et al.](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/ScienceIndus.pdf), [Sproat publication and preserved source-review scope](SOURCES.md).

Mycenaean sequence and automatic-decipherment research supplies further precedents. Papavassiliou, Owens and Kosmopoulos assembled Linear B word/logogram sequences for restoration. Luo, Cao and Barzilay use cognate correspondences to a related known language. Their observation conditions differ from an Indus corpus without established word divisions or a verified related-language reference. [Dataset paper](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.311/), [decipherment paper](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1303/).

## 2. Sources and observation units

The Ur III archive contains 80,181 named text JSON entries. A fixed hash rule selects 3,000; the parser retains 2,986 editions with confirmed Sumerian token-language evidence. The Akkadian collection includes 121 letter editions and 378 named Rīm-Anum corpus entries, of which 40 contain invalid payloads and 338 parse successfully. Completeness is assessed for each edited object/module and separately for its lines. Seals impressed on a tablet retain the parent document association but have their own text sequence. [Source register](SOURCES.md), [census](run-v2/normalization-census.json).

The Egyptian archive supplies lexical tokens and source grammatical annotations. AED-TEI metadata identifies vessels, physical support IDs, dates and the sentence/word extent of an edition. The final complete edited vessel set contains 458 texts before the 3–12-word filter. Agreement across the two transformed source corpora is required for that designation. Tomb-inscription sentences with complete word glyph encodings supply a separate glyph extension. The data distinguish an edited vessel text, an individual sentence and a photographed object's full surface; only the first two have the required machine-readable coverage here.

One inspected Qubbet el Hawa vessel edition, No. 89/136 (259), has a five-token text combining a commodity, named people and kinship terminology. Its source dates span the sixth through eighth dynasties, with stored negative-year bounds −2363 and −2187. This is a concrete short-label comparison with material and identity metadata. Fischer's Old Kingdom cylinder-seal study separately supplies examples of royal names and official titles alongside decorative figures. Those seals inform the documentary discussion but are outside the numerical Egyptian vessel cohort. [Observation O03](observations.json), [Fischer](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/old_kingdom_cylinder_seals_for_the_lower_classes_the_metropolitan_museum_journal_v_6_1972.pdf).

LiBER's catalogue supplies 5,638 entries. A fixed site-stratified rule selects 512 for acquisition. The final parser retains the edition's displayed transcription and its search-link form. Searches can normalize away underdots or supplied-letter brackets, so both representations determine certainty. Earlier outcomes using the insufficient query-only check remain preserved. No replacement documents replenish the corrected exclusions. [Selection](cohort-selection-v1.json), [source repair](SOURCE-MARKUP-AMENDMENT.md), [before/after outputs](source-correction-comparison.json).

The Indus comparison reuses the project's pinned observed-face corpus and its full-register physical/text closure. It supplies 1,593 distinct faces of length 3–12, containing 7,970 Wells codes. No source meaning or word boundary is supplied to that population.

## 3. Shared methods and limits

Complete edited objects, seal modules, natural lines and sentences form separate cohorts. Structural tests use distinct sequences and retain lengths and unit multisets. Cuneiform sign readings, grouped numbers, Greek syllables/logograms, Egyptian glyphs and Egyptian editorial words have different encodings. Their inventories and statistics are reported with those units rather than converted into a common linguistic scale.

Known-category calibration uses source training tags and anonymous identity/context features. The hidden information is the held-out answer and readable lexical identity. The source categories are broad word/POS classes, including several kinds of named entities, nominal forms, verbs, functions and numbers. Greek supplies a different three-class writing key. Thus its score concerns syllabic spellings, logograms and numerals, rather than personal names. Physical/source aliases and repeated complete sequences close into groups before five-fold evaluation. Test groups and exact sequences cannot appear in training.

The principal inferential limits concern observation equivalence. The corpora differ in genre, period, representation, completeness, annotation and vocabulary coverage. Some readings represent more than one physical glyph identity, and source word divisions are editor-provided observations. Category calibration tests the declared predictor against those source keys; it does not recover the language of an unfamiliar collection. The lexical construction supplies separate per-face compatibility witnesses without a consistent global key. These shared conditions govern the interpretation of every result below. [Detailed methods and exact formulas](METHODS.md).

## 4. Ordering survives the record-specific controls

The structural statistic measures how strongly frequent units prefer positions away from the normalized sequence center. Units need at least five occurrences. There are 1,999 within-record permutations, with an exact finite-population expectation checked separately.

All eleven testable cohorts reject the position-allocation reference after Holm adjustment across the complete family. One tiny Akkadian seal cohort lacks a frequent type and has no testable statistic. The IVS statistic is 0.059784 against a reference mean of 0.003253. Sumerian seals, Sumerian complete edited tablets, Egyptian vessel texts and Greek complete documents also retain position concentration under their own references. Their differences reflect the particular populations and encodings being compared. [Exact table](TABLES.md#structured-short-records), [numeric endpoint](run-v2/structural-results.json).

This result establishes that the selected ordering is unusual under a length/multiset-preserving allocation. Known labels and accounts can produce that observation while serving different social functions. The comparison therefore gives candidate models a documented environment in which to test record organization. It does not select which known collection supplies the Indus vocabulary.

## 5. Known categories: lexical memory and contextual information

The complete edited Sumerian word-control population has 759 records in 514 closed groups, with 3,806 scored tokens. Context/identity/position prediction reaches 90.41% micro accuracy, compared with 81.79% for identity alone and 69.73% for position/length. Its balanced accuracy is 66.49%; rare category support, including one FUNCTION target, matters to that measure. Among 760 unseen identities, context accuracy is 70.26%. The outcome indicates that the surrounding record can add information beyond a remembered spelling under these source categories.

The Egyptian vessel word population contains 302 records in 288 groups and 1,202 scored targets. Context accuracy is 97.00%, identity-only accuracy 95.09%, and positional accuracy 67.30%. Most identities are already represented in training; the unseen-ID subset contains 60 targets. The strong overall result therefore partly reflects recurring lexical forms in this vessel collection. Source labels and repeated words are valuable observations, but their availability must accompany the reported success.

Greek's corrected complete word/logogram population contains 25 records and 143 targets. Context reaches 83.92%, compared with 69.23% for identity and 65.73% for position. Its three source classes have balanced context accuracy 78.03%. The complete Akkadian word cohort has eight groups and 50 scored targets; context and identity both score 50%, with weaker balanced context performance. That small outcome remains part of the record.

The extensions clarify why more rows do not automatically create a larger independent test. The Sumerian word-line extension has 15,436 rows, but 12,563 belong to one closed component. One fold carries 55,488 of its 68,322 scored tokens. Other extension outcomes also show that adding context can lose micro accuracy against identity alone. These measurements retain their grouping and class distributions rather than becoming a single headline score. [Word results and class support](run-v2/category-results.json), [tables](TABLES.md#word-category-calibration).

## 6. Predicting categories without supplied word boundaries

The initial word control has an observation advantage over IVS. A post-outcome amendment therefore expands the source word categories over their constituent signs/readings, then hides word boundaries from the predictor. The same identity, context, position, smoothing and group rules are used. Length 3–12 now counts units rather than words, creating different eligible populations. These scores are assessed against their own baselines; their differences from the word table are not a measured causal effect of boundary deletion.

In the Sumerian complete sign/readings cohort, identity-only prediction slightly exceeds contextual micro accuracy, while context improves balanced recall. The Egyptian glyph-sentence extension reaches 69.38% context accuracy against 64.48% identity-only accuracy; its balanced recall remains 48.23%. This is a different Egyptian documentary and encoding population from the vessel-word control. Greek complete unit sequences reach 91.37% context accuracy against 82.74% for both majority and identity; the task still distinguishes syllabic, logographic and numerical units. The complete Akkadian sign-length cohort has four groups and cannot support five folds. Its line extension is evaluated separately. [Amendment](AMENDMENT-SIGN-CONTROL.md), [corrected endpoint](sign-control-v2/results.json), [table](TABLES.md#categories-with-word-boundaries-concealed).

These controls expose two useful targets for further research. First, a predictor should improve on remembering frequent unit identities within the same held-out population. Second, it should retain category recall when the most common class no longer dominates the score. Source annotation coverage and grouping remain part of both tests.

## 7. Many lexical matches under a weak correspondence rule

The lexical construction considers 1,522 distinct IVS faces of length 3–8. Their strings reduce to 47 equality patterns: whether units repeat, and in which positions. Candidate spelling inventories retain complete observed word forms in their native reading/glyph units. Each face/candidate pair may choose its own correspondence.

Natural inventories contain 5,918 Sumerian-cohort spellings, 2,885 Akkadian-document spellings, 2,016 Egyptian spellings and the smaller Greek inventory recorded in the corrected endpoint. To reduce the effect of entry count, the test samples 200 entries from each inventory 500 times. Mean compatible fractions are 92.02%, 97.13%, 96.93% and 91.16%, respectively. Multiple-candidate fractions also remain high. The correspondence rule has therefore preserved very little information capable of separating these documentary sources. [Exact counts, ambiguity and subset intervals](run-v2/lexical-ambiguity.json).

A shared-key requirement is a different constraint. The complete target has 536 distinct units. The observed spelling inventories provide 899, 566, 362 and the smaller Greek set of reference units. An injection into the two smaller observed inventories fails the cardinality bound. That rejects the particular literal-injection construction into those incomplete inventories, not the historical languages. Passing the bound for the two cuneiform inventories likewise supplies no compatible global solution.

The previous Sanskrit window searches use dictionary letters, selected stem values and bounded joins. They do not share the native script-unit and segmentation conditions of this construction. A comparative phonetic test would need one fixed mapping model, equally specified spelling freedoms, a dated lexical scope and risky withheld predictions. Pooling the existing scores would conceal those differences.

## 8. What the comparisons make testable

The source examples support several record models worth testing against Indus objects: personal or institutional identity; commodity/recipient labels; quantity/transfer records; and mixed phonographic, logographic and numerical notation. Their distinguishing observations concern independently identified carriers, contents, people, issuing groups, amounts and use histories. The prior project's repeated whole-object designs and variable tall-stroke modules give a starting frame for such tests. [Existing whole-object study](../2026-10-11-two-approaches/administrative/REPORT.md).

The next comparison should strengthen correspondence between observation units. Source-reviewed physical glyphs can replace ambiguous reading aliases; genuinely short Akkadian seals/accounts can strengthen its complete-record population; richer Greek annotations can test named entities; and matched source objects can isolate the information supplied by word boundaries. The designs in [NEXT-TESTS.md](NEXT-TESTS.md) identify the required observations and outcomes that would weaken each approach.

The completed contribution is a set of acquired source frames, explicit representation rules, known-category controls and reproducible ambiguity measurements. It establishes which tasks work under available annotations and which collections fail to provide the promised observation. That record can guide the next investigation without replacing a failed or insufficient endpoint.

## 9. Verification and preservation

The first normalization failure, successful censuses, pre-score representation repairs, first statistical run and post-result Greek source correction remain preserved. The successor changes no non-Greek numeric cohort outcome. A separate Counter implementation replays sampled held-out predictions across both endpoints; the structural checker independently derives observed values and the exact shuffle expectation. These checks concern implementation and preserved source scope. The [verification files](README.md#verification) state their actual coverage.

Our code, prose, aggregates and source pointers form the reusable package. Acquired corpora, source photographs, per-record outputs and identity maps stay local. The [retrospective](RETROSPECTIVE.md) records externally observable source problems, corrections and results. No CI/CD, recurring automation, external submission or repository publication was added.
