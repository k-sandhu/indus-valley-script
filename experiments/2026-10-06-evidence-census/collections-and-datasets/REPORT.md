# Findings

The evidence is split across museum catalogues, publication-based image corpora and derivative datasets. Some official portals expose detailed single-object records. No source inspected in this pass yielded a complete, current, public inventory of inscribed Indus objects across institutions. The registers distinguish a catalogue search from an object hit and distinguish both from a museum's overall holdings.

## Museum records and fields

The register contains 37 institutions or institution groups, including all named South Asian, European, North American, Gulf and Central Asian targets in the package, plus Bharat Kala Bhavan and Cambridge's Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, which appear in a secondary museum directory. Batched live web searches surfaced official object pages for the Lothal Archaeological Museum in NMMA, CSMVS Mumbai, Indian Museum Kolkata, the British Museum, Louvre, Met and Cleveland Museum of Art, as well as comparator records at Spurlock Museum and Smithsonian. The National Museum of India also exposes a Harappan gallery and selected object pages. Search access is not equivalent to a catalogue-wide Indus filter: the National Museum's official page describes a gallery and shows selected objects, while the museum's 2018 government profile reports over **3,500 Harappan objects** on permanent loan from ASI. That is a broader holding count, not an online-inscription total ([official collection page](https://www.nationalmuseumindia.gov.in/en/harappan-civilization), [Ministry of Culture profile](https://www.indiaculture.gov.in/sites/default/files/Treasures_of_Indian_Museums/NationalMesuemDelhi_12.09.2018.pdf)). Rows with `not separately queried` in `search_status` remain follow-up targets and contribute no count evidence.

Only one numeric museum search result was captured directly: the British Museum's Indus Valley Civilisation term page lists **724 related objects**. The category spans types beyond inscribed artifacts. Harappa.com's secondary museum directory reports 94 results for Indus Valley at the Met, but that figure was not revalidated against the current Met query. The sample BM object 1892,1210.1 records Harappa as findspot, steatite, dimensions, date and bibliography. The Met's pages show object number, medium, date and dimensions; Met policy marks qualifying data and images Public Domain and its Open Access API exists. The API request failed during this run, so no current query total was retained. Louvre's SB 2425 record identifies Indus script and gives SB/AS/CCO numbers, Susa findspot, Indus origin, date, dimensions, steatite and bibliography. These examples show that one record may carry multiple catalogue identifiers without supplying a CISI join ([British Museum term](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/term/x46239), [British Museum object](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1892-1210-1), [Met object](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/38287), [Louvre object](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010176471), [Harappa.com museum directory](https://www.harappa.com/museums)).

Indian portals expose useful records. NMMA's Lothal record ASM-KML-GJ-1425186 describes a steatite seal with five Harappan letters and records its site museum and context fields. CSMVS record CSMVS IV 2056 gives Mohenjo-daro, date range, fired clay and dimensions. The JATAN Indian Museum record 10382/A7251/Hr4869 identifies a Harappa terracotta seal-amulet with date and dimensions. The sampled pages provide fewer excavation identifiers and bibliographic joins than basic accession, findspot, date, material and dimensions. In the 37-row matrix, accession number and date each appear in 13 rows; photograph availability appears in 12, dimensions/material in 11, findspot in 10, bibliography in 5, and excavation number in none. No row's sampled record confirmed multiple separate views. The matrix records positive fields visible in this bounded sample; blank fields mean not documented here, not absent from the institution's internal records ([NMMA Lothal record](https://nmma.nic.in/nmma/antiqDetail.do?object=32&refId=1425186), [CSMVS record](https://networksofthepast.csmvs.in/object/sealing/), [Indian Museum record](https://www.museumsofindia.gov.in/repository/record/im_kol-10382-A7251-Hr4869-26924)).

The public Museums of India/JATAN portal has item records, and the National Museum of India describes its Harappan gallery. However, a portal-wide object count cannot be converted into a count of Indus inscriptions. The official National Museum count of over 3,500 Harappan objects and the 2023 ICIT count of 4,665 inscribed objects have different scope and provenance; they should not be added or treated as competing census totals.

Several large holdings remain poorly indexed at object level in the sources captured here. National Museum New Delhi's reported 3,500-plus Harappan objects is the clearest numeric holding statement. A secondary Harappa.com museum directory reports about 800 mixed objects displayed at Lothal, including replicas; it does not enumerate inscribed originals. The same directory describes seals at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro site museums, but this pass found no current online item inventory for either museum. Lahore Museum confirms Indus seals in its collection without a searchable object count. Pakistan's national and site-museum catalogues need direct institutional access or a complete published concordance before a holdings count can be stated. For India, NMMA search records and printed ASI catalogues point to objects from Lothal, Kalibangan, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi, while online object counts by site museum remain unknown.

## Datasets, images and reuse

ICIT remains the most comprehensive integrated online resource identified. Its 2023 abstract describes 4,665 inscribed objects, 5,650 texts, 17,966 legible signs, 16,015 photographs/drawings, 29 database tables, and over 1.2 million database entries. The same abstract identifies excavation number, findspot, material, artifact type, preservation, sign direction/completeness, and iconographic fields. These historical database counts are not a current dump or a count of photographs available for unrestricted reuse. The existing landscape work established access-on-request and unresolved bulk-reuse terms; this package reuses that assessment.

CISI volumes and Helsinki editions expose published object photography and identifiers. The 2 October landscape established a CC BY 4.0 route for the 1979 and 1982 editions, but a book-level licence does not automatically clear every photograph or scanned copy for redistribution. ICIT, IM77/RMRL, Lipi, Mayig, epachayan, Hugging Face, Kaggle and Zenodo represent different scopes: source corpus, concordance, derived annotations, synthetic sequences, repositories, or individual scholarly deposits. Counts from those sources use different units and overlap. They must not be summed as object totals. Prior pinned counts for Lipi and Mayig are carried into the register with their original denominators.

The Met provides an Open Access route for records and images it marks public domain. Cleveland Museum of Art allows reuse of designated public-domain images and offers collection metadata through its API. Brooklyn Museum exposes per-object rights statements and a search filter for Creative Commons or no-known-copyright images. British Museum Collection Online permits spreadsheet download of searches up to 10,000 records; its image rights vary by asset, non-commercial images may be free to use, and its 3D photography uses CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 terms. These policies provide reuse routes for eligible items, not blanket clearance for every Indus object photograph ([Met record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/38287), [Cleveland Open Access](https://www.clevelandart.org/open-access), [Brooklyn rights guidance](https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/en-GB/image-services), [British Museum search/download guide](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/collection-online/guide), [British Museum terms](https://www.britishmuseum.org/terms-use/copyright-and-permissions)). Harappa.com credits source photographers and institutions, and its pages combine primary excavation documentation with reproduced publications. Sketchfab, RTI and other 3D/photo archives were not exhaustively enumerated; no archive-wide object count or blanket licence was established. Raw third-party captures remain local-only. The package itself does not redistribute image payloads.

Searches also surfaced 2024–2026 repository and Zenodo releases. One Zenodo 2025 record advertises a 417-glyph proposed lexical/function framework under CC BY-NC 4.0; it is a derived interpretive release, not new archaeological observation. The epachayan repository README names a Zenodo DOI and a 179-seal CISI-derived file, but its full release payload and rights were not audited. No newly surfaced release was verified as a complete, rights-cleared museum-to-corpus object register.

## Paired sample audit

The planned sample requires museum and corpus records joined by an explicit accession, excavation, CISI or published concordance key. **Zero of 30 pairs** passed that rule in this pass. Several museum records provide useful accession or excavation identifiers, and the earlier landscape supplies corpus records, but no 30 common museum/CISI pairs were validated. The audit file preserves all 30 unfilled slots and the join rule. No visual resemblance was used to manufacture a match. A valid 30-record comparison needs CISI volume indexes or a current ICIT export together with museum catalogue records and permission-aware image access.

## Scope and limits

Core searches were run live on 6 October 2026, with regional and bibliographic follow-up on 7 October. The search/open browser returned official page content for a subset of records. Local PowerShell requests to six official museum URLs failed during transfer, including the Met API; their failures are listed in the local raw directory. Search-result snippets are marked as such in `sources.json`. No complete raw page/API responses were recovered locally, so the raw folder preserves query logs, limited field extracts and failure reports rather than full response bodies. Fifteen institution rows have no separately executed direct catalogue query; four of those have only a secondary museum-directory reference. Three more were included in a live search batch with no relevant catalogue count. Other rows surfaced a source page or record without an institution-wide total. Null counts do not mean zero holdings. The data support a coverage map and an explicit list of next retrieval targets, not a claim that all findable evidence is indexed.
