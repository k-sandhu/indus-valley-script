# Comparisons that can sharpen a persistence theory

11 October 2026. Proposed extensions following the exploratory survey. No comparison below has been fitted to archaeological data. The omission assessments apply to the declared readings in [SOURCES.json](SOURCES.json); a claim about prevalence across a field requires systematic sampling. [COMPARISONS.json](COMPARISONS.json) gives the measures, rival explanations and rejection conditions.

The defensible opportunity is to connect archaeological observations with an explicit account of continued provision. Maintenance, joint production, institutional diversity, cross-scale change and practical memory already have close predecessors. Sraffa and von Neumann model interdependent reproduction; Martin separates present acceptability from viable trajectories; Cavdaroglu and colleagues integrate repair selection and scheduling; Pichancourt and colleagues connect coupled pathways with institutional action. Jervis already interprets invisible maintenance and recommends joining material context with network analysis. The proposed work is an archaeological procedure drawing these ideas together. [L17, L16, L24, L27, L11, L26]

## Compare present service with capacity for its next renewal

A working asset may persist beyond an interruption to training, procurement or repair access. This suggests a test of whether observed service and its replacement route change at different times. The relevant comparison is between a facility's continued usable output and independently dated evidence about replacements, teachers, materials or work crews. Counts of surviving facilities alone describe only part of that relation.

The mechanism follows viability and material reproduction. An informative addition would estimate how long existing assets can continue under an independently supported wear law, then ask whether required renewal fits the available capacity. Martin's selected model explicitly places control-change costs outside its chosen resilience cost; expanding the boundary would count the reproduction of those providers. Evidence of continued service after a supposed total loss of every renewal route would reject that specific reconstruction, directing attention to imports, substitutes or errors in chronology. It would not identify a hidden institution by itself. [O-L24-1–3, O-L17-1]

## Compare average resources with obligations due together

An expansion can create several assets of similar age. If their working lives overlap, their renewal dates could converge on the same seasonal labor or transport window. Mean annual expenditure could conceal this conflict. A settlement with a heterogeneous stock might have a more manageable schedule despite using comparable total resources. This proposed archaeological comparison needs construction and renewal intervals precise enough to resolve overlap, plus independently bounded task duration and shared inputs.

Joint scheduling is already an operations-research problem, and Pichancourt's recent framework includes coupled institutional interventions. The extension is to ask whether dated archaeological operations support a common schedule and whether synchronized acquisition predicts synchronized replacement. Broad phases that admit many possible schedules would leave the test unresolved. Reuse, different wear rates and staggering construction are concrete alternative explanations. [O-L27-1–2, O-L11-1]

## Compare provider counts with independently available routes

Two workshops or ports can depend on the same quarry, skilled supplier or transport corridor. Apparent diversity can therefore coexist with one prerequisite. Conversely, different-looking products can use interchangeable local techniques. Priß's route graphs and Jervis's proposed material/network integration already provide a comparative basis. To test independence, reconstructed connections need sourcing, technical and temporal evidence for the inputs they carry.

A useful pilot would compare two provider networks with similar visible size but different independently supported upstream dependencies. Its endpoint should first be whether alternative operations remain feasible after removal of a specified prerequisite. A claim about historical failure risk would additionally need evidence about disturbance and response. Finding genuinely separate input and expertise routes would weaken the shared-prerequisite explanation. [O-L28-1–2, O-L26-3]

## Compare local continuity with imported support and relocation

Residents can retain useful practices through movement or recruitment while a settlement loses population or institutions. A city can preserve service by importing both goods and the people able to produce them. These mechanisms are already present in resilience, urban provisioning and polycentricity studies. The measurement opportunity is to place several outcome series beside one another: habitation, demographic scale, service access, production techniques, input origins and movement of people.

The root theory's conservation bound can require additional delivery only when demand is bounded below and all baseline supply routes are bounded above in commensurable units. Smith and Lawrence's inspected main text measures or discusses duration and its correlates; it does not supply that combination of quantities. Their articles motivate outcome definitions and comparisons rather than an immediate estimate of renewal. Unknown imports or initial output leave a zero or unresolved lower bound. A positive residual still allows several renewal or acquisition mechanisms. [O-L09-1–3, O-L10-1–3, O-L21-1]

## Compare expertise available now with access to its successors

Population size, teacher access and successful transmission need separate observations. Henrich models an effective interacting pool; Derex and Boyd show that task structure and patterns of contact can change accumulation. Graham and Thrift add apprenticeship and inventive repair. These suggest comparing replacement cadence with the time and conditions required for people to acquire practical competence.

The proposed test would follow a specific operation across dated generations, recording technical variation, learner-related material, input access and recruitment alternatives. Its outcome would be continuation of the defined capability, allowing useful changes in procedure. A replacement method that needs less expertise could preserve service while ending an older craft trajectory. Product disappearance with no evidence about demand, procurement or deposition would leave a skill-loss explanation unresolved. Neither a census threshold nor cultural affiliation follows from this comparison alone. [O-L12-1, O-L13-1–2, O-L22-1]

## Compare technical opportunity with participation and distribution

A feasible shared repair can remain undone when actors lack information, cannot use the service or have incentives to withhold contributions. Ostrom and Anderies already develop these links, while Tainter discusses the distribution and deferral of costs. A reconstructed plan therefore needs an account of access and participation alongside materials and work capacity.

The comparison should track who receives service, who supplies recurring work and how those relations change. It can test household, reciprocal, negotiated, paid and coercive arrangements using their predicted material and documentary traces. An aggregate city outcome may conceal continued service for some residents and losses for others. Lawrence's call for complete settlement systems and Sellberg's cross-scale trade-offs give direct precedents. Demonstrating a workable schedule establishes a technical possibility; participation evidence determines which historical mechanisms can explain its enactment. [O-L07-1–2, O-L08-2, O-L23-1–2, O-L10-3, O-L20-2]

## Build comparisons that can fail

The strongest initial study would select one service and its observation frame before coding a favored renewal explanation. It would include interruptions and uncertain cases, record excavation coverage and dating resolution, and distinguish a new occupation episode from a long continuous one. Outcomes could be represented by intervals or censoring rather than exact lifetimes. Alternative wear laws, supply boundaries and shared-input assignments should be declared before outcome comparison.

Jervis's critique of phased temporal blocks and Lawrence's duration definitions make chronology central to the tests. A schedule effect cannot be resolved when uncertainty spans the entire proposed work window. A material dependency cannot be resolved by counting route lines that may not be contemporaneous. In those cases the useful result is a completed observation account and a missing-measurement list. It directs the next excavation, sourcing study or replica experiment toward evidence capable of discriminating the mechanism. [O-L26-1–2, O-L10-1–2, O-L28-1]
