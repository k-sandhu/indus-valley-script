# How societies keep their capacities available

11 October 2026. A purposive survey of 25 primary works, inspected at the scopes recorded in [SOURCES.json](SOURCES.json), with 45 attributable statements in [OBSERVATIONS.json](OBSERVATIONS.json). Three further records preserve a publisher description and two unavailable leads. The search follows mechanisms and direct predecessors; it does not measure how common a theme is across scholarship. Complete downloads, selected reading and publisher-only access remain separate in the ledger.

A society with a large population and recurring exchange needs ways to keep its required services obtainable as people, tools, supplies and relationships change. The relevant capacities can reside in households, shared facilities, networks of specialists or external providers. Their arrangement depends on the form of life being maintained, the geographical boundary and the period being considered. Food imports can support residents; recruited makers can sustain production; new drainage routes can preserve waste removal. Each route has inputs and participants whose continued availability needs explanation.

This literature supports treating persistence as the continued accomplishment of specified activities. Its strongest contributions concern material reproduction, practical learning, collective action and the preservation or alteration of available responses. Joint replacement feasibility is a synthesis of these mechanisms with close formal predecessors. An archaeological contribution can specify how dated operations, service demand and independent supply bounds would support or reject particular accounts of renewal.

## What continues, and for how long?

Smith and colleagues distinguish occupation, demographic scale, institutions and culture. A place can retain one while changing another. Their 2021 agenda calls for standardized settlement data and theory connecting duration to geography, population and collective action. Archaeological phases and present-day names need separate treatment when they are used to describe continuity. These distinctions give the proposed theory several outcomes to explain, rather than one civilizational longevity score. [Smith et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157985/) [L09; O-L09-1–3]

Lawrence and colleagues' 2025 comparison makes the outcome more concrete. Its duration measure follows substantial permanent occupation until total abandonment, allowing changes of size and importance within an episode. Reoccupation after a gap starts a new episode; ephemeral occupation is excluded. Its methods also explain how regional evidence and dating resolution can conceal short interruptions. The authors ask for complete settlement systems because benefits to a large center could arise alongside costs imposed on smaller settlements. Housing disparity and duration provide comparative observations, while cumulative service output and renewal work remain further measurements. [Lawrence et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12036995/) [L10; O-L10-1–3]

Chase and colleagues describe several Mesoamerican arrangements for urban provisioning: localized reservoirs and intensive cultivation at Caracol, food imports at Chunchucmil and nearby cultivation at Tikal. Their research agenda includes material, landscape and social infrastructure. These author syntheses encourage comparison of successful adaptation and changing institutions, alongside interruptions. They also show why a boundary around city walls can leave important providers outside the account. [Chase et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10400939/) [L21; O-L21-1–2]

Two recent studies already pursue connections between these outcomes and their underlying relations. Priß and colleagues reconstruct period-specific settlement and route networks in the Khabur Valley, then use density, centralization and site counts as proxies for adaptive-cycle properties. Their methods acknowledge difficult route dating, reconstructed missing sites and limits on settlement size and specialization. Jervis examines how yards, buildings and boundaries persist through maintenance, altered materials and changing uses. His conclusion proposes integrating comparative cycles, network metrics and material contexts. The present survey inspected selected passages of these works, including the assumptions behind their proxies. [Priß et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2025.10272), [Jervis 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10816-026-09803-2) [L28, L26]

## Recreating the means of production

Childe's *The Urban Revolution* links specialist production to food transfers and distant inputs. His particular institutional typology belongs to its historical setting; the useful functional question is how complementary producers acquire what they need. This question connects directly to classical models of economic reproduction. [Childe 1950](https://faculty.washington.edu/plape/citiesaut11/readings/Childe-urban%20revolution%201950.pdf) [L01]

In Sraffa's opening examples, agricultural and industrial processes recreate their inputs and redistribute outputs among producers. With three commodities, exchange can involve a triangular relationship. The inspected witness is the 1963 Indian edition of the 1960 work, and the reading covers Chapter I. Von Neumann's model explicitly allows goods to produce other goods, joint products, capital at different stages of wear and processes broken into time steps. Hawkins and Simon examine the positivity of simultaneous linear production equations and interpret their conditions in terms of supplying intermediate requirements together with consumption. These are substantial predecessors to any claim that persistence depends on replacing the means of replacement. [Sraffa, Chapter I](https://www.nuevatribuna.es/media/nuevatribuna/files/2013/04/15/production_of_commodities_by_means_of_commodities.pdf), [von Neumann 1945](https://www.erlinger.name/Tekster/VonNeumann 1945.pdf), [Hawkins and Simon 1949](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Simon_box00029_fld02213_bdl0001_doc0001/Simon_box00029_fld02213_bdl0001_doc0001.pdf) [L17, L16, L18]

Those models select particular production laws and factor assumptions. An archaeological application must add evidence for quantities, working lives, time windows and access to inputs. It should distinguish the economy enclosing all producers from an urban center that imports products or expertise. This preserves the models' insight about joint material requirements while identifying the additional work needed to reconstruct a historical trajectory.

The publisher description of Halstead and O'Shea's *Bad Year Economics* identifies mobility, diversification, storage and exchange as responses to variable food supply. The volume itself was unavailable in this survey, so that description serves as a research lead. Provisioning comparisons should examine the combination of such routes and the conditions under which each remains accessible. An apparent local shortage can be covered by trade; several trade routes can still depend on one producing region. [Publisher description](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/bad-year-economics/2249365CCA983DAC9E902E08D7990E48) [L15, outside the primary census]

## Keeping services and repair providers available

Martin's viability formulation asks whether at least one admissible course of control can maintain a specified property through a chosen horizon. A presently acceptable state may lie outside that viable subset. Her model gives the controlling institution enough resources to change controls and excludes those institution-borne costs from the chosen resilience cost. The scope is explicit. Extending the boundary to repair providers asks how their labor, tools and supplies are reproduced, and whether these demands conflict with other obligations. [Martin 2004](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol9/iss2/art8/inline.html) [L24; O-L24-1–3]

Cavdaroglu and colleagues already integrate restoration choices, assignment to work groups and scheduling across interdependent infrastructures. Their objective follows service recovery through the horizon. Their constraints link network flows, input dependencies, work schedules and restored component availability. Consequently, a theory of shared crews and joint repair deadlines has a direct formal predecessor. The archaeological extension concerns recovering the operations and credible limits that would allow such a test. [Cavdaroglu et al. 2013](https://mitchjrpi.github.io/papers/integrated.html) [L27; O-L27-1–2]

Repair also reproduces capabilities through practice. Graham and Thrift describe apprenticeship, improvisation, cannibalization and alternative material combinations. A repair can restore useful performance and introduce a new procedure. Jervis's urban cases connect repeated maintenance with enduring relations and with changes concealed by coarse archaeological phases. These works give positive mechanisms for continuity amid material change and for the practical knowledge required to carry it out. [Graham and Thrift 2007](https://raley.english.ucsb.edu/wp-content/Engl800/Graham-Thrift-repair.pdf), [Jervis 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10816-026-09803-2) [L22, L26]

Tainter's inspected 2006 synthesis adds the costs of successful problem solving. Solutions can accumulate costs, and new subsidies, deferred expenditure or altered information arrangements change who bears them. His proposed responses include improving cost awareness and connecting costs with benefits. For persistence research, the provider's continued resources and the distribution of obligations therefore belong alongside the visible service. [Tainter 2006](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/61158.pdf) [L02]

## Arrangements through which people act

Anderies, Janssen and Ostrom explicitly connect resources, users, infrastructure and infrastructure providers. Their framework makes user-provider feedback a central relation: contributions can diminish when useful infrastructure is not supplied. The chosen outcome and boundary also matter when users can substitute another resource. This connects technical maintenance with participation rather than treating a repair task as automatically performed. [Anderies et al. 2004](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol9/iss1/art18/inline.html) [L07]

Ostrom's 2009 Science framework organizes nested resource, user and governance variables for comparison. The effect of group size depends on the work involved: additional participants can increase coordination costs and supply needed labor. Her Nobel lecture describes local direct providers combined with larger-scale indirect services in water and policing. It also distinguishes rules governing choices, information, aggregation and payoffs. These mechanisms allow several organizing arrangements to produce complementary services. They give a research design for testing how people gain access to a technically feasible plan. [Ostrom 2009 Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1172133), [Ostrom 2009 Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/ostrom_lecture.pdf) [L08, L23]

## Reproducing expertise and preserving alternatives

Henrich's formal model makes access to an interacting pool of learners relevant to skill retention under stipulated copying errors. Its effective population is a learning network, and its thresholds belong to the declared transmission law. Derex and Boyd's experiment supplies a complementary mechanism. In their computer task, partial connection preserved productive branches that could later recombine, whereas complete connection encouraged early convergence. Together, these studies motivate measuring which teachers and alternatives are accessible, how frequently capabilities must be replaced, and what kind of task is being learned. [Henrich 2004](https://coevolution.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5841/files/culture_cognition_coevol_lab/files/henrich_2004-2.pdf), [Derex and Boyd 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1518798113) [L12, L13]

Holling's 1973 formulation concerns persistence of relationships through disturbance; his later work connects accumulated resources, skills and trust with cross-scale reorganization. Walker and colleagues distinguish adjustment within an arrangement from the ability to create another. Folke and colleagues explain how transformation at one scale can support persistence at another. These mechanisms make changed settlement form a relevant outcome: people or expertise can continue through contraction, movement and altered institutions. [Holling 1973](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/26/7/RP-73-003.pdf), [Holling 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10021-001-0101-5), [Walker et al. 2004](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol9/iss2/art5/inline.html), [Folke et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.5751/ES-03610-150420) [L03–L06]

Lade and colleagues express alternatives as pathways through time: a present decision can preserve or remove later options. Sellberg and colleagues operationalize this approach for a Swedish landscape, including collective strategies and supporting interactions. Their results and recommendations address horizon dependence, switching barriers, uncertainty and the possibility that a livelihood gain can undermine a landscape outcome. Pichancourt and colleagues closely integrate nested institutional actions and coupled adaptation pathways. Their framework already includes infrastructure, knowledge and investment; they identify missing data on the costs of moving between institutional arrangements and recommend incorporating those costs. [Lade et al. 2020](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol25/iss3/art19/), [Sellberg et al. 2024](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol29/iss3/art26/), [Pichancourt et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11867035/) [L19, L20, L11]

## Recommendations that can be made operational

The following prescriptions belong to the inspected authors. The proposed archaeological comparisons that develop them are kept separately in [GAP-ASSESSMENT.md](GAP-ASSESSMENT.md) and [COMPARISONS.json](COMPARISONS.json).

| Inspected prescription | Source locus | A measurement it requests |
|---|---|---|
| Standardize settlement data and connect them with explanatory theory | Smith et al. 2021, research agenda | Separate occupation, scale, institutions and culture; record interruptions and dating resolution |
| Study complete settlement systems | Lawrence et al. 2025, Outlook | Benefits and costs to smaller settlements as well as major centers |
| Compare diverse urban adaptations and infrastructures | Chase et al. 2023, new research dialogue | Provisioning routes, local facilities and shared amenities |
| Model heterogeneous costs and benefits with nested variables | Ostrom 2009, Science conclusion | Contributions, access, monitoring and higher-level interventions |
| Incorporate institutional transition costs | Pichancourt et al. 2025, paragraphs 92–93 | Costs and time to change arrangements, alongside the resulting service |
| Refine feedbacks, uncertainties and power comparisons | Sellberg et al. 2024, reflections | Switching barriers, actor-specific access and structural sensitivity |
| Relate learning outcomes to contact and task structure | Derex and Boyd 2016, discussion | Teacher access, complementary branches and opportunities to recombine |
| Join network metrics to material context | Jervis 2026, conclusion | Shared prerequisites and dated operations behind apparent connections |

The inspected settlement-duration sources have not supplied the joint demand, initial-output and import bounds needed for a numerical minimum-renewal inference. That identifies the next evidence collection: dated services and operations, independently bounded supply, and the capacity to meet their combined demands. It leaves the organizing mechanism open until observations discriminate among household provision, collective work, exchange, recruitment and other arrangements.
