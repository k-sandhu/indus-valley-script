# What a long-lived society has to renew

11 October 2026. A conditional theory and archaeological research procedure.

A society can continue while its people, buildings, suppliers and rules change. The minimum requirement is that the services needed for the specified form of life remain obtainable over the period being considered. If initial supplies and unmaintained assets cannot cover that period, some combination of production, repair, replacement, exchange, immigration or substitution must cover the difference. These activities have to fit together in time and have arrangements through which people can carry them out.

The proposed research procedure has three parts. Establish what has persisted and which services it required. Bound how much of those services could have been supplied without further renewal. Then examine whether the remaining demands could have been met together, given shared inputs, deadlines and participation. This connects existing work on economic reproduction, maintenance and viability to archaeological observations with explicit inference rules. The [survey](literature/SURVEY.md) and [predecessor assessment](literature/GAP-ASSESSMENT.md) delimit the contribution.

## Define the society and the duration

“Persistence” needs a stated object. The occupation of a place, a large urban population, a trade network, an administrative institution and a craft tradition can have different histories. A smaller settlement after a city's contraction may preserve inhabitants and knowledge while losing the services needed for dense urban life. A city can recruit people and expertise from elsewhere while its own resident families or training institutions disappear. An archaeological sequence can also contain intervals when occupation was sparse or absent.

Choose a geographical boundary, a time horizon, a population or activity range and the function whose continuation matters. Include an allowable range of change rather than demanding that every feature remain identical. Define the disturbances being considered and the observations that count as continued operation. A claim about several centuries of occupation then becomes a claim at the actual resolution of the evidence. It does not require a continuous sequence of rulers or an unchanged name for the community.

Michael E. Smith and colleagues already distinguish persistence of occupation, demographic scale, institutions and culture. Their settlement comparisons make duration an empirical question. The procedure here keeps those outcomes separate when constructing a renewal demand rather than treating longevity as a single measure of civilizational success. See their [2021 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8157985/) and the reading scope in the [literature ledger](literature/SOURCES.json).

## Minimum functions depend on the form of life

The following table is a set of conditional questions. Each row becomes a requirement when the specified population or activity depends on it and the available stocks, natural flows or external supply cannot satisfy the chosen horizon on their own.

| Function that may need continued provision | What must be obtainable | Arrangements compatible with the function | Evidence that would help discriminate the arrangements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bodily provisioning | Sufficient usable food, water, shelter and other locally necessary support | Household production, shared facilities, markets, tribute, gifts or outside deliveries | Production and storage contexts, access routes, distribution within neighborhoods, independently bounded imports |
| Productive and infrastructural renewal | Usable replacements for worn tools, exhausted supplies and facilities whose failure would interrupt the required service | Repair, new construction, reuse, rerouting, substitution or purchased capacity | Dated repairs, changed routes, reused materials, sourcing evidence and actual service interruption |
| Availability of participants and expertise | People capable of the required work when it becomes due | Birth and upbringing, practical learning, recruited specialists, immigration or simpler methods | Successive production techniques, learning-related material, movement of people and inputs, overlap of working generations |
| Joint allocation and enactment | Access to the labor, materials and cooperation required by the combined obligations | Kinship obligations, reciprocal practice, negotiated rules, paid work, public action or coercion | Shared interfaces, repeated decisions over access, contributions, conflict, distribution of work and benefits |
| Response when an arrangement stops working | An available alternative if continuation under the specified disturbances requires it | Buffers, alternative providers, changed demand, movement or a different settlement configuration | Substituted products, new supply paths, changed housing and population patterns, continued practices in other contexts |

A city dependent on imported food needs a continuing route to usable food; it need not grow that food within its walls. A craft whose practitioner cohort is being replaced needs a way to retain or reacquire the relevant expertise; a locally organized school is one possible arrangement. A drainage system needs usable waste removal at the required level; replacement by a different route may preserve the service. The requirements concern outcomes and mechanisms appropriate to the boundary and horizon.

Specific institutions become stronger hypotheses when their distinctive traces are present. Urban scale by itself does not decide whether obligations were organized through a palace, household groups, assemblies or several arrangements at once. Monica L. Smith's [infrastructure study](https://doi.org/10.1080/00438243.2015.1124804) already describes maintenance responsibilities renegotiated among providers and users across generations. The observations needed to infer a particular organization therefore include who could use, modify and maintain a shared facility, along with its construction.

## A current surplus can conceal a renewal deficit

Suppose a city has working drains, stocked food and experienced makers. These observations describe its present condition. Whether it can maintain that condition also depends on the working lives of assets, the timing of supplies and the availability of replacements. Food deliveries, rebuilding and training can compete for the same workers, materials or transport. It is possible for each task to be feasible when considered separately and for their combined deadlines to be infeasible.

This is a distinction already expressed by viability theory: some states satisfy current requirements yet have no admissible trajectory that keeps satisfying them. Sophie Martin's [2004 study](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol9/iss2/art8/inline.html) makes objectives, controls, disturbances and time horizon explicit. Joint restoration scheduling is also an established infrastructure problem. Cavdaroglu and colleagues jointly select restoration tasks, assign work groups and schedule service recovery. The [review](review/REVIEW.md) examines these counterprecedents and the stronger recent combination of adaptation pathways with Ostrom's framework.

The archaeological question is whether a dated set of obligations could be renewed together and what observations distinguish alternative answers. Counting buildings, occupations or alternative suppliers does not settle this question. Two suppliers may use the same quarry, transport route or expertise. Several facilities built in one expansion can later require work at similar times. Conversely, reuse or less demanding methods may reduce renewal requirements even while the city's visible infrastructure becomes simpler.

The cost of sustaining service and reproducing expertise also needs direct observation. Repair can teach an apprentice, making one operation produce two useful outputs. The separate-cost baseline and a post-baseline shared-operation variant give different renewal possibilities under explicit invented laws. [MODEL-RESULTS.md](MODEL-RESULTS.md) reports that comparison. It turns a presumed resource burden into a test of actual working and learning arrangements.

## A formal definition

Let a state `x(t)` record the quantities relevant to the defined outcome: usable stocks, asset condition, people and skills, accessible inputs and obligations. Let `K(t)` contain the states that meet its stated service and distribution requirements. Let `U(x,t)` contain actions that are technically possible with the available inputs. A transition law relates a state, chosen action and disturbance to the next state.

An admissible trajectory remains in `K(t)` for the specified horizon. A robust policy keeps every supported trajectory in `K(t)`, with actions based only on information available at their decision time. A possible trajectory allows some favorable sequence. Neither quantifier supplies a historical probability. The [finite model](coordinator/MODEL-PROTOCOL.md) implements both quantifiers under invented laws and full state observation; a historical reconstruction may require a narrower information set.

Technical admissibility is only one part of a societal explanation. A proposed course of action must also be accessible to the people whose participation it requires. Their information, authority, incentives and obligations need to be represented or supported independently. No society has to solve an optimization problem consciously for a functioning routine to emerge. Repeated local practice can implement a trajectory; it can also produce incompatible choices. The review's small voluntary-action example shows a technically sufficient shared repair that nobody chooses under its declared payoffs.

The resulting condition is: there must be a jointly feasible course of actions, and a historically available arrangement must enable its enactment. This is a necessary condition relative to the specified services, transition law and horizon. Existence of a model policy establishes sufficiency only inside that model. It becomes an archaeological explanation only with evidence for the inputs, law, outcome and means of participation.

## A work-window constraint

A narrow renewal explanation can be screened before constructing a complete schedule. Suppose its required operations have independently bounded work and completion windows. Count resource use once after allowing shared operations or co-production; a repair can also teach an apprentice. For a resource `r` and interval `J`, consider the operations whose entire permissible work window lies within `J`. Their minimum required use of that resource cannot exceed the maximum usable supply of it within the interval:

`sum(required r-work of operations confined to J) <= maximum usable r-capacity during J.`

After those shared uses are accounted for, resource consumption is assumed additive. Every such operation must use the resource inside that interval, so their combined use consumes its interval capacity. For a consumable resource, capacity includes usable inventory carried into the interval as well as new deliveries. This is an ordinary resource constraint from scheduling, not a new mathematical result. It is necessary under the stated operations and windows; passing it does not establish a full feasible schedule, because dependencies, lead times and simultaneous skill requirements may impose further restrictions.

The constraint adds an archaeological measurement question to an average cost account. Repairs that appear affordable over a century may exceed the workers or material deliveries available in a particular season. If alternative renewal routes exist, their choice has to be included before declaring the task set mandatory. In the finite example, state `(1,1,1)` requires two asset renewals and training in the same period for indefinite continuation. Budget 2 fails that one-period constraint even though it exceeds the long-run mean work of `4/3` per period. A short horizon can end before renewed expertise is needed, so the task's necessity depends on the defined horizon.

## What longevity can identify

For one commensurable usable commodity or service, let `Dmin(H)` be a justified lower bound on cumulative demand during horizon `H`. Let `Smax(H)` bound delivery from surviving initial stocks and assets without renewal over that entire horizon. Let `Imax(H)` bound usable imported delivery and `Emax(H)` bound other usable supply obtainable without the additional actions being investigated. These quantities must collectively bound the available baseline supply.

Then additional usable delivery `Q(H)` satisfies:

`Q(H) >= max(0, Dmin(H) - Smax(H) - Imax(H) - Emax(H)).`

The proof is ordinary accounting. Observed demand cannot exceed all baseline deliveries plus additional delivery. Substitute the lower demand bound and the upper supply bounds, and retain the nonnegative residual. The separate upper bounds can be conservative; if their maxima cannot occur together, their sum may weaken the conclusion further.

The units matter. Food calories, raw-water volume, drainage throughput and wall counts cannot share a single numerator. A standing asset's future output must be bounded over the full horizon under an independent wear or capacity law. If water and a conduit are complementary inputs to a delivered service, adding their capacities as though they were separate deliveries would misstate the account. A conservative archaeological pilot should begin with commodities or specified outputs whose quantities can be reconstructed consistently.

A positive residual identifies the need for some additional provision beyond the bounded baseline sources. It leaves a disjunction of possible mechanisms: new production, maintenance that prolongs output, replacement, reacquisition or another source excluded from the baseline. It does not count repair episodes or identify a local political institution. If initial output, imports or natural supply has no defensible finite upper bound, the residual is uninformative. If population contraction or a habitation gap reduces the demand lower bound to zero, duration alone yields no positive requirement.

This makes the inverse problem useful even when it cannot produce a numerical answer. It specifies the missing measurement. A repair layer can demonstrate that renewal occurred; it does not by itself prove that all other routes were impossible. A long occupational sequence can motivate a renewal question; it cannot replace measurements of demand and supply.

## Testable extensions

Four operational hypotheses deserve explicit tests. Their mechanisms have close precedents, including joint repair deadlines and shared dependencies. The proposed extension specifies the archaeological observations needed to discriminate them.

1. **Renewal losses can precede service losses.** Where service depends on expertise that takes time to acquire, interruption of training or recruitment can leave working assets temporarily intact. The test needs independently dated evidence for both continuing service and a narrowing replacement route. Disappearance of a finished product alone cannot identify loss of expertise because procurement, demand and deposition may change.
2. **Shared deadlines can matter beyond average expenditure.** A group of assets with similar replacement dates can exceed an available work window even if mean annual work is affordable. Testing this requires repair dates and realistic work windows fine enough to resolve overlap. Broad archaeological phases can conceal the proposed effect.
3. **Alternative providers can share a vulnerable prerequisite.** Counting providers will overstate independence when several rely on the same input or route. A discrimination test compares sourcing and upstream dependence alongside the apparent diversity of providers. The claim concerns dependency structure; collapse likelihood also requires disturbance frequencies and response options.
4. **Changing settlement form can preserve a subset of capacities.** A smaller, differently organized settlement may retain provisioning or expertise after losing an urban configuration. The comparison must report these outcomes separately. The Harappan application contains a source-qualified example of selective wall maintenance and changed occupation after an interruption.

These are hypotheses for observation and comparison. None is introduced as a newly discovered law. The contribution is a set of explicit joins from demand and dated renewal to schedule, dependency and enactment constraints. The [prospective research design](EMPIRICAL-PROTOCOL.md) supplies definitions, rival explanations and stop rules.

## Complexity and inherited knowledge

Large cities and extensive trade create many interacting obligations. They can support specialized expertise and accumulated practical knowledge, while also depending on distant production and recurring contributions. A society can distribute that knowledge among households, makers, traders and ritual specialists. Its members need not hold a comprehensive account of the whole system for their combined practices to reproduce it.

Long renewal histories make procedural memory a plausible topic of research. People can inherit recipes, ways of measuring, decisions about access and expectations about whose turn it is to act. Such knowledge can remain useful through changes to buildings and organizations. The inspected maintenance literature already connects repetitive practice, memory and enduring urban relations, including [Jervis 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/00438243.2024.2387660) and [Jervis 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10816-026-09803-2).

Explicit accounts of a remote past are a further claim. Mature concepts in early literature could draw on long intellectual and social development, but this model cannot assign their origin to a particular earlier civilization. The [preceding early Indian continuity studies](../2026-10-11-early-indian-continuity/REPORT.md) supply the separate chronology and source comparisons. The present theory suggests a mechanism to investigate: how practical and institutional knowledge is reproduced when settlement form, population and authority change.
