# Renewing Harappan services through changes in settlement

11 October 2026. A bounded documentary application of the [renewal theory](../THEORY.md).

Harappan excavation records contain specific replacements, repairs and changes of use that can make the persistence question empirical. At Harappa, a clogged drain was followed by a new route through a former gateway. At Dholavira, people returning after a desertion retained wall segments useful to some reservoirs and salvaged other structures for houses. In another part of Dholavira, later activity occupied the filled East reservoir after its water function ended. These sequences distinguish the continuation of activity at a place from the continuation of a particular service. They also suggest how a community could continue by changing the arrangement through which it obtained water, shelter or craft products. [Meadow and Kenoyer 1997, p.146](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1997_Excavations%20at%20Harappa%201994-1995%20New%20Perspective.pdf), [ASI Review, p.30](https://www.asiaurangabadcircle.com/assets/files/publications/1991-1992.pdf), [Bisht preliminary report, pp.163–164](https://archive.org/details/dholavirareportpreliminaryjan2015_860_K).

The application records 34 observations from 14 inspected works. It identifies operations and plausible dependencies, but the sources do not provide a complete account of usable service delivery, demand, initial output, imports or jointly timed work. Consequently, the accounting and scheduling conditions in the parent theory remain uncalibrated here. The positive result is a set of source-qualified cases and measurements that could discriminate narrower renewal explanations. [Sources](sources.json), [observations](observations.json), [operational tests](TESTS.md).

## Evidence and chronological units

This is a purposive survey of field reports, technological research, archaeobotany, residue analysis and selected human remains. Reading depth and exact loci are recorded in the ledger. Fourteen works do not represent fourteen independent excavations: the two Jansen publications reuse Mohenjo-Daro records; the ASI review and Bisht report share the Dholavira programme; several agricultural papers overlap Land, Water and Settlement sites. Kenoyer's craft syntheses are reused within a stated scope. Mirrors and alternate formats preserve access to a text rather than adding archaeological witnesses.

The outcome is specified separately for each comparison: a drain's function, a reservoir's function, settlement activity, a production technique, a regional network or a measured food proxy. A structural phase supplies relative ordering at its actual resolution. The date ranges below are source reconstructions, not new harmonized event dates. Harappa Period 3C is assigned broadly to 2250–2000/1900 BCE in the 1997 paper. Bisht's table assigns Dholavira Stage IV approximately to 2500–2100, V to 2100–2000 and VI to 1950–1800 BCE, using stratigraphy, typology and limited thermoluminescence checks. The report retains interruptions. These ranges cannot resolve seasonal task overlap. [S03, S05; E06, E15](observations.json).

Source identity matters for that ordering. *Indian Archaeology 1991–92—A Review* was published in 1996. The composite file named `jansen2011.pdf` contains a 2011 magazine item followed by Jansen's 2014 book chapter. The craft article hosted under a 1998 filename belongs to the nominal 1996 issue. The original preliminary Dholavira PDF was inspected after initial transcription access; its printed pages now control the reservoir claims. Download challenges, unsuccessful routes, the excluded interpretive wrapper and source count discrepancies remain in [the acquisition record](acquisition-index.json) and [source checks](source-checks.json).

## Drain renewal changed a working interface

The Harappa Mound E gateway sequence provides the most specific first pilot. Three earlier drains, numbered [116], [144] and [50], ran beside the exterior wall. The last, attributed to Period 3C, was corbelled and filled with silty refuse. The excavators describe a later culvert dug farther east through the former gateway after that drain ceased operating. This associates a service interruption with replacement and rerouting at the same local interface. The associated doorway and streets changed too, giving a way to test whether drainage and circulation imposed competing requirements on the work. [S03; E07–E08, printed pp.143–146/PDF pp.5–8](sources.json), [primary field paper](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1997_Excavations%20at%20Harappa%201994-1995%20New%20Perspective.pdf).

The next reconstruction should establish where the refuse entered, which connected installations operated before and after it, and whether another disposal route existed during the change. In-use accumulation, dumping after abandonment and deliberate closure predict different sediment and access relationships. Only after those observations should sediment removal, culvert construction and access changes become tasks in a joint schedule. The original record does not supply the interruption length or workers' time. A century-scale phase cannot be converted into an annual repair allowance.

Mohenjo-Daro supplies complementary evidence about maintenance access. Removable covers, settling pits with access steps and household catchment vessels offered several ways to handle deposits and effluent. Jansen also reports three successive drain rebuilding phases in the southern DK-G sector. The physical arrangement and rebuilding support different claims: one permitted intervention, while the other records structural renewal. [S01; E02–E03, pp.189–190](observations.json), [Jansen 1989](https://doi.org/10.1080/00438243.1989.9980100).

Jansen's later chapter explains why an apparently detailed plan still cannot provide a delivered-service budget: an earlier flow study found no complete intact interconnected network from which to calculate it. His proposal to use connected drains for broad contemporaneity is useful for selecting functional clusters, provided their co-use is established from local relationships. A network might include older segments reused by later buildings. The linked cluster is therefore a candidate sampling unit for upkeep, with its chronology tested separately. [S02; E04–E05, chapter pp.66–68](observations.json), [inspected chapter](https://publ.icomos.org/publicomos/jlbSai?html=Bur&base=technica&ref=42596&file=1263.pdf&path=110601geo_06_2011_indian_edition_email.pdf).

## Selective retention at Dholavira

The ASI field review reports a brief desertion before Stage VI. Later occupants retained outer-wall segments important to some reservoirs, took stones from older structures for houses and introduced a different street and housing arrangement. That combination is a concrete basis for a selective-renewal hypothesis: preserve an inherited component where it still contributes to a needed service, while using other inherited material to build a changed settlement. The observation concerns specified wall segments and later housing. It leaves the occupants' identity and the allocation of work unresolved. [S04; E10–E11, printed p.30/PDF p.38](observations.json), [ASI field review](https://www.asiaurangabadcircle.com/assets/files/publications/1991-1992.pdf).

The East reservoir supplies an internal comparison. Bisht describes repair of its damaged northern embankment during Stage IV. Later, damage and sediment filled much of the basin; fireplaces and kilns appeared on the resulting surface during later Stage V and VI. The report identifies the end of water use and explicitly distinguishes the later activity from regular continuous occupation in that area. The excavator's economic explanation and extension to all eastern reservoirs require additional evidence. The ASI review's retained wall segments are not individually identified as supports for this particular reservoir. [S05; E12–E14, printed pp.161–164/PDF pp.178–181](observations.json), [original preliminary report](https://archive.org/details/dholavirareportpreliminaryjan2015_860_K).

These observations make “Dholavira persisted” several different propositions. A selected water route was retained after reoccupation; a named reservoir lost its function; materials were reused; and activities continued at some earlier locations. A feasible smaller settlement could depend on fewer reservoirs, changed access or other water sources. Testing that possibility requires mapping retained and abandoned components against independently reconstructed later demand. An unused large basin could reflect reduced demand or substitution as well as an inability to repair it.

## Tools and expertise form a renewal chain

Kenoyer and Vidale's drilling study identifies roughouts, manufacturing waste, grinding traces and worn tools at Indus lapidary sites. Their reconstruction connects making a specialized drill to sharpening it, using it, resharpening it and eventually using a smaller remnant or recycling part of the tool. A craft inventory should therefore include the operations that reproduce its tools. Modern experiments in the paper further show that material and operator differences change performance; their measured hours cannot be transported directly into an ancient labor budget. The proposed closely supervised apprenticeship remains an interpretation requiring evidence for actual transmission. [S08; E20–E23, pp.508–513](observations.json), [Kenoyer and Vidale 1992](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1992_A%20new%20look%20at%20stone%20drills%20of%20the%20Indus%20Valley%20T.pdf).

Kenoyer's later synthesis reports the disappearance of the specialized drilling stone after urban contraction and describes alternative drills and imitation materials. The material's original source and the reason its use ended remain unresolved. Lost access to raw stone, lost source knowledge, declining demand for difficult long beads and replacement by another technique make different predictions. The broader material/technique distinction in his 1996 article helps keep these prerequisites separate. Modern crafts supply comparisons for mechanisms, with no demonstrated continuous line of practitioners. [S09–S10; E24–E26](sources.json), [1996 craft article](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1998_Craft%20Traditions%20of%20the%20Indus%20Civilization%20and%20t.pdf), [2007 bead synthesis](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer%202007%20Intl%20beads%20conf.pdf).

Gola Dhoro adds a smaller-settlement comparison. Workshops, stocks and waste attest several production activities. The authors infer separate shell collectors and distribution of some raw stock from its condition and spatial pattern. Those are logistical hypotheses rather than counted supply organizations. Their later occupation phase reportedly continued after several earlier craft, trade and fortification indicators ended. This separates persistence of occupation from persistence of a specialized production network. [S11; E27–E29, pp.1–5](observations.json), [Bhan and colleagues 2005](https://doi.org/10.15184/S2978855200904840).

The developed hypothesis is that support operations can determine the fate of a particular production configuration. An active bead maker can continue consuming old drills while the system that replaces them has already failed. A subsequent contraction in difficult products would then lag behind the input or learning failure. That temporal ordering differs from demand-led contraction, which predicts reduced product demand with usable inputs and replacement knowledge still available. Existing broad phases do not choose between these mechanisms.

## Provisioning alternatives need their dependencies identified

Petrie and Bates combine crops, weeds and ecological expectations to propose varied seasonal cultivation strategies. They also explain why finding several crop species in a settlement does not establish that they grew together, on the same parcel or in independent seasons: storage and deposition can mix them. Petrie and colleagues show that varied village practices and multi-season crop use preceded urban contraction. Those observations identify possible productive options without measuring the delivered food or independence of their failures. [S06–S07; E16–E19](sources.json), [Petrie and Bates 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10963-017-9101-z), [Petrie and colleagues 2017](https://doi.org/10.1086/690112).

A useful next question is whether apparently different options relied on the same constrained input. Winter and summer cultivation might use different rains while sharing land preparation, storage, transport or skilled work. The relevant observation would be dependence between specified routes, not a count of crop names. Crop isotopes, associated weeds and processing contexts can narrow the growing regime; they must be joined to evidence for the operations and routes delivering food.

Pottery residues provide a limited comparison of daily practice through larger changes. Suryanarayan and colleagues report broad signature similarities and no detected period effect in their sampled cross-period comparison. Mixtures and regional reference baselines leave some ingredients ambiguous, and an unchanged broad isotope class can conceal changed recipes. The residue result can therefore frame a prospective culinary-practice test; it does not supply a food-quantity account or establish equivalent whole diets. [S12; E30–E32, §§4.1, 4.3–4.4 and 5](observations.json), [Suryanarayan and colleagues 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2020.105291).

Distribution also matters to the outcome. The Harappa skeletal study reports differing disease indicators among selected burial groups. Its surviving, funerary-selected samples do not estimate all residents' health, but they make equal benefit an additional question rather than part of the definition of occupation. The regional settlement compilation similarly leaves relocation and population identity unresolved because locations, chronology and survey coverage are uneven. [S13; E33](observations.json), [Robbins Schug and colleagues 2013](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084814), [S14; E34](observations.json), [Green and Petrie 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/00934690.2018.1464332).

## Developed hypotheses and the next discriminating observations

Three hypotheses were refined after the documentary audit. They are prospective extensions, with no fitted historical probabilities.

Selective retention predicts that later maintenance follows the services still required by a changed settlement. A comparison should map retained wall segments, service access and salvaged structures before coding later use. Preservation conditions, construction type and convenience of taking material form controls. If retained segments show no relationship to continuing service after those controls, functional retention loses its proposed explanatory value. The Dholavira records make this comparison worth constructing; they do not already complete it.

Support-chain failure predicts that interruption of tool material, tool production or technical transmission precedes contraction in products that depend on it. Source characterization and dated waste sequences would separate raw access from working knowledge. Preserved inputs and successful difficult production during the alleged shortage would reject a broad supply-loss explanation. Declining demand while replacement tools remain available would favor another ordering. The same protocol should include crafts that continued and cheaper techniques that replaced an earlier configuration.

Concentration of renewal work predicts that tasks can become jointly difficult even when each is manageable separately. For the Harappa pilot, independent measurements must bound sediment removal, material preparation, new construction and restored service. For Dholavira, compare repair and alternative water arrangements with shelter construction using newly acquired versus salvaged material. Joint work windows and a shared input must be demonstrated before applying the scheduling condition. If chronological uncertainty remains wider than those windows, the result stays unresolved at that resolution. [Tests and rejection conditions](TESTS.md), [parent empirical procedure](../EMPIRICAL-PROTOCOL.md).

The minimum-capacity question can then be asked for a particular service and horizon: what additional usable delivery was required, and could the observed and allowed operations supply it together? At present, these sources identify renewal mechanisms and changed outcomes. The quantities needed to convert those mechanisms into a minimum remain explicit missing data, recorded in every observation and in [the audit](run-v2/summary.json).
