# Contemporary civilisations, c. 2600–1900 BCE: methods note

`civilisations.json` compares the Mature Harappan civilisation with 21 other societies of the same centuries on three things: how much land they occupied or controlled, how large their biggest settlements were and how many people lived in them, and which institutions and technologies they had. It is built for a chart and a table, so every number carries a source that a reader can open, with a page or section where the source has one. We read all sources on 20 October 2026. Six research passes, one for the Indus and five for other regions, produced the rows. We merged them, checked every row against the schema and reviewed the weak figures before writing the file.

## Which societies we included

The brief named fourteen groups, and we split or added rows where the evidence required it. Southern Mesopotamia has two snapshots, Early Dynastic III to Akkad (c. 2600–2150 BCE) and Ur III (c. 2112–2004 BCE). The Iranian plateau has three rows, because Elam and Susiana, the Helmand towns around Shahr-i Sokhta and the Halil Rud sites around Jiroft were separate cultures. We added the southern Levant in Early Bronze III, southern Turkmenistan in Namazga V (Altyn Depe has an Indus-style seal), the Shijiahe culture of the middle Yangtze and El Argar in southeast Iberia. Some rows are here because their people dealt with the Indus or appear beside Meluhha in Mesopotamian texts: Mesopotamia, Elam, Marhashi, Dilmun, Magan and the Oxus towns. Egypt is the usual benchmark for an early territorial state. China, the Andes and Europe show large settlements and monuments that arose without contact with the Indus.

Each row is restricted to its phase inside 2600–1900 BCE. Some of the best figures belong to a phase that only touches the window, and the `date` field says so. Shahr-i Sokhta reached its peak in Period II, which ends about 2500 BCE. Liangzhu's dams and walls were built before 2600 BCE. El Argar's territory of 35,000 km² and the figure of 10,000 people at Kerma both belong to later centuries.

## How areas were measured

The `extent_km2` field holds three kinds of measurement, and its `basis` says which one applies. The Indus figure, 650,000–1,000,000 km², is an envelope drawn around the distribution of Mature Harappan sites. Kenoyer's "more than 250,000 square miles" sets the lower bound and Khan and Lemmen's 1 million km² the upper. A second kind of figure measures land that was settled or farmed. This covers Egypt's 17,000–22,400 km² of floodplain, from Butzer as summarised by Snape, and the areas of Crete, Bahrain, the Halil Rud survey and the Lake Tai plain around Liangzhu, which we derived ourselves. The third kind is political control, such as Akkad at 450,000–1,000,000 km² (Seshat and Taagepera), the Ur III state at 100,000 km² and Elam at 200,000–300,000 km² (Seshat, uncited). These kinds do not compare directly. The Indus envelope measures how far one material culture reached, and Fisher's estimate of a densely settled core of 30,000–100,000 km² is the Indus figure to set beside Egypt's floodplain. Twelve rows have no published extent and carry null.

Settlement areas measure different surfaces too: a mound, a walled circuit, a scatter of surface finds or a remote-sensing outline. At Ganweriwala, 81.5 ha is a 1970s jeep-odometer estimate, 66.7 ha is a 2015 contour survey of cultural material, and about 33 ha is the visible mound in satellite data (Gulzar; Orengo et al. 2020). Ur is 50–60 ha inside its walls or 108 ha at the Ur III peak, with suburbs beyond. Rakhigarhi is 80 ha for the main mound group or 350 ha when seven mounds are counted together. Where we computed an area from published dimensions, the `method` field says "derived by us": Dholavira's fortified enclosure, Lahun, Kerma, Kültepe, Alaca Höyük, Küllüoba, Tepe Yahya and Dilmun's land base.

## How populations were estimated

None of the population figures is a count. Most multiply an area by an assumed density. McMahon and colleagues apply the common 100–200 persons per hectare to Lagash and obtain 60,000–120,000 people, the QADIS survey uses 150 per hectare, Butzer allows no more than 200 for Memphis, and Ascalone and Fabbri use 25–83 per hectare at Shahr-i Sokhta. Other figures come from house counts, as at Kahun (about 200 houses at six people each) and in Whitelaw's household densities for Knossos, or from granary capacity, again at Kahun. The kingdom of Ebla's 200,000–260,000 comes from readings of administrative texts. The Giza figure of 8,000–10,000 is a provisioned workforce, not a resident population, and Egypt's 1.6–2.0 million is Butzer's estimate from cultivable land and carrying capacity. Cemeteries give large numbers that no source we reached converts into a living population: about 75,000 Early Dilmun burial mounds counted from 1950s aerial photographs ([Abe and Uesugi 2021](https://researchmap.jp/masashiabe/published_papers/32034116/attachment_file.pdf), pp. 75–77) and 30,000–40,000 tombs at Kerma's Eastern Cemetery ([Kerma mission](https://kerma.ch/en/?p=544)).

Many city figures come from compilations (Chandler, Modelski, Morris) or Seshat codes that state no density. We kept them as ranges and named their origin in `method`. Where a figure implies an implausible density we said so: Namazga Depe's 17,000–20,000 people on 52 ha means about 330–385 persons per hectare. We left Seshat's 200,000 for Ur III Ur out of the range because it would mean about 1,850 per hectare. Total population is null for seventeen rows. For southern Mesopotamia the sources give the urban share in place of a total: Adams put 71–82 per cent of the Early Dynastic population in cities ([McMahon et al. 2023](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/j5qwn-b4642), p. 2), and Ur put about 80 per cent in sites of 40 ha or more ([Marchetti et al. 2019](https://site.unibo.it/eduu/en/work-package-1-archaeological-cultural-and-environmental-research/results/marchetti-et-al-2019.pdf/@@download/file/Marchetti%20et%20al.%202019.pdf), p. 216).

## Main disagreements

The Indus disputes concern where to draw the edge of the civilisation and whether its polity was a state, a set of city-states or a complex society without a ruling class (Green 2021 reviews the positions). In southern Mesopotamia the question is density: Fletcher's model caps dense agrarian settlements near 100 ha, while imagery at Lagash shows building across several hundred hectares. Ebla's city population ranges fourfold between estimates. For Troy II, the Kolb–Korfmann dispute mainly concerns Troy VI; Kolb accepts the Troy II palisade, and the open question is how densely the 8 ha outer settlement was occupied. Knossos measures 20–30 ha in one survey and over 40 ha in Whitelaw's account. Shahr-i Sokhta's peak may fall mostly before 2600 BCE, and the identification of the Jiroft sites with Marhashi rests on texts, not finds. Desset's 2022 decipherment of Linear Elamite has both support and critics. Gonur Depe is read as a city or as a palace and ritual centre with few residents. The political reach of Caral, Shimao and Taosi is contested in the same way as the Indus state.

## What we could not verify

Several standard syntheses were reached only through quotations in Wikipedia notes or other secondary pages: Possehl 2002, McIntosh 2008, Dyson 2018 and Coningham and Young 2015 for the Indus, Liu's *The Chinese Neolithic* and Liu and Chen 2012 for China, Whitelaw 2012 for Crete and Vidale 2017 for Turkmenistan. We did not open Adams's 1981 period totals, Butzer's 1976 tables, Steinkeller's estimate for Umma, Weiss's population figure for Leilan or Fairservis's 1967 city estimates. The Lucknow teaching note behind the 8 October count of 1,022 Indus sites is a scanned PDF with no text. Many pages returned 403, including UNESCO, Britannica and harappa.com essays, and a figure seen only in a search-result summary is marked that way in `page_or_section`. The 200-search web budget shared by all six passes ran out before the end, so we made the last checks from pages already reached and from direct lookups.

Pleiades blocked most automated requests, so most coordinates come from Wikipedia or Wikidata and are labelled as a last resort. The Indus coordinates come from the project's site census, which gives a gazetteer, UNESCO entry or excavation report for each point. Three points are approximate and labelled in `coord_source`: Heit el-Ghurab uses the Great Pyramid, Al Tikha uses Rustaq town, and Chupacigarro uses Wikidata's "Chupacigarro Grande" item. No page we read contained instructions aimed at us. The passes met only Wikipedia's notices to translators, a honeypot comment in a form on an Encyclopaedia Iranica page and bot-check screens, and ignored them.

## What the comparison supports

In this window the Indus spread one material culture over 650,000–1,000,000 km², an area matched in the table only by Akkad's political control at its height, while its largest cities stood in the second size class. Mohenjo-daro covered 200–250 ha, against 240–600 ha for Early Dynastic Uruk, Lagash, Adab and Umma, about 400 ha for Shimao and 630–800 ha for Liangzhu, whose walls predate the window. The Indus is the only row coded with writing but with neither palaces nor royal tombs. Two tests would decide this reading. Excavation at a large unexcavated Indus site, such as Ganweriwala or the unexcavated mounds of Rakhigarhi, would find a palace or a rich tomb if one exists. A core-area measurement made by one method for the Indus, Egypt and Mesopotamia would settle the size comparison.
