# Study A: Yajnadevam's Sanskrit key and its unicity argument

Frozen design in the [package protocol](../PROTOCOL.md); additions after outcomes are in Amendments [1](AMENDMENT-1.md), [2](AMENDMENT-2.md), [3](AMENDMENT-3.md) and [4](AMENDMENT-4.md). ## Claim and prior critiques

The preprint *A cryptanalytic decipherment of the Indus script* (version compiled 13 November 2024, read from the Rare Book Society of India copy) treats the corpus as a homophonic cipher of Sanskrit. Signs carry consonant or vowel values; aspirates, retroflexes and all sibilants with *h* are pooled; vowels, word division and compounding are supplied by the reader. It applies d = N/ρ with N = 330 effective signs and ρ = 0.7, obtains about 471 characters, and states that reading the 50 longest inscriptions beyond this distance shows the information came from the inscriptions. Another version uses N = 439 and d = 627, as quoted by Raghavendra. The preprint also states that "if a decipherment is utterly incorrect, it won't even be possible to make any words."

Nikhil Raghavendra's preprint *On the Non-Specificity of Statistical Measures Used in Script Decipherment* ([arXiv:2608.02999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02999), August 2026) is the main prior critique. It reconstructs the regular-expression and dictionary search behind this key from the public code, which writes expressions for 32 sign headings but no complete signary algorithm, and applies it to SIGIL, a constructed emblem system that encodes no sounds. That search reaches in-sample dictionary coverage of 83.0% for English, 88.1% for Sanskrit and 67.7% for Tamil under its literal protocol and about 97% under the Sanskrit script protocols, where random script keys already average 27.9%; bootstrap keys agree on 9.3% of sign values. The paper also shows that SIGIL exceeds the distance given by the preprint's formula about 22 times, so the inequality "cannot validate a reading on its own." An informal January 2025 r/IndianHistory critique, seen here only as a search summary, argues that the distance is larger than claimed. This project's community-proposals memo had asked whether a proposed key predicts the corpus better than competing keys with the same freedom.

This study works on the real corpus with the complete published key table. It measures the redundancy of the representation that table fixes, calibrates a lexical emulation of the acceptance criterion, scores the key under an independent Sanskrit model against rearrangements of its own values and against the best arrangements the same freedom reaches, compares languages under matched freedom, and builds synthetic Indus-shaped encodings to show what a correct key would score.

## Findings

**A lexical emulation of the acceptance criterion accepts random strings.** Readability is tested against attested DCS lemmas and Monier-Williams headwords, with an optional final ending. When every stem must have at least two consonants, random consonant strings drawn at Sanskrit letter frequencies are readable from DCS lemmas in 91.8% to 96.4% of cases at lengths 4 to 16, and from Monier-Williams in 98.5% to 99.7%. The DCS lexicon contains only words attested in texts, which meets the preprint's instruction that roots, grammatical terms and lexicographers' words "should be removed". The key reads 94.8% of the 1,859 unique inscriptions from DCS lemmas. That is above all 1,000 frequency-stratified permutations of its own values (mean 88.9%, maximum 94.1%), so the values were fitted to a dictionary. Hill-climbing over the same within-stratum swaps reaches 99.4% to 99.6%, so the key achieves 56% of the gain over rearrangements that its own freedom allows (38% with Monier-Williams). With Monier-Williams the key reads 96.0% against a permutation mean of 93.9% (maximum 96.6%) and a fitted 99.5%. Allowing one-consonant stems, as the published glossing does (354 of its 3,491 lemma rows), makes every non-empty skeleton readable, because each of the fifteen classes is itself a headword; that comparison only separates texts made entirely of vowel signs. [Stage 2 summary](stage2/summary.json), [fitted control](stage2/fitted_readability.json), [figure](figures/readability.svg).

**The key's consonant sequences score far below Sanskrit text and within about two standard deviations of rearrangements of its own values.** Under an order-5 Kneser–Ney model trained on two million consonants of DCS text (a mixture of unsandhied Vedic and Arthaśāstra lines with sandhied epic, legal and Brāhmaṇa lines), order gain measures how much better the model predicts a string than Sanskrit letter frequencies alone. Held-out Sanskrit spans scored from a sentence start gain 0.29 to 0.42 bits per consonant, single held-out words 0.35, and random Monier-Williams headwords 0.06. The key's skeletons score −0.393, a little above random letter strings with the same lengths (−0.435) and 1.2 permutation standard deviations above the mean of 1,000 rearrangements of its own values (−0.463, sd 0.057). Training only on the unsandhied texts, projecting anusvara to *m*, or both, moves the key to 2.0, 2.1 and 2.4 standard deviations above its rearrangements (0.5% to 2.5% of rearrangements at or above it). An order-only statistic, the order gain minus that of the same texts with signs shuffled within each text, puts the key inside its rearrangements under all four settings (z between −0.55 and +0.60). The key's small advantage comes from which consonants occur in the inscriptions; their order adds nothing Sanskrit-like. The published readings, including the consonants they add beyond the table, score −0.442, their individual word forms −0.195, and longer readings score lower (−0.25 at three consonants, −0.56 at seven). [Stage 3 summary](stage3/summary.json), [training and projection sensitivity](stage3/sensitivity_training.json), [word-level validation](stage3/validation.json), [exhaustive *n/m* choice](stage3/sensitivity_exhaustive.json), [figure](figures/order-gain.svg).

**Better arrangements of the same values exist, and they use the attested sign order.** Searching only within frequency strata, so that letter frequencies and consonant counts stay fixed (7,702 to 7,882 consonants against the key's 7,833), the best arrangements found from four rearranged starts reach +0.19 to +0.23. The same search on inscriptions whose signs were shuffled within each text reaches only +0.02 to +0.04, and those keys score −0.22 to −0.33 on the real order. Fits on half A reach +0.29 in sample and −0.09 to −0.13 on half B, where the key scores −0.39 and rearrangements average −0.47. Unconstrained swaps across all signs reach +0.47 to +0.69, but they turn 22% to 46% of sign tokens into vowels and delete up to 118 texts from the score, so they bound what deletion plus fitting can reach rather than measure Sanskrit-likeness. Holding every other sign fixed, the key's value ranks 5.96 of 15 classes by log-likelihood (rearrangements 6.14, sd 0.12) and 6.86 by order gain (rearrangements 7.25, sd 0.16); the first ranking favours frequent classes and the second rare ones. [Stage 3g](stage3g/derived.json), [deletion check](stage3/token_check.json), [local ranks](stage3f/summary-v2.json).

**The unicity distance follows from the representation the key fixes.** On the same held-out Sanskrit, an order-5 model of the full transliteration with vowels and spaces has redundancy 0.573; the fifteen-class consonant skeleton has 0.167, of which 0.096 comes from unequal letter frequencies alone. The preprint itself notes that abjads "would require multiplying the signs by the number of vowels". With its formula and N = 330, the distance is 1,980 consonants; N = 293, which drops the preprint's allowance for pooled phonemes already absorbed by the fifteen classes, gives 1,758. Order-5 models understate redundancy. If longer contexts raise the skeleton's redundancy in the same proportion as they would raise full text from 0.573 to 0.7, ρ ≈ 0.20 and d ≈ 1,620; if by the same absolute amount, ρ ≈ 0.29 and d ≈ 1,120; the reviewer's highest correction (ρ ≈ 0.42) gives about 790. The 50 longest unique clean inscriptions supply 473 key consonants (504 across all distinct records). For the lexical criterion the effective redundancy is close to zero, so no corpus length makes a reading unique under it. [Stage 1 summary](stage1/summary.json).

**If the corpus were a homophonic consonant encoding of running text, a correct key would stand far outside its rearrangements.** Held-out text of each language was encoded into synthetic corpora with the shape of the unique Indus texts: 1,859 texts with the same lengths, written with Indus sign types at Indus token frequencies by a homophonic key. The true key exceeds the mean of its within-stratum rearrangements by 27.4 standard deviations for Sanskrit, 33.2 for Latin, 37.1 for Old Tamil and 72.5 for Sumerian; no rearrangement reached it. The published key sits 1.2 to 2.4 standard deviations above its rearrangements, depending on the training and projection choices above. [Amendment 3](AMENDMENT-3.md), [derived tables](stage5/derived.json), [figure](figures/key-separation.svg).

**Random keys produce readings of the same kind.** A frequency-stratified rearrangement of the key, fixed by seed before inspection, segments all twelve of the longest unique inscriptions into attested Sanskrit nouns, adjectives and verbs. Record M-391 becomes *dātṛ-anvita-śiras-mahiṣa-ya*, whose first Monier-Williams glosses are giver, gone along with, the head, great or powerful; M-355 becomes *diś-mithuna-vimāna-nirmā-jāmi*, to point out, paired, devoid of honour, to mete out, related as brother and sister. Some extracted glosses are poor first senses of their headwords; the segmentations are the point. [Twelve random-key segmentations](stage4/demo.md).

**The published readings use freedom beyond the table.** For 1,747 inscriptions with their own reading, 69.2% of reading skeletons match the key's skeleton exactly. The remainder mostly add consonants that no sign writes (126 additions of *s* or visarga, 57 of *m*, 43 of *t*, 33 of *d*) or read the *an/aṃ* value as *m* (186 cases, which the table allows). [Stage 0 summary](stage0/summary.json).

**The corpus does not prefer Sanskrit over Latin or Old Tamil under matched freedom.** Each language's held-out text was projected onto at most fifteen consonant classes, signs were assigned classes with that language's token frequencies, and within-stratum swaps optimized order gain on half A. On half B, the fitted keys rose above random token-matched assignments by 7.7 null standard deviations for Sanskrit, 8.6 for Latin, 8.1 for Old Tamil and 6.4 for Sumerian Ur III administrative text; as shares of the gap between random assignment and each language's own text, 0.34, 0.33, 0.35 and 0.17. In 356 of the 927 half-B texts a half-A text lies within one substitution, insertion or deletion, and 84 are contained in a half-A text. Refitting with the same procedure and scoring only the 568 half-B texts with neither relation gives z = 6.3 for Sanskrit, 7.0 for Latin, 6.4 for Old Tamil and 6.5 for Sumerian (7.0, 7.8, 6.8 and 6.9 on all of B in that rerun). In the synthetic corpora scored the same way, no model of a wrong language exceeded z = 3.1. Near-duplicates do not produce the equal transfer, and the Indus regularities are fitted by unrelated languages to the same degree. The first, unconstrained version of this comparison failed through a rare-class artefact and is preserved ([Amendment 2](AMENDMENT-2.md)). [Derived values](stage3g/derived.json), [near-duplicate control](stage3g/novel_transfer.json).

**Blind recovery and language identification are only partly possible at this size.** In the synthetic corpora, annealing from random starts under the correct model recovered 51% and 71% of sign tokens for Sanskrit, 37% and 73% for Latin, 55% and 48% for Old Tamil, and 83% and 7% for Sumerian (one run failed). Half-split transfer identified the plaintext language for Old Tamil (z = 6.8 under the Tamil model against at most 3.5 under the others) and Sumerian (8.9 against at most 3.5), but not for Sanskrit, where the Latin model transferred better (2.8 against 1.6), or Latin (2.7 against 2.2 for Sanskrit). The identification depends on the seeds: in the near-duplicate rerun, with different seeds and scored on novel B, the correct model led for Latin (3.2 against at most 1.6) and Old Tamil (7.6 against at most 2.5), tied with Latin for Sanskrit (3.6 against 3.1), and did not lead for Sumerian (1.8 against up to 2.3). The Indus texts also reuse exact sign sequences far more than the synthetic corpora: 37.4% of the trigrams in half B occur in half A and 40.5% of half-B texts share an internal trigram with half A, against 8.2% to 10.1% and 3.0% to 6.4% ([n-gram reuse](stage5/ngram_reuse.json)). A key with dozens of freely interchangeable signs per consonant would spell repeated words with varying signs; conventional spellings, logograms or fixed formulae would produce exact repeats. Which of these applies is a hypothesis for the structure-first program, not a result of this study.

## What the results establish

The preprint's argument is that reading beyond the unicity distance proves the information came from the inscriptions. That holds only for a criterion whose redundancy matches the formula's ρ. Readability with free vowels, division and compounding has redundancy close to zero, so readability carries no evidence about the key; the key's dictionary fit is real but reaches between a third and a half of what its freedom allows. Under a likelihood criterion, which does have usable redundancy, a correct homophonic key on a corpus of this shape would stand 27 to 72 standard deviations clear of its rearrangements. This key stands 1.2 to 2.4 clear, entirely through which consonants it assigns, and the order of its consonants is no more Sanskrit-like than that of its rearrangements.

The result concerns this key and this evidential argument. It does not decide whether any Indus inscription records an Indo-Aryan, Dravidian or other language. The Sanskrit model is built from running Vedic and classical text, which may differ from seal formulae; that difference does not explain the gap, because the published readings are offered as grammatical Sanskrit phrases and score below random dictionary headwords. The same calibration applies to this project's own lexical work: the October 8–11 Bonta extension searched short Sanskrit residuals under imported stem values, and the October 12 anchor expansion builds complete-record semantic walks. Their support should be compared with frequency-matched rearrangements of the same assignments and with the best assignment the same freedom reaches.

## Methods

The population is the 1,859 distinct reading-order sequences of length two or more among the 2,930 complete single-line Lipi records without unknown signs. The key is `xlits.csv` from `yajnadevam/lipi` commit `fc8813a5` (716 rows: 714 signs plus the placeholders 000 and 999), with empty values of ligatures filled from their components in the app's order; 577 sign types occur in the population. Values and Sanskrit text are projected onto fifteen classes matching the key's pooling (k, g, c, j, t, d, n, p, b, m, y, r, l, v, s, with aspirates, retroflexes, nasals, sibilants and *h* merged as in `decipher.csv`), and vowels and word spaces are deleted. The *an/aṃ* value may be read *n* or *m*; Stage 3 chooses greedily along the text, and the sensitivity chooses exhaustively (key −0.351; 10% of 300 rearrangements at or above it).

The Sanskrit text is the DCS CoNLL-U release at commit `8aeed5a1` for the Ṛgveda, Atharvaveda (Śaunaka), Śatapathabrāhmaṇa, Manusmṛti, Arthaśāstra and Rāmāyaṇa; every tenth chapter file of each text is held out (82,311 training lines, 1.99 million consonants; 213,349 held-out consonants). The `# text` lines of the Ṛgveda, Atharvaveda and Arthaśāstra are unsandhied with compounds split, those of the other three sandhied. Models are interpolated Kneser–Ney with one discount per order. Inscriptions and reference spans are scored from four start symbols with no end symbol. Order gain is Σ[log₂ P₅ − log₂ P₁]/Σ|s|. The Monier-Williams lexicon is the set of 116,151 distinct skeletons of `k1` headwords in Cologne `csl-orig` `mw.txt` (SHA-256 `555ecf5a…`); the DCS lexicon has 12,767 lemma skeletons. Readability allows stems of at least m consonants followed by an optional ending in {∅, s, m, n, y, t, sy}.

N1 permutes the key's value options among signs within eight token-frequency strata; N0 permutes them uniformly. Annealing swaps two signs' option sets for 300,000 to 400,000 proposals with temperature falling geometrically from 2×10⁻³ to 2×10⁻⁶. Halves A and B (932 and 927 texts) are assigned by the first byte of an MD5 hash of the sequence. All raw sources, models and per-inscription outputs stay in `data/raw/2026-10-12-decipherment-claim-tests/`.
