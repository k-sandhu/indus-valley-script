## Statements of the claim

**Asko Parpola (2005), "Study of the Indus Script."** *Transactions of the International Conference of Eastern Studies* 50: 28–66, Tōhō Gakkai, Tokyo. Inspected in the PDF hosted by Harappa.com, <https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/indusscript(2).pdf> (local SHA-256 `13ebcfee…f5693d`), printed pp. 47–48. On p. 47 Parpola contrasts Near Eastern square seals, which carry typical Indus sequences, with round seals of Gulf and Elamite type and Mesopotamian-type cylinders, which use common Indus signs "in sequences completely dissimilar from those occurring on native Harappan texts". The same page states that the most frequent sign never stands next to itself in the Indus Valley but does so on a round seal that Parpola describes as probably found in Mesopotamia. On p. 48 he concludes that Harappan agents in the Gulf and Mesopotamia became bilingual, adopted local names and wrote them in the Indus script. The volume, page range and December 2005 date come from the PDF's printed page numbers and from bibliographic listings found by search; the printed volume itself was not checked.

**Asko Parpola (2018), "Indus Seals and Glyptic Studies: An Overview."** In Marta Ameri, Sarah Kielt Costello, Gregg Jamison and Sarah Jarmer Scott (eds), *Seals and Sealing in the Ancient World: Case Studies from the Near East, Egypt, the Aegean, and South Asia*, pp. 127–143, Cambridge University Press. The author's final proof (June 2016) was inspected: <https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/127255979/Parpola_Asko_2018_Indus_Seals_and_Glyptic_Studies_An_Overview_final_proof_2016_.pdf>. The local copy is `data/raw/2026-10-06-chronology-region/sources-2026-10-06-v1/parpola2018.pdf` (SHA-256 `c71b4c28…cd3834`). Section III.A (proof PDF page 14) says inscriptions on round "Gulf" seals and Mesopotamian cylinder seals "often have sign sequences that differ from those of South Asian Indus texts", and that square "unicorn" seals found in Western Asia have typical sequences. It attributes the difference to Indus people in the west adopting local languages and names. Section II.E (proof PDF page 12) says the round Gulf type, dated 2100–2000 BCE, is rare in the Greater Indus Valley, where four come from Mohenjo-daro. Section I.C describes the Gonur seal as bearing native Harappan sequences. Printed page numbers within pp. 127–143 were not verified.

**Asko Parpola, "Full Text Version of Deciphering the Indus Script."** Harappa.com slide essay, <https://www.harappa.com/script/parpola15.html>, inspected 12 October 2026 (local SHA-256 `d0ebb9c9…3e695ad`). Section 2 attributes the "fundamentally different sign sequences" of some West Asian texts to local proper names written with syllabic values. The page states neither its date nor an original print publication.

**Asko Parpola (1994a), "Harappan inscriptions."** In Flemming Højlund and H. Hellmuth Andersen (eds), *Qala'at al-Bahrain* 1: *The Northern City Wall and the Islamic Fortress*, pp. 304–315, Jutland Archaeological Society, Aarhus. Not inspected. Reported by Laursen (2010): the paper catalogues the Indus inscriptions found outside the Harappan sphere and compares each sequence with the native corpus (Laursen p. 115). It finds only three complete Gulf Type sequences well paralleled (Parpola's nos. 22, 30 and 31; Laursen p. 117). It counts four Near Eastern texts that begin with the "twins" sign against three in the Indus Valley (Parpola p. 309, via Laursen p. 117). Laursen p. 98 quotes Parpola p. 315: the inscribed seals in Bahrain burials "definitely point to an acculturation of Harappan traders".

**Asko Parpola (1994b), *Deciphering the Indus Script*.** Cambridge University Press. Not inspected. Rao et al. (2009, ref. 14) cite it for the observation that West Asian texts contain unusual sign combinations. The page was not identified.

**Simo Parpola, Asko Parpola and Robert H. Brunswig (1977).** Laursen prints the title as "The Meluhha villages: evidence of acculturation of Harappan traders in late third millennium Mesopotamia?", *Journal of the Economic and Social History of the Orient* 20: 129–165. Not inspected. Laursen (2010, pp. 117–118) cites it for the hypothesis that a language other than Harappan was used on these seals, and its p. 164 n. 57 for linking the "man" sign with personal names.

**Robert H. Brunswig, Asko Parpola and Daniel T. Potts (1983).** "New Indus type and related seals from the Near East." In D. T. Potts (ed.), *Dilmun: New Studies in the Archaeology and Early History of Bahrain*, pp. 101–115, Dietrich Reimer, Berlin. Known only from bibliographic records. The Tübingen KEIBI record (<https://keibi.uni-tuebingen.de/Record/KEI00064000>) prints the title as "New Indus and Related Seals from the Near Kast"; Laursen prints "New Indus related seals from the Near East". The contents were not inspected.

**G. R. Hunter (1932), "Mohenjo-Daro – Indus epigraphy."** *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* 1932: 466–503. Not inspected. Laursen (2010, p. 115) reports Hunter's p. 469 conclusion: the circular seals from Mesopotamia had unsupported sequences, while the four circular seals from Mohenjo-daro and the square Indus seals found in Mesopotamia had sequences well supported in the Indus Valley.

## Restatement and earlier quantitative test

**Steffen Terp Laursen (2010), "The westward transmission of Indus Valley sealing technology: origin and development of the 'Gulf Type' seal and other administrative technologies in Early Dilmun, c.2100–2000 BC."** *Arabian Archaeology and Epigraphy* 21: 96–134. Inspected in the copy acquired by the chronology-region package (`data/raw/2026-10-06-chronology-region/review-response-sources-2026-10-07-v1/laursen2010.pdf`; public PDF <https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/The_westward_transmission_of_Indus_Valle.pdf>), pp. 96, 98 and 115–118. The paper reports Indus inscriptions on 28 Gulf Type seals (p. 115). It groups the seals morphologically, with Indus-Valley finds in Group 1, Iran in Group 2 and Mesopotamia and Bahrain in Group 3. It finds that seals with sequences supported in the Indus Valley fall in Groups 1 and 2 and at Girsu. It concludes that "a language other than that of the Harappan culture was employed" on the Gulf Type seals of Bahrain and Mesopotamia (p. 117). Pp. 117–118 develop the patronymic reading of the "twins" and "man" signs, following Vidale (2004, 2005), and discuss a pseudo-inscription on a newly found Bahrain seal.

**Rajesh P. N. Rao, Nisha Yadav, Mayank N. Vahia, Hrishikesh Joglekar, R. Adhikari and Iravatham Mahadevan (2009), "A Markov model of the Indus script."** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 106: 13685–13690. Inspected: <https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/PNASIndus.pdf> (local SHA-256 `642d316d…3345d5`), Table 2 and text on p. 13688 (page 4 of 6). The authors trained a first-order Markov model on Mahadevan's 1977 corpus. They scored eight complete West Asian texts by raw likelihood, two of which were zero because of unseen signs, and compared them with the median of 100 held-out Indus texts of the same average length. The West Asian median likelihood was about 10⁵ times lower. The authors note that Parpola had found that "the West Asian texts often contain unusual sign combinations". The comparison matched average length only, used no object-type matching and no interval, and reported raw text likelihoods rather than per-sign rates.

**Massimo Vidale (2004, 2005).** The patronymic interpretation of the "man" and "twins" signs and its sources are recorded in [the chronology-region report](../../2026-10-06-chronology-region/REPORT.md), section "Human and paired-human signs".
