## Findings

**The key's variant groups substitute and share contexts far more than frequency-matched pairs, including pairs the Mayig graphic classes keep apart.** At the primary threshold (both signs attested at least three times in the 1,859 unique texts), the 377 variant pairs (P1) in 58 roots have a mean of 0.199 minimal pairs, 10.9 times the null mean of 0.018; 46 pairs (12.2%) have at least one minimal pair, against 1.4% under the null. Their mean neighbour divergence is 0.564 against 0.657. The 235 variant pairs whose two codes fall in different Mayig classes (P1-out) also pass both primary tests: 0.098 minimal pairs per pair against 0.021 (4.6 times; 7.7% of pairs with a minimal pair against 1.6%) and neighbour divergence 0.599 against 0.661. All six P1, P1-out and P3 tests have permutation p = 0.0005 (Holm-adjusted 0.004), so decision rule 2 is met: the key's variant grouping carries distributional similarity beyond what the Mayig classes group. Its size is much smaller than for pairs that share a Mayig class. The 71 variant pairs inside one Mayig class (P1-in) average 0.662 minimal pairs (34 times the null) and a neighbour divergence of 0.417; the difference in mean per-pair excess between P1-out and P1-in is −0.57 minimal pairs (cluster-bootstrap 95% interval −1.00 to −0.26) and +0.18 in neighbour divergence (0.11 to 0.25). The Mayig graphic pairs as a whole (P3, 80 pairs) behave like P1-in: 0.588 minimal pairs (32 times the null) and neighbour divergence 0.419.

**The key's homophones do not substitute or share neighbours beyond frequency.** The 230 pairs of base signs with the same pooled value (P2) average 0.083 minimal pairs against a null mean of 0.053 (1.57 times; p = 0.13, Holm 0.26) and a neighbour divergence of 0.668 against 0.677 (p = 0.21, Holm 0.26). Decision rule 3 is therefore not met: the same-value groups show no corpus-internal sign of interchangeability beyond frequency. On neighbours, every P2 and P2-strict sensitivity (k = 1 and 5, duplicates counted, length-two frames, β = 0, root-collapsed contexts, a null without ligature codes) stays above p = 0.05, the smallest being 0.064 at k = 5. On minimal pairs, P2 and P2-strict stay above 0.05 except when duplicate texts are counted (p = 0.021, ratio 3.3; 71 of the 121 record-level minimal pairs come from the two pairs 176/803 and 032/176, which have four unique-text minimal pairs between them). P2-all, which adds every variant code of a shared value (1,964 pairs at k = 3), gives p = 0.021 to 0.030 at the three thresholds with a ratio near 1.5. Neither sensitivity changes the primary decision. Homophone bases do share positional profiles: their positional divergence is 0.144 against 0.200 (two-sided Holm p = 0.010).

**Variant pairs share positions overall; the stroked jar variants are the clear positionally conditioned group.** P1's positional divergence is lower than the null (0.145 against 0.193, Holm p = 0.004), and so is P1-out's (0.145 against 0.192, Holm p = 0.008), so the aggregate variant relation does not look complementary. The jar group does. Unstroked jar W740 has positional divergence 0.48 from the one-stroke W741 (null expectation 0.29) and 0.53 from the three-stroke W745 (0.20), and stroked jar tokens sit at a mean relative position of 0.33 against 0.90 for W740. The preprint's jar-ordering observation holds in this corpus: of 97 unique texts with at least two jar tokens, one of them stroked, 94 have stroke counts that do not increase in reading order (48.0 expected if the jar labels in each text were shuffled; exact Poisson-binomial p = 3 × 10⁻²⁵), and 96 end with the unstroked jar (49.0 expected; p = 1 × 10⁻²⁷). This regularity is what a positionally conditioned variant would produce, and equally what distinct signs with fixed positional roles would produce.

**Variants of one root are mixed within inscriptions more often than chance, against the preprint's stylistic-variant statement.** Section 4.14 says that different forms of one allograph rarely occur together. Among 29 roots with at least two attested codes (excluding the jar root), 180 unique texts contain two or more tokens of one root, and 125 of them mix codes, against a null mean of 108.5 (95% range 97 to 120; lower-tail p = 0.998). *Post-outcome:* the fish root W220 accounts for the excess, with 65 of 67 multi-fish texts mixed against 48.3 expected; the preprint itself names fish variants as an exception. The other 28 roots together give 60 mixed texts against 59.9 expected, and two roots fit the statement individually: the tree root W390 (1 mixed of 9, 5.8 expected) and W900 (1 of 6, 4.2 expected).

**The conjunct decomposition test was uninformative.** Replacing each of the 670 ligature tokens (156 types) by its components in reading order never produced another attested unique text, and neither did the written order. The frequency-matched null produced a mean of 0.11 matches, so the corpus has too few near-identical texts differing by one expansion for this test to discriminate.

**The table is larger than the preprint's description.** The key has 88 base signs (87 attested in the corpus), 412 variant rows (eight pointing to another variant) and 214 ligature rows; the preprint's text speaks of 76 allographs. Among the 88 bases there are 18 distinct pooled values: *s* has 14 base signs, *m* 9, and vowel *a*, *n*, *t* and *d* 8 each. Counting every non-ligature code, *s* covers 93 codes, *r* 58, *t* 53, *m* 48, *y* 42, *n* 40, *a* 39, *d* 32, *l* 23, *k* 19 and *b* 13. These eleven values, each shared by at least ten codes, account for 75.9% of sign tokens in the unique texts (75.2% with duplicates); ligature tokens are 7.9%. Three variants carry a value that differs from their root's (W139 *y* under W090 *a*, W261 *tat* under W260 *t*, W722 *an* under W717 *d*), and the main app page assigns different values from the glyph page to W261, W563 and W722 because of number parsing (see Methods). Of the 1,355 variant pairs with both codes in the Mayig crosswalk, 30.4% share a Mayig class.

## Post-outcome diagnostics

These analyses were chosen after reading the primary results, to locate what drives them. Each uses the same k = 3 universe and stratified null with a separate seed.

The P1-out result does not rest on one family. The fish root W220 contributes 10 of the 23 P1-out minimal pairs (1.3 expected), and without it P1-out still has 13 against 3.8 expected (p = 0.0055) and neighbour divergence 0.605 against 0.659 (p = 0.0005). Removing any other single root gives smaller p-values. Splitting by catalogue distance gives the same picture: the 203 P1-out pairs whose Wells codes differ by more than two have 19 minimal pairs against 4.2 expected (p = 0.002).

The Wells numbering itself groups similar shapes, and adjacent codes that the key assigns to different roots carry a similar excess. The 63 pairs of non-ligature codes at most two numbers apart in different key roots have 13 minimal pairs against 2.8 expected (4.7 times; p = 0.007) and neighbour divergence 0.615 against 0.663 (p = 0.003), close to P1-out's 4.6 times and 0.599 against 0.661. Most of those minimal pairs involve the stroke signs W003, W004 and W005 (values *j*, *c* and *p* in the key) and W014–W017, which alternate in one slot as numerals would. Substitution and shared contexts therefore mark the key's variant groups as distributionally related signs, at about the level of other graphically related signs, without singling them out as allographs.

The homophone minimal pairs concentrate in two values: *r* (6 against 1.9 expected, almost all W820/W861) and *s* (9 against 3.1). The 21 pairs among the eight vowel-*a* bases have none (1.7 expected). The lower positional divergence of P2 comes mainly from *s* (0.067 against 0.189) and *y* (0.063 against 0.207).

## Methods

The population is the 1,859 distinct reading-order sequences among the 2,930 complete single-line Lipi records without unknown signs (`data/normalized/lipi_clean_rtl_singleline.jsonl`); all have at least two signs, and sign counts are token counts among them. The key is `xlits.csv` from `yajnadevam/lipi` commit `fc8813a5`. A base sign has `canonical` equal to itself; a variant points to a single other code and is assigned to the base reached by following pointers; a ligature has a hyphenated `canonical` read in reversed order. Values follow the glyph page's `resolveXlit` (own `xlit`, else root, else components). The main page's `mkregex` differs because `json-2-csv` turns codes without a leading zero into numbers and that branch overwrites a variant's own value with its canonical's; this affects W261, W563 and W722 and none of the base signs used in P2. Pooled values delete vowels and merge *h* into *s*, so *an* joins *n* and *as* joins *s* as preprint section 4.10 describes; vowel-only signs are grouped by vowel. Mayig pairs share a class when their class lists intersect; in this crosswalk each mapped code has one class.

A minimal pair is a pair of unique texts of equal length, at least three, that differ at exactly one position. Neighbour divergence is the mean of the base-2 Jensen–Shannon divergences of the left-neighbour (with a start symbol) and right-neighbour (with an end symbol) distributions, each smoothed toward the corpus-wide context distribution with weight 1; positional divergence compares initial, medial and final shares smoothed the same way. The null applies a uniform permutation of codes within frequency strata (floor of log₂ count, with strata of fewer than five codes merged downward, so the top stratum spans 130 to 902 tokens) to every pair of a set, 2,000 times, which keeps each set's pair structure and each member's frequency class. P-values are one-sided in the free-allograph direction for the eight primary tests, Holm-corrected, and two-sided for position; 0.0005 is the smallest attainable value. Effect intervals resample whole roots, values or Mayig classes so that a large family is not treated as many independent pairs.

Two assumptions limit what the tests can show. Substitution and shared contexts are necessary for free allography and are also produced by any functional class whose members fill the same slot, such as numerals or a set of titles; a passing set is distributionally related, which is weaker than being allographic. The Mayig classes are one annotator's fine-grained graphic classification, so "beyond graphic similarity" here means beyond that classification; the Wells catalogue order supplies a second, coarser graphic grouping, and the key's out-of-class pairs perform at about its level.

## What this means for the published claims

The key's variant column agrees with corpus distribution most strongly where its groupings coincide with fine graphic classes, and with a smaller, still clear excess where they cross those classes. Its homophone structure, the part that carries the Sanskrit reading, has no internal support from substitution or neighbour similarity: signs that the key reads as the same consonant do not occupy each other's frames or neighbours more than frequency-matched sign pairs do, though they share positional profiles. The stroked jars, which the preprint treats as direction markers, show a strong positional order; the general statement that variants rarely co-occur fails in aggregate because of the fish signs and holds for a few other roots.

## Next tests

1. Compare the key's variant groups with a second sign list (Mahadevan 1977 or Parpola 1994 concordances), and with groupings that maximise substitution directly, to see whether the key's out-of-class pairs exceed what a data-driven clustering of the same corpus produces.
2. Test whether the key's values track position: if consonant assignments follow position-dependent readings such as case endings, homophone groups would match on position but not on neighbours, as P2 does here.
3. Retest conjunct decomposition with local contexts (the neighbours of a ligature against the neighbours of its component sequence) rather than whole-text matches, which this corpus cannot supply.
