# Rival routes and the maturity of early literature

12 October 2026. Independent source controls and substantive review. The [final assessment capsule](reviewer-final-v1/checks.json) pins the integrated files assessed.

The controls support a history in which practical operations, specialist offices, vocabulary and explanations can follow different routes. Jamison and Brereton explicitly argue that the Rigvedic poet’s position in patronage was inherited from Indo-Iranian and Indo-European traditions. Old Avestan passages represent ritual specialization, addressed listeners and relations between song and support. Later Vedic collection and school organization can bring received materials together. These observations make intellectual maturity intelligible without assigning all its components to one earlier civilization.

A particular local contribution remains testable. Its strongest evidence would concern a distinctive operation or relation, the contexts in which it continued, and the way it entered the later represented repertoire. The coordinator’s separation of continuation, carrier and literary descent is useful because the present findings advance some of those questions while leaving others open.

## What the inherited-institution controls establish

Jamison and Brereton describe the poet’s exchange with patrons and then identify the role as inherited. They also distinguish the hymns’ composition from older poetic conventions, family collections and later consolidation. Their account makes an advanced verbal tradition an available source for early literary sophistication. Their proposed Kuru consolidation remains an attributed historical reconstruction. See printed pp. 5, 9–10 and 14–17 and observations `O-R01-1` through `O-R01-3`. [University-hosted introduction](https://vedisk.sites.ku.dk/files/2021/09/Jamison-Brereton-s.-2-15.pdf).

Skjærvø’s 2006 lessons provide concrete Iranian comparisons. Yasna 45.1 addresses listeners arriving from near and far. Yasna 33.6 gives the ritual role *zaotā*, translated as libator, with a qualified divine referent in the accompanying interpretation. Yasna 46.14 represents song, gift exchange and Vištāspa at an audition. These passages support the presence of an organized poetic and ritual repertoire in that textual tradition. They do not supply a surveyed school or independently dated royal teaching lineage. The full relevant source pages and the translation’s uncertainty were inspected; the teacher’s selected exercises are not a complete comparative corpus. See `O-R02-1`, `O-R02-2`, `O-R02-4`. [Selected teaching edition](https://www.lexicons.ru/old/a/avestan/_pdf/old-avestan-exercises.pdf).

The same edition’s Yasna 31.9–11 phrases relate a cow, cattle tender or forager, divine fashioning and thought. They specify no ploughing, seed-processing or water-lifting sequence. The Iranian comparator therefore tests broad conceptual and pastoral relations while leaving a particular agricultural operation unmatched. This distinction matters when comparing it with RV 10.101. See `O-R02-3`.

Skjærvø’s 2013 comparative essay connects poet-sacrificer roles and *hotar/zaotar* vocabulary. It also argues that inherited poet/priest labels acquired negative associations in Old Avestan. That is a positive example of the distinction between lexical continuity and unchanged evaluation of a role. His objection to projecting sovereignty from later royal narratives into Avestan remains his disputed interpretation. It should prevent an unmarked backward identification of Vištāspa, rather than settle his historical identity. See `O-R07-1` through `O-R07-3`. [Comparative essay](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kayanian-i/).

Boyce attributes a common core to haoma/soma ritual similarities while describing substantial differences. Her detailed performance account draws on a modern Bhagaria description; proposed changes tied to Old Avestan liturgy are presented as suggestions. The shared core supports an inherited-ritual alternative, with each later operation retained at its actual source layer. See `O-R03-1` and `O-R03-2`. [Boyce’s article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haoma-ii/).

An inherited poetic office can carry a newly learned local operation. The comparative control weakens a claim that local urban inheritance was necessary for every specialist institution, while leaving component-specific local contributions available. That conclusion concerns the proposed necessity claim and the inspected comparisons; it is not an exclusion of all local learning.

## What ancestry and later organization establish

Narasimhan and colleagues analyze demographic histories at a different observational scale. The northernmost South Asian transect begins around 1200 BCE; the IVC-related profile is partly modeled from culturally connected sites. Figure 3 explicitly treats its arrows as plausible directions whose exact routes remain unresolved. The modern priestly-group comparison retains qualifications about shared ancestry, nonblinded selection and changing classification. Its discussion also presents more than one Dravidian-route scenario. Independent reading and visual inspection support the coordinator’s `C-O01` through `C-O04` wording. See `O-R04-1` through `O-R04-4`. [Author-institution PDF](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/AAAS/Narasimhan_Formation_Science_2019_3166350.pdf).

These findings can locate opportunities for contact and constrain demographic scenarios. A specific recipe, craft affiliation or teaching relation is a further object of observation. Its passage between people cannot be recovered merely by assigning the same ancestry label to both endpoints.

Witzel’s chapter proposes collection, local school differentiation, neighboring-school influence and later comprehensive compilations. It also states that many final compilation and redaction dates remain unknown. Its strong local-transmission and political explanations should retain their scholarly attribution. They establish a developed research argument for textual assembly, not the first age of every component in the assembled repertoire. The inspected author PDF is a 93-page witness to the 1997 chapter; only the recorded pages were read. See `O-R05-1` through `O-R05-3`. [Author chapter](https://michaelwitzel.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/canon.pdf).

Earliest surviving literature is also a selected outcome. A preserved specialist corpus can retain elaborate verbal traditions while routine working knowledge remains unwritten or is represented only through an image. Collection can combine differently aged materials, and preservation can retain some schools while losing others. The current small frame does not estimate the scale of that selection. It identifies why the maturity of a received text is insufficient to date its whole represented society.

## A positive bridge within the textual stage

The coordinator’s new refinement of RV 10.71.2 has direct support. I read all eleven paired stanzas and checked the source-language line against the stored Sanskrit witness. The comparison marked by *iva* relates meal cleansing through a sieve to fashioning speech. The hymn also uses water-depth, thread or spinning and food-provider imagery, alongside sacrifice, affiliation and differentiated performance. This is represented conceptual reuse of practical relations within an advanced verbal account.

That observation supplies more than an occupation label: a working relation helps express what making competent speech involves. It can support the hypothesis that practical repertoires contribute to conceptual organization. The poem’s representation does not establish an apprenticeship between grain processors and poets or identify the archaeological origin of its sieve. Those routes need separate evidence. The refinement was made after reading within the prespecified hymn frame and should retain that exploratory status. Its source pin and selected reading are recorded separately from the six-work control frame in the final anchor audit.

## Material anchors and the proposed crossing

Independent inspection of the Bhagwanpura catalogue confirms the material contributor’s narrow object-stage result. BPR-36 is an unfinished roughly faceted black-jasper bead from a late Sub-period IB level. BPR-208 is one mid-IB accession described as quartz on printed p. 118 and banded agate on p. 122. The report’s printed p. 30 infers local bead making from unfinished objects and raw material, and pottery manufacture from dabbers. It also explicitly proposes that PGW users learned certain terracotta and pottery-design practices from neighboring Late Harappans. That transmission proposal is an existing author-attributed interpretation, with archaeological culture labels kept separate from authenticated ethnic or linguistic identities.

The unfinished object creates a problem for an account restricted to completed ornaments at that endpoint. Importation or curation of roughouts and movement of specialists remain possible. Neither its stage nor the author’s local-production inference identifies the proposed route into a later literary institution. The explicit field-report transmission hypothesis should be credited when describing what this continuation develops.

The mixed Harappa bead pot supplies a different control: its own description places objects from several occupation phases in one later deposited collection. A deposition date cannot become every bead’s manufacture date. Hulas’s rice-impression discussion retains identification and marsh-clay alternatives, with the latter introduced for comparative Gujarat cases. The Jognakhera supplement supplies three directly dated specimens but gives site-level entries in its dating table. It does not join each date to an individual layer or demonstrate crop rotation, same-field cultivation or a teaching chain. The additional anchor audit records these exact scopes.
