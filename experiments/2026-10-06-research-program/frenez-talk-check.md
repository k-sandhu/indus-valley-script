## Specific claims that can be tested with data

**Early writing (17:05–24:00).** Pre-firing potters' marks occur at almost all Indus sites in the second half of the fourth millennium BCE. At Mehrgarh, where recording is good, he reads them as identifying the potter or owner during a firing event rather than capacity, and notes they almost disappear when a new kiln type is introduced. Around the late fourth to early third millennium the same sign repertoire appears on seals, for example an ivory button seal from a site the captions render as "Rakandere", probably Rehman Dheri; the captions also place it in north-western Baluchistan, which should be checked against the published find. At Harappa, before 2800 BCE, seals and pottery share signs and the first clay sealings appear; a sealing discarded in a fireplace radiocarbon-dated to about 2800 BCE shows what becomes the most frequent Indus sign. He dates the conceptual foundation of the script between the end of the fourth millennium and 2800 BCE.

**Sign inventory and site size (28:45–29:30).** The full corpus has about 700 signs. The largest and most excavated sites, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, show more than 500 signs; medium or less excavated sites 100–200; small rural sites 20–50. He suggests investigating script use by context. Sign inventory size grows with sample size, so this is a rarefaction question before it is a question about site function.

**Frequency concentration and position (29:30–30:40).** Most signs are rare or unique. In Wells's corpus sign 740 accounts for 10% of occurrences, the top three signs for 20%, the top six for 30% and the top twenty for half. Sign 740 usually stands at the end of an inscription.

**The oligopoly and its persistence (41:30–43:30).** He defines an unbalanced oligopoly as a system with a limited number of components in which one holds the absolute majority: the unicorn within the Indus Valley and the "Indus bison" outside it. Such a system is unstable unless rules protect the minority components, and he observes that the iconographies kept the same frequencies for almost a millennium. Motif frequencies that stay stable across dated phases are the testable prediction.

**Diversification of inscribed media (53:30–54:10).** Around 2100 BCE he sees a change in media, a diversification of inscribed object types, which might reflect wider literacy or a reorganization of the system. Object-type diversity by Harappa period tests this.

**Inscriptions outside the Indus Valley (51:00–53:00).** Following analyses beginning with Asko Parpola in the 1980s, inscriptions found outside the valley show non-canonical sequences: unusual sign affixation and positions. Seals made outside the valley run the inscription along the round edge. Signs he calls "man" and "twins" are exceptionally frequent in these inscriptions; some colleagues read them as patronymics, the usual Mesopotamian and Gulf seal formula, and others as encoding a different language from that used in the valley.

**A forthcoming paper (16:20–17:05).** He says his group is about to publish Indus-style seals carrying cuneiform and Linear Elamite inscriptions, some encoding Sumerian names and others names that are neither Sumerian nor Akkadian. The project's external-anchor audit (`research/literature/external_phonetic_anchor_audit.md`) tracks the related paper listed as in preparation in Frenez's 2024 CV. The talk confirms it was still forthcoming when recorded.

**Later writing in India (48:25–48:55).** He remarks that oral transmission was preferred by priests in later India and that writing was reintroduced by Buddhist merchants in the fifth century. This is his statement in the talk; the backward-chronology package should weigh it against the dated early Brahmi evidence.

**Conclusion (54:30–55:10).** He concludes that it is justified to say a language, or several languages, was conveyed through the script, and that much work remains.
