# How complex institutions can precede the earliest surviving literature

The inspected texts already assume skilled making, ritual duties, patrons and gathered speech. These components can develop and persist through households, trained specialists and recurring exchanges. Continuous cities are one possible support for such knowledge, but the evidence also supplies older Indo-Iranian poetic and ritual inheritance, local farming histories and later institutional reorganization. A useful continuity hypothesis therefore follows particular operations and relationships through time. It does not require every institution to have the same origin.

This study curates 30 observations from 14 works or editions. Its developed proposal is selective transmission through specialist networks: practices that learners can repeat with available materials may survive settlement change, while practices dependent on distant procurement or concentrated political support may disappear or be redesigned. The evidence gives this proposal a concrete shape. It does not yet identify a diagnostic practice shared by Harappan archaeology, an intermediate community and a particular Vedic institution. The [observation ledger](observations.json), [claim ledger](claims.json) and [corrected audit](run-v2/audit.json) expose those missing links.

## The textual components

RV 2.1.2 addresses Agni through differentiated ritual duties and names, including hotṛ, potṛ, neṣṭṛ, agnīdh, praśāstṛ, adhvaryu and brahman, together with the house-lord. The enumeration makes sense to an audience able to recognize different responsibilities. Because the poem predicates these duties of a god, the passage does not supply the staffing chart of a human ceremony. It can support differentiated ritual knowledge without establishing the later full priest roster or the institutions of four Vedas. The Sanskrit lines and current philological commentary are pinned in observations T01 and source records S07–S08. [Restored RV Book II](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/rigveda/RV02), [Jamison commentary](https://rigvedacommentary.alc.ucla.edu/).

Other family-book passages make the social setting tangible. RV 2.1.16 connects gifts, cattle and horses with collective ritual speech. RV 5.29.15 compares making praise with garments and an artisan-made chariot. RV 6.28.6 praises cattle in a setting of assembly speech. These are representations of exchange, making and public performance. They leave the scale of production and the membership of an assembly unresolved. Jamison distinguishes the sabhā setting of RV 6.28.6 from the vidatha refrain in Book II; treating both as one named political constitution would discard that philological distinction. Observations T02–T04 retain the verse, commentary and translation locators. [RV 5.29.15 translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/rig-veda-english-translation/d/doc833105.html), [RV 6.28.6 translation](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/rig-veda-english-translation/d/doc834009.html).

Within-book chronology changes the agricultural comparison. RV 4.57.4–8 includes ploughing, draught animals and the personified furrow Sītā, but Witzel lists these verses as a later addition in his uncorrected chapter proof, internal p. 197/PDF p. 113. RV 10.34.13 advises the gambler to cultivate his field and restore his livelihood; Book X belongs to later collection material. These passages give agricultural representations, without specifying rice, rotation or intercropping. They cannot be used as the earliest core's description of the particular crop schemes found archaeologically. T05–T06 preserve both the relevant words and the stratigraphic qualification. [Witzel author proof](https://michaelwitzel.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Erdosy1995.pdf), [Restored RV Book X](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/rigveda/RV10).

The Brahmana comparison shows increased coordination directly in a ritual prescription. Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 5.3.1 visits specialist households in the royal consecration sequence. The purohita receives an offering; the courier pālāgala is dispatched along a road and included among the ruler's ratnins. Assigned gifts connect role, task and royal allegiance. Eggeling's notes also show why office labels require care: the functions of grāmaṇī, saṃgrahītṛ and bhāgadugha have disputed or changing glosses. The chapter supports a later organized representation without turning those terms into a modern ministry or dating an actual dispatch network. B01–B02 record the Mādhyandina locus and the nineteenth-century translation separately from the modern website's umbrella date. [ŚB 5.3.1](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/satapatha-brahmana-english/d/doc63275.html).

Early Buddhist texts broaden the comparison. DN 2:14.1–3 enumerates crafts and services while asking about the visible fruits of renunciation. The inspected Sujato version includes potters, dyers, embroiderers, accountants and finger-talliers. DN 5:11.7–11 gives a priest's advice to provide seed and fodder, trade funding, and food and wages for royal service. The advice belongs to the inner story of king Mahāvijita, a legendary past within the Buddha-era narrative frame. DN 16:1.4.3–8 praises frequent harmonious Vajji meetings; 1.6.11–12 applies meeting principles to mendicants. That internal transfer is especially useful: the text itself adapts a coordination rule between social settings. N01–N04 cite exact Pali and English segment IDs in the [SuttaCentral edition](https://github.com/suttacentral/bilara-data).

These later representations have an additional control: institutional growth around the early historic urban and political world. The selected suttas need comparison with independently dated settlement, production and exchange evidence from their geographic horizons. Their richness cannot by itself establish a Harappan source. Allon's analysis allows formulaic preservation and deliberate adaptation to coexist; a memorized narrative is not automatically a contemporary report of every event it describes. [Allon 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10781-021-09499-6).

## Component histories in the material evidence

The agricultural sequence offers a regional practice history. Bates, Petrie and Singh report winter and summer crops at Masudpur and Bahola. Rice use occurs in source-assigned Early, Mature and Late Harappan and PGW deposits. The chronological support differs: directly dated rice grains support Mature and Late use at Masudpur, earlier contexts have dates on other crops, and Bahola had no radiocarbon dates in that paper. Rice occurrence at Bahola extends into PGW contexts. Its four PGW spikelet contexts were excluded from the domestication analysis for low recovery, so the paper's Tables 2–4 do not provide a quantified PGW domestication endpoint. A01–A06 preserve this distinction. [Bates, Petrie and Singh 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2016.04.018).

The executable sensitivity check reassigns uncertain spikelet bases to the two endpoint categories. The lower bound keeps all uncertain bases outside the domesticated category; the upper assigns all to it. Immature bases retain their published classification. These are assemblage bounds using rounded published percentages, with no independent-trial assumption.

This is a bounded source-table result, not a domestication curve or a population confidence interval. Its code and exact inputs are in [audit-v2.py](audit-v2.py) and [run-v2](run-v2/audit.json).

Petrie and Bates give a second agricultural constraint: finding summer and winter seeds together does not establish that farmers grew them on the same plot, in sequence or simultaneously. Weed communities, processing stages and deposit types help distinguish those practices. Their Late Masudpur discussion also identifies small samples and possible single-event burning. A proposed inherited farming system therefore needs a bundle of management operations, rather than a crop name alone. A07–A08 retain those distinctions. [Petrie and Bates 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10963-017-9101-z).

The craft sequence supplies a useful contrast between components. Kenoyer distinguishes dispersed craft networks from production dependent on support for distant procurement. His bead comparison reports loss of ernestite drills and reduced access to distant materials after urban reorganization, alongside changed drilling and ornament technologies. Preliminary Sanauli comparisons and his stated gap in comprehensive PGW/early NBP technological studies leave a specific research task. Similar finished beads may be inherited objects or imports; manufacture needs its own evidence. His pottery discussion also supplies a convergence control: available clay and efficient techniques can produce long-lived similarities. C01–C05 separate observations, preliminary comparisons and proposed causes. [Kenoyer craft study](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1998_Craft%20Traditions%20of%20the%20Indus%20Civilization%20and%20t.pdf), [Kenoyer bead comparison](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer%202007%20Intl%20beads%20conf.pdf).

## A developed hypothesis: institutions carried by recurring work

A craft survives when practitioners have learners, workable materials, occasions to practise and people willing to support the product. Farming requires recurring seasonal decisions and knowledge of soils, water and processing. Ritual poetry requires trained performers, audiences and patrons. Each activity can be supported through a network smaller and more mobile than a city. Changes in settlement hierarchy may redistribute those carriers instead of removing every skill.

This gives selective transmission a discriminating prediction. Locally supplied operations with frequent practical feedback should be more likely to persist across settlement reorganization than operations dependent on rare resources and concentrated procurement. A particular hard-stone drilling recipe could fail while ordinary shaping, perforation or other ornament skills continued. Agricultural scheduling could persist while trade, weights and official records changed. The relevant evidence is a joint sequence of operations and production contexts, with demonstrable learners or repeated local manufacture where recoverable. A common object shape alone makes a weak test.

The textual counterpart is a combination mechanism. Indo-Iranian inheritance can carry ritual names, poetic forms and patronage relationships. Local communities can contribute farming and craft knowledge, practitioners and practical constraints. Later political consolidation can combine those elements into more standardized priestly and royal institutions. Schmitt's Vedic/Avestan ritual cognates and Jamison and Brereton's account of inherited patronage supply positive controls for this mechanism; Witzel's Kuru thesis supplies an explicit later-consolidation model. The presence of a sophisticated early hymn is therefore compatible with several already mature histories meeting in one textual tradition. [Schmitt, ARYANS](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/aryans/), [J&B introduction excerpt](https://vedisk.sites.ku.dk/files/2021/09/Jamison-Brereton-s.-2-15.pdf), [Witzel Kuru study](https://www.michaelwitzel.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/ejvs0104article.pdf).

A further, explicitly theoretical connection concerns complex concepts. Repeated specialist work confronts communities with competence, obligation, allocation, reliable sequences and the relation between individual roles and collective success. Oral specialists can represent these relationships through analogy, ritual and argument. Agni's many duties in RV 2.1.2 provide a poetic way to place differentiated functions under one agent. DN 16 transfers a meeting rule from the Vajjis to mendicants; DN 5 reasons about how supporting different livelihoods changes collective security. These examples show forms of institutional reasoning in the texts. The hypothesis is that practical dependencies supplied material for abstraction long before the surviving redactions. Establishing a particular Harappan contribution would still require its own component history.

This proposal predicts uneven continuity: stable operations can coexist with altered role names, and stable names can coexist with altered coordination. It also predicts that local additions need not displace inherited poetic or ritual knowledge. These are testable patterns, rather than a demand that a single civilization or language explain every element of an early text. No broad methodological priority is claimed; this package develops selected comparisons and freezes their evidence and failure conditions.

## Tests and adversarial constraints

The [test plan](TESTS.md) specifies four follow-ups. A bead study should classify drill-hole microstructure, abrasives, heat-treatment choices, material sources and production waste blindly across dated regional phases, separating finished heirlooms from local manufacture. An agricultural study should combine directly dated short-lived remains with weed and processing associations, using matched ecology and deposit type to test convergence. A philological study should code role–action combinations in selected family cores, additions, later RV and Brahmana material while retaining Old Avestan cognate controls. A Buddhist comparison should examine independent parallel recensions before treating a policy speech or meeting rule as a historical institutional memory.

The corrected categorical audit shows how the evidential requirements change this selected collection. Its counts describe our ledger, with no prevalence or probability interpretation.

| Restriction | Textual observations retained | Candidate component hypotheses eligible |
|---|---:|---|
| All curated material | 12 | Agriculture, craft, priests, patronage, assemblies |
| Remove flagged late RV and later textual material | 4 | Craft, priests, patronage, assemblies |
| Also require authenticated locus-specific family-core dating | 0 | None |
| Require a source-reported intermediate archaeological candidate | 12 | Agriculture, craft |
| Also require a diagnostic feature crossing archaeology and the textual locus | 12 | None |

The zero in the third row records the absence of locus-specific core authentication in this inspected set. It is not a claim that the family-book verses were all composed late. The craft candidate includes preliminary Sanauli finished-bead evidence; local manufacture and secure operation continuity remain to be established. The last row records the central unfinished empirical task: none of the five proposed links yet has the cross-domain diagnostic required to identify transmission. New matching evidence could change that row; adding more generic examples would not.

The shared inferential limits belong together. Hymn composition, collection, manuscript witness, narrative time and archaeological context are separate axes in every observation. J&B's 1400–1000 BCE assessment is retained as a contentious author estimate for the hymns, without assigning it to an excavated institution or every verse. A family-book location alone does not authenticate a core stratum. Metaphor attests a representation, prescriptions specify ideal arrangements, and narratives may adapt inherited material. Similar skills can arise through convergence, while shared Indo-Iranian terms can carry pre-South-Asian inheritance. The package therefore identifies candidate mechanisms and operations for testing, without assigning Harappan language, ethnicity or authenticated Vedic identity.
