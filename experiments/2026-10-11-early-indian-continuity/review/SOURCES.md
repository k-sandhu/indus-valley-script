# Chronology and source controls

The earliest surviving Indian texts represent skilled, organized traditions. Their literary sophistication does not determine when the represented institutions began. A hymn can use inherited poetic conventions, a later compiler can join family traditions, and a narrative can place familiar social relations in a remote past. These are different histories. The [chronology ledger](chronology.json) records archaeological or physical time, composition, collection or redaction, extant witness and narrated time separately. The [source ledger](sources.json) records exactly what was inspected.

The dates below retain their source and scope. No date is inferred from a text's sophistication, a story's stated antiquity or a proposed identification of a river. The corrected [backward-chronology retrospective](../../2026-10-06-backward-chronology/amendment-2026-10-07-review-corrections/RETROSPECTIVE.md) supplies the relevant precedent: broad phase ranges and collections lacking specimen-to-context joins cannot establish a transmission chain.

It does not identify the speakers or historical ancestry of a later institution. | Rigvedic hymns | Jamison and Brereton offer approximately 1400–1000 BCE, or the second half of the second millennium, while identifying older inherited poetic conventions. | This is their contentious philological/material estimate for the surviving hymns. Related Iranian traditions are necessary controls. The inspected comparative essays discuss common ritual and religious elements while separating the sources and developments used to reconstruct them. A modern Iranian rite, a Young Avestan prescription and an Old Avestan passage cannot be treated as one uniformly ancient observation. Shared elements also do not reconstruct a complete ancestral institutional system. [Mary Boyce, “HAOMA ii. THE RITUALS”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/haoma-ii/), [Gherardo Gnoli, “INDO-IRANIAN RELIGION”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/indo-iranian-religion/), [Jean Kellens, “AVESTA i. Survey of the history and contents of the book”](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/).

Preservation needs a similarly precise unit. Allon's comparative study describes texts designed for memorization alongside community-sanctioned changes, including narrative development. This makes fixed recitation compatible with revision. It supplies no general error rate or automatic calendar date for a story. The article's publication is 2022; its acceptance and copyright are 2021, which explains the older year in the local capture filename. [“Early Buddhist Texts: Their Composition and Transmission,” Introduction and narrative-change discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8789374/).

The root control case has a directly inspectable relation: SB 11.5.1 embeds quoted dialogue in a developed prose frame and ends in a prescription for fire production. Jamison and Brereton's judgment that the frame distorts or misunderstands the hymn is identified as their assessment. An older unrecorded backstory remains possible. The review checks the selected Sanskrit and historical translation without assigning a calendar date to the narrated event. [University teaching edition of selected RV 10.95 verses](https://vedisk.sites.ku.dk/rv-10-95-1-pururavas-og-urvasi/), [SB 11 Sanskrit](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/1_veda/2_bra/satapath/sb_11_u.htm), [Eggeling, SB 11.5.1.1–17](https://www.sacred-texts.com/hin/sbr/sbe44/sbe44025.htm).

The reviewer also checked SB 1.8.1.6 in the [GRETIL Sanskrit text](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/1_veda/2_bra/satapath/sb_01_u.htm). Its descent compound supports the intended reading “Manu's descent” against a transcription glitch in the web translation. This repairs a textual reading; it supplies no modern geographical identification.

## Access and independence

Acquisition and reading are separately recorded. The Kenoyer PDF is image-only: its printed pp. 9–13 were visually inspected, and empty automated extraction was not counted as a reading. The two university PDFs are overlapping excerpts of the same Jamison–Brereton work. The Śatapatha translations and electronic texts are editions of the same underlying text. They check transcription and interpretation, rather than multiplying historical witnesses.

Witzel's 1997 chapter remains a metadata-only access in this review. The author-host PDF failed certificate validation; the institutional PDF route returned a bot-check page. Its detailed contents are not claimed as independently read. The inspected 2014 introduction explicitly attributes the collection hypothesis to Witzel. The British Museum object record returned a direct-access 403; its search description is not counted as a complete source reading.

Hultzsch's *Inscriptions of Asoka* was inspected through Internet Archive OCR and a teaching transcription, including the Calcutta-Bairat text at printed pp. 172–174. Its facsimile plates were not acquired. The ruler's recommendation of named teachings is a written representation of organized Buddhist audiences. Linking those titles to every word of an extant discourse would require an additional textual argument. [Critical edition](https://archive.org/details/InscriptionsOfAsoka.NewEditionByE.Hultzsch), [transcription and stated limits](https://www.payer.de/quellenkunde/quellen031.htm).

All acquired PDF, HTML, OCR and rendered witnesses remain under the local raw review directory. The tracked package contains our summaries, locators, hashes and code. Access to a public source did not become assumed permission to redistribute its assets. The source ledger retains the pandemic-duration notice on Allon's article without assuming that the temporary permission still applies.
