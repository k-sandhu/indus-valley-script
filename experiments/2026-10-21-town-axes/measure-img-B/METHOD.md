# Method of measurer img-B: automatic orientation from satellite imagery

Measurer img-B estimates the direction of the walls and streets of each Harappan town from image gradients in Esri World Imagery, working one part of a town at a time. Three other measurers work on the same towns without seeing our numbers: img-A picks individual lines by hand on the same imagery, and plan-A and plan-B measure published plans. We read only the measurement brief, the town list, our own directory and the sources we measured; we wrote only in `experiments/2026-10-21-town-axes/measure-img-B/` and in `data/raw/2026-10-21-town-axes/img-B/`.

## Shared assumptions

All angles are measured in Web Mercator pixel space. The tiles are in EPSG:3857 with north up, and the projection is conformal, so a direction computed from global pixel coordinates (x east, y south) as `atan2(dx, -dy)` is a bearing from true north at that point. A line's bearing runs from 0 to 180 degrees. Its deviation is the bearing folded to the nearest cardinal direction, from −45 to +45 degrees, positive when the line is turned clockwise; a line at 7 degrees and a line at 97 degrees both have deviation +7. Because an orthogonal layout has two perpendicular families of lines, every automatic value we report is a deviation, and the bearing fields of an area measurement give the two perpendicular bearings that correspond to it. Pixel size at zoom 18 is 156543.03 × cos(latitude) / 2^18 metres, between 0.46 and 0.56 m at our sites.

## Imagery

We used the Esri World Imagery tile service at zoom 18. The service's identify endpoint, queried at each town, reports a maximum map level of 18 for every block of current imagery we met (Vantor Vivid mosaics of WorldView-2, WorldView-3 and Legion images at 0.31 to 0.5 m), so zoom 19 tiles would only be resampled from the same data and we did not use them. For each town we downloaded the tiles covering the site, pasted them into a PNG mosaic, and recorded the zoom, the tile range, the SHA-256 of the mosaic and the acquisition date, sensor and resolution that the identify endpoint reports. Every source record carries the attribution "Source: Esri, Vantor, Earthstar Geographics, and the GIS User Community". Tiles, mosaics and every image derived from them stay under `data/raw/2026-10-21-town-axes/img-B/`, which Git ignores.

Where the current imagery shows the architecture poorly we also used the Esri World Imagery Wayback archive, which serves earlier releases of the same map through the same tile scheme. For a chosen tile we asked the archive's tilemap endpoint which release actually holds the image used by each of the roughly two hundred releases, fetched one tile from each distinct holder, and compared them side by side. At Lothal the release of 13 October 2021 shows the low walls of the acropolis and lower town much more clearly than the current one; at Harappa the release of 15 August 2024 shows the walls inside the southern fenced excavation. We measured those parts on the archived images and repeated the measurement of a part visible in both releases (the Lothal dock, the Lothal north-western block and the Harappa two-row building) as a second witness. The acquisition date of an archived image comes from the release's own metadata layer, from the finest layer whose zoom range covers zoom 18.

## Locating the towns

The coordinates in the town list were treated as guesses. For each town we took published coordinates from Wikipedia, Wikidata or the archaeological-site nodes of OpenStreetMap, inspected a wide mosaic around them, and placed the town on the remains visible in the imagery. Each town file states the source of the coordinates and what the imagery shows there. Where neither the published position nor its surroundings showed a recognisable mound or wall (Surkotada, Juni Kuran, Kotada Bhadli) we say that the location is not confirmed.

## Polygons around Harappan architecture

We inspected each site on gridded crops of the mosaic, enlarged up to six times and, where the remains were faint, contrast-enhanced with CLAHE. Around the exposed Harappan architecture of each part of a town we drew polygons in mosaic pixel coordinates, then converted their vertices to longitude and latitude for the town file. Modern and later features were kept out by the polygon boundary or cut out as holes: the Kushan stupa and monastery on the Mohenjo-daro citadel, visitor paths (including a straight cleared band at Mohenjo-daro that could pass for a street), perimeter fences and roads, protective roofs and covers, site buildings, trench edges, ponds, and the field and canal grids, which in Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan follow the cardinal canal-colony layout. Where a polygon unavoidably contains a modern feature on the line of an ancient one, such as the visitor path along the main street of the Dholavira middle town, the town file says so. Each polygon is one part of the town (a citadel, an excavated block of the lower town, a dock), and at Mohenjo-daro, Harappa and Lothal we also measured the union of several polygons to give one value for a larger part.

## The automatic measurement

Inside a polygon we convert the mosaic to luminance and compute the gradient with derivative-of-Gaussian filters of scale 1 pixel. We keep the pixels whose gradient magnitude lies above the 60th percentile of the magnitudes inside the polygon, so that the histogram is built from edges rather than from texture. The gradient azimuth `atan2(gx, −gy)` of each kept pixel, folded modulo 90 degrees, equals the deviation of the edge that produced it, because an edge runs at right angles to its gradient and the fold removes the difference. We add each pixel to a histogram of deviation with 0.25-degree bins, weighted by its gradient magnitude, smooth the histogram circularly with a Gaussian of 1 degree, and locate its highest peak by fitting a parabola through the peak bin and its two neighbours. This peak is the automatic value of the part.

Alongside the peak we record the magnitude-weighted fourth-harmonic mean within 10 degrees of it, every other local maximum higher than a tenth of the main peak above the histogram floor and at least 3 degrees from a higher one (the secondary peaks), the share of edge weight that falls in the near-north and the near-east family when the histogram is left unfolded over 180 degrees, the peaks of the four halves of the polygon's bounding box, and the contrast of the main peak over the floor. A control polygon on bare ground near each measured site gives the histogram of natural texture, against which a spurious cardinal peak from the imagery itself would show.

## Errors

The error of an automatic value combines resampling and sensitivity. For resampling we divide the polygon into square blocks 30 m on a side (60 m for the combined lower town of Mohenjo-daro), keep a separate histogram for each block, draw blocks with replacement 1000 times, and take the standard deviation of the resulting peaks. This treats neighbouring walls as correlated and measures how much the value depends on which subareas the polygon happens to contain. For sensitivity we repeat the measurement with gradient scales of 0.7, 1 and 1.5 pixels and magnitude thresholds at the 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles, rotate the image content by +17 and −29 degrees and subtract the rotation from the recovered peak, and rotate it by ±2 and ±5 degrees and re-encode it as a JPEG of quality 75 before measuring again. The standard deviation of these fourteen variants about the default peak is the sensitivity error, and the reported one-sigma error is the root sum of squares of the two. A part whose histogram has two near-equal peaks therefore receives a large error, because rotation and recompression move the maximum from one peak to the other; we also mark such parts as low confidence and say why.

The rotation and recompression tests address a specific risk. The tiles are JPEG images with 8 × 8 blocks aligned with the pixel grid, and discrete gradient operators respond slightly differently along the grid axes than across them; either could pull an orientation peak towards exactly north. We tested this on synthetic images of short wall-like segments in two perpendicular families at known deviations, drawn on an eight-times supersampled canvas and averaged down. The method recovered the true deviation within 0.16 degrees from 0 to 8 degrees, and within 0.17 degrees after JPEG compression at quality 90 or 75, with no systematic pull towards north. Only at quality 50, much harsher than the quality of about 85 that the tile quantisation tables imply, were deviations of 0.5 and 1 degree pulled about halfway towards zero. Our first synthetic test drew the segments with integer pixel endpoints and showed a strong spurious pull towards north; that came from the drawing, which quantised the segments themselves, and disappeared with supersampled rendering. The script and its output are `code/validate_synthetic.py` and `synthetic_validation.json`.

## Checks by eye and check lines

For every polygon we drew an overlay: the enlarged image with a grid of lines at the peak direction and at right angles to it, the polygon and its holes, and the smoothed histogram underneath. We looked at each overlay to confirm that the grid runs along the walls and streets rather than along a modern feature, and to identify what produced each secondary peak; the result of that check is the `eye_check` field of each area structure.

As a check on the automatic value we measured individual lines from their endpoints. The OpenCV line-segment detector was run inside each polygon; collinear pieces of one edge (within 2.5 degrees and 2 pixels, with gaps of up to 12 pixels) were merged and fitted with a total-least-squares line, and merged lines longer than a minimum of 8 to 15 m, depending on the site, were drawn with numbers on an enlarged crop. We accepted by eye those that follow a wall face, a fortification face, a street edge or a platform edge, described each one, and took its endpoints from the fit. A line's one-sigma error is `degrees(√2 × e / L)`, where L is its length and e is the endpoint uncertainty, taken as the larger of one pixel and the fit's rms residual. These lines come from the same image and the detector also uses gradients, so they are not an independent measurement; they test whether the area histogram is dominated by the long walls the eye sees, or by something else.

## Systems

Each system in a town file is one part of the town. Its `deviation_deg` is the automatic peak of that part's polygon and its `se_deg` is the combined error described above. The check lines in the same part give the length-weighted mean deviation, its standard error (the larger of the scatter-based value, `sd / √n_eff` with the effective number of lines from the length weights, and the propagated measurement error), the standard deviation of the individual lines, their total length and their number; these appear both under `check_lines` and in the `sd_deg`, `total_length_m` and `n_lines` fields of the system. The main system is the part with the longest and clearest Harappan lines, and each town file gives the reason for the choice. Parts that are turned differently from the main system are kept as separate systems.

## Results by town

We measured Mohenjo-daro (two excavated areas of the citadel mound and five lower-town blocks, plus the lower town as one area), Harappa (two conserved buildings on the northern mound, a trench beside them and a fenced excavation further south, two of them also on the 2024 release), Lothal (the dock, two areas of the acropolis and two lower-town groups, on the 2026 and 2021 releases) and Dholavira (the castle, the reservoir east of it, the bailey and the middle town). Within these towns some parts give unreliable automatic values, because their walls are faint or short and the histogram has no single peak; the town files mark them as low confidence.

We could not measure Kalibangan, Banawali, Rakhigarhi, Chanhu-daro, Kanmer, Shikarpur or Surkotada. At Kalibangan and Banawali the mounds are backfilled or eroded, and the current and archived images show only tracks and, at Kalibangan, one ambiguous straight strip. At Rakhigarhi the recent trenches appear only as protective covers, shelters and trench outlines. Chanhu-daro and Kanmer are scrub-covered mounds without visible walls, and Shikarpur lies under a dense stand of trees. At Surkotada neither the published coordinates nor the ground around them show an identifiable mound or wall. Of the optional towns we examined Juni Kuran, Sutkagen-dor, Nausharo, Kotada Bhadli and Bhirrana, found no measurable Harappan line at any of them, and wrote town files that record what is visible and why we did not measure.

## Problems and how we resolved them

All measurements are at zoom 18, because the current imagery at every site is published to level 18 only. Requesting one tile from each of the roughly two hundred Wayback releases was slow and failed for releases that hold no copy of their own; asking the tilemap endpoint which release holds the tile gave the distinct images in a few seconds per site. An imagery seam runs east–west just south of the Dholavira castle; all Dholavira polygons lie north of it, in one image. At Mohenjo-daro a straight, cleared visitor path runs along the east side of the northern block and continues south through unexcavated ground; it looks like a long street in the imagery and we excluded it after following it across the site. At Lothal the square outline around the warehouse units may be a modern revetment of the platform edge, so we left it out of both the polygon and the check lines. The disk filled while we were writing PNG inspection images; we switched all overlays and crops to JPEG and made the contrast-enhanced crops in memory, which did not touch the mosaics we measure.

## Code and reproduction

The scripts are in `code/` and run from the repository root with Python 3.13:

- `tiles.py` downloads and caches tiles, builds mosaics, converts between longitude–latitude and global pixels, and queries the identify endpoint and the Wayback archive.
- `overview.py`, `survey.py`, `view.py`, `enhance.py` and `wbsheet.py` make the overview mosaics, gridded and contrast-enhanced crops, and the contact sheets of Wayback versions that we inspected by eye.
- `orient.py` holds the gradient histogram, peak finding, block bootstrap, rotation and recompression tests, and the line-bearing functions.
- `candidates.py` proposes check lines with the line-segment detector, and `accept.py` copies the lines we accepted (listed in `code/specs/<town>.json`) into the town configuration.
- `configs/<town>.json` holds every decision made by eye: sources, polygons, holes, control area, accepted check lines, system definitions and the descriptive text.
- `measure.py <town>` computes everything from a configuration and writes `towns/<town>.json`, with diagnostics (control area, bootstrap and sensitivity details, half-polygon peaks) under `diagnostics`.
- `make_unmeasured.py` writes the configurations of the towns we could not measure, and `write_sources.py` writes `sources.json`, which lists the services, the gazetteer lookups, every identify response and every mosaic with its SHA-256.
- `validate_synthetic.py` runs the synthetic test of grid and compression bias.

Running `python experiments/2026-10-21-town-axes/measure-img-B/code/measure.py mohenjo-daro` (and likewise for the other towns) reproduces a town file from the cached mosaics, or downloads the tiles again if the cache is empty; the mosaic hashes in the town files show whether the service now returns the same imagery.
