The Indus script: findings

What the inscriptions, their catalogues, and a series of statistical, archaeological and simulation studies show about an undeciphered writing system.

Introduction

A writing system no one can read

Between about 2600 and 1900 BCE, people of the Indus civilization carved short rows of signs on stamp seals, moulded them on small tablets and scratched them on pottery. Several thousand of these inscriptions survive, from more than a hundred places between Afghanistan and western India, and from trading partners as far as Mesopotamia.

Nobody can read them. There is no bilingual text, the language of the writers is unknown, and most inscriptions are only three or four signs long.

This document sets out what can be established about the script without reading it, which methods would work if outside evidence appeared, and how much of the evidence is recorded well enough to use.

Introduction

Why it matters

The last unread script of the first cities

Egypt, Mesopotamia and the Indus valley produced the Old World's earliest large cities. Egyptian hieroglyphs and Mesopotamian cuneiform were deciphered in the nineteenth century; the Indus script is the one that remains unread.

The civilization's own voice

Everything else known about the Indus people comes from excavated objects and from what Mesopotamian texts say about a land they called Meluhha. The inscriptions are the only records the Indus people made themselves.

A test of method

Many readings have been announced and none has been accepted. Showing what can and cannot be established, and how a claimed reading should be tested, is useful whatever the script turns out to encode.

Introduction

Goals

The work set out to make progress that can be checked, without assuming a language or a meaning for any sign. It had five goals:

1

Measure the structure

Find what the inscriptions show about their own structure, using only the signs and their objects, with controls for copies, sample size and object type.

2

Audit the evidence

Establish how complete and well documented the record is: which inscriptions are indexed, what is known about each, and where the gaps are.

3

Test published claims

Check specific statements, from a numerical proposal to a recent lecture and a resemblance study, against exact counts.

4

Test reading methods

Try code-breaking and statistical methods on constructed systems whose answers are known, before trusting them on the real script.

5

Find anchors, share tools

Identify the outside evidence that could tie a sign to a number, a use or a word, and build resources others can use: an open corpus, an object ledger and a site map.

Introduction

Five questions kept apart

Claims about an unread script often jump from one level to another: from a pattern in sign order to a language, or from a picture to a word. This work keeps five questions separate and asks, for each result, which level it actually reaches.

The first two levels can be studied from the inscriptions alone. The third needs archaeological context. The top two need evidence from outside the script, such as a bilingual text, a named owner or a counted quantity.

Part I

The civilization and its writing

QuestionWho made the inscriptions, and why are they still unread?

Short answerAn urban society along the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra rivers, at its height about 2600–1900 BCE, with planned cities, standard weights and trade reaching Mesopotamia. Its writing survives only as short texts on small objects, with no bilingual and no known language behind it.

The civilization

Where

The civilization grew along the Indus River and its five Punjab tributaries, and along a second, now mostly dry river system to the east, called the Ghaggar in India and the Hakra in Pakistan. Its sites lie mainly in modern Pakistan and northwest India, from the Makran coast to western Uttar Pradesh and south to Gujarat, with a trading outpost at Shortughai in northeast Afghanistan.

Published estimates of its extent range from about 0.7 to 1.25 million km², depending on where authors draw the boundary. More than 1,000 Mature Harappan settlements are known, of which about 100 have been excavated.

Possehl 2002; Wright 2010; Khan and Lemmen 2013; Prabhakar (e-PG Pathshala)

The civilization

When

Archaeologists divide the sequence into an Early (or Regionalization) phase, a Mature (Integration) phase of about 2600–1900 BCE when the cities flourished, and a Late (Localization) phase. The Mature cities were contemporary with Egypt's Old Kingdom pyramids, the Akkadian empire and the Third Dynasty of Ur, and had ceased to be urban before Hammurabi's reign.

Kenoyer and Meadow 2010; Possehl 2006; Shaw 2000; Van De Mieroop 2016; Woods 2010; Chadwick 1987; Liu and Xu 2007; Salomon 1998. Mesopotamian dates follow the Middle Chronology.

The civilization

How it was discovered

Charles Masson described the mounds at Harappa in 1829. In the 1850s, railway contractors took Harappa's bricks for track ballast. Alexander Cunningham, later the first Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India, published the first Indus seal in 1875 and took it for a foreign object.

Excavation began at Harappa in 1920–21 under Daya Ram Sahni and at Mohenjo-daro in 1922 under R. D. Banerji. In September 1924 John Marshall announced a previously unknown civilization in the Illustrated London News.

After 1947 most known large sites lay in Pakistan, and survey in India intensified, bringing Lothal, Kalibangan, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi to light.

Cunningham 1875; Marshall 1931; Vats 1940; Kenoyer and Meadow 2010; Gadd 1932; Rao 1979–85; Lal et al. 2003; Bisht 2015. Dholavira was listed by UNESCO in 2021.

The civilization

Planned cities

Mature Harappan cities were built on raised platforms with streets running roughly north–south and east–west. Many were divided into a walled high mound, often called the citadel, and a lower residential town. Harappa has four major mounds; Dholavira has a citadel, a middle town and a lower town.

Houses were built around courtyards. Many had bathing floors and latrines that drained into covered brick drains along the streets. Mohenjo-daro had an estimated 700 or more wells, their positions apparently fixed when the city platforms were first built.

The Great Bath on Mohenjo-daro's high mound is a sunken tank of about 12 × 7 m and 2.4 m deep, of fitted baked brick sealed with bitumen. It is most often read as a place of ritual bathing; no text identifies it.

Kenoyer 1998; Possehl 2002; Jansen 1993; Kenoyer and Meadow 2010

The civilization

Water and the Lothal basin

Dholavira's reservoirs

In the dry Rann of Kutch, Dholavira's builders cut a series of large stone-lined reservoirs inside and around the fortified town and diverted water from seasonal streams into them.

The Lothal basin Disputed

At Lothal, S. R. Rao excavated a brick-lined basin about 218 m long and 37 m wide and identified it as a tidal dockyard. Leshnik (1968) and later Possehl read it as a reservoir or irrigation tank; recent sediment studies have been presented in support of the dockyard reading.

Rivers

Harappa stands by the Ravi and Mohenjo-daro by the Indus. Many sites in Cholistan and Haryana stand along the dry Ghaggar-Hakra, whose history is central to debates about the cities' decline.

Bisht 2015; Rao 1979; Leshnik 1968; Possehl 2002; Giosan et al. 2012; Singh et al. 2017

The civilization

Crafts, farming and daily life

Crafts

Long carnelian beads drilled with specially made stone drills; bangles cut from conch shell; glazed faience; steatite seals fired to harden the surface; cast and hammered copper and bronze tools, vessels and ornaments.

Farming

Wheat and barley in winter; millets, rice and pulses in summer, many settlements using both seasons. Cotton was cultivated, with the earliest cotton fibres in the region at Neolithic Mehrgarh. The main livestock was humped zebu cattle, with water buffalo, sheep and goats.

Daily life

Terracotta figurines of people and animals, model carts with solid wheels, whistles and gaming pieces are common in houses and streets.

Inscribed objects were made in many of these materials: terracotta, stoneware, faience, shell, bone, ivory, sandstone, steatite, gypsum, copper, silver and gold.

Kenoyer 1998; Kenoyer and Vidale 1992; Petrie and Bates 2017; Moulherat et al. 2002; Kenoyer and Meadow 2010

The civilization

Weights and measures

Harappan cubical stone weights, most often of banded chert, follow one series across the whole region: the smaller weights double (ratios 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) and the larger ones continue in decimal steps. The weight at ratio 16 is about 13.7 g. The same weights already appear at Harappa before the cities, in the Kot Diji phase.

Three marked rulers are often cited for length: a shell fragment from Mohenjo-daro with divisions of about 6.7 mm, an ivory scale from Lothal with divisions of about 1.7 mm, and a bronze rod from Harappa with divisions of about 9.34 mm. Whether they share a unit is debated.

Part V reports a new fit of the Dholavira weights and a test for Indus weights abroad.

Kenoyer 2010, pp. 116–117; Hemmy 1937–38; Kenoyer and Meadow 2010

The civilization

Trade and Meluhha

Mesopotamian cuneiform texts name a distant land called Meluhha, which most scholars identify with the Indus region. Sargon of Akkad boasted that ships from Meluhha, Magan (modern Oman) and Dilmun (Bahrain) moored at the quay of his capital. Texts also mention a Meluhhan village in Mesopotamia.

A cylinder seal now in the Louvre (AO 22310) belonged to Shu-ilishu, whose inscription names him an interpreter of the Meluhhan language. Indus-style seals were found at Ur and other Mesopotamian sites from the 1920s, and etched carnelian beads of Indus manufacture in Ur's Royal Cemetery. In the north, Shortughai lies near the Badakhshan lapis lazuli sources.

Possehl 2006; Gadd 1932; Kenoyer 1998; Francfort 1989

The civilization

Society and belief

No palaces or royal tombs

No palace, royal tomb or large temple has been identified, and no image or text names a ruler. The "Priest-King" bust from Mohenjo-daro owes its name to modern excavators.

Governance Debated

Possehl argued for urban complexity without a state; Kenoyer for competing city-states run by merchants, landowners and ritual specialists; Jacobson and Ratnagar for a centralized state. Uniform weights, bricks and seals fit several of these models.

The "proto-Śiva" seal Disputed

Marshall saw a horned seated figure surrounded by animals as an early form of Shiva. Srinivasan and Hiltebeitel read it through buffalo symbolism; Parpola accepts a link to Shiva by another route. The comparison rests on images made some two thousand years later.

Fire altars and burials

At Kalibangan, clay-lined pits with ash were read as fire altars, though others see hearths. Most burials are extended inhumations in cemeteries outside the settlements, with pottery and ornaments.

Possehl 1998, 2002; Kenoyer 1997, 1998; Ratnagar 1991; Marshall 1931; Srinivasan 1975–76; Hiltebeitel 1978; Parpola 1994; Lal 1997; Wheeler 1947

The civilization

Decline and transformation

Between about 1900 and 1700 BCE the large cities shrank or were abandoned, and seals, weights and the script went out of use. Settlement shifted toward smaller villages, holding up or growing in Haryana, the upper Ganga–Yamuna area and Gujarat while the Hakra valley in Cholistan emptied. Possehl called it a transformation rather than a collapse. Most current authors combine several causes:

Rivers

The Ghaggar-Hakra was fed by monsoon rain in Harappan times rather than by glaciers, and the Sutlej had left its channel thousands of years earlier; declining floods would have hit towns along it.

Climate

Lake and cave records show the summer monsoon weakening around 4,200–4,100 years ago. Farmers adapted with drought-tolerant crops, and effects differed by region.

Trade

Mesopotamian references to Meluhha fall away after the Old Babylonian period, and Gulf trade shifted to Dilmun as intermediary.

Invasion Abandoned

Wheeler blamed invaders for skeletons in Mohenjo-daro's upper levels. Dales (1964) showed they came from different levels and periods, with no destruction layer.

The writing

What they wrote on

Indus signs survive on small, hard objects. No long documents are known; if the Indus people wrote on perishable materials, nothing has survived.

Seals are small carved stamps, usually square, often with an animal below a line of signs, mostly of steatite hardened by heating. Tablets are small flat or prism-shaped pieces, moulded in relief, incised, or of copper, often made in many identical copies. Sealings and tags are lumps of clay pressed with a seal. Signs also appear scratched on pottery, and on bangles, rods and tools.

The writing

Seals are carved backwards

A seal works like a rubber stamp. The carver cut the signs in mirror image, so that the clay impression, the sealing, shows them the right way round. Catalogues record seal texts as they read on the impression.

The back of a sealing often keeps the print of whatever it was pressed onto: a cord, a cloth, a jar neck or a wooden board. Sealings are therefore direct evidence of how seals were used.

The writing

What the signs look like

A sign is one distinct shape. Some resemble people, fish, animals, plants, tools or containers; others are strokes, grids and abstract forms. Sign lists number them, so "sign 740" means the shape a list numbers 740. The numbers label shapes and carry no sound or meaning, and resemblance to an object does not establish what a sign stood for.

The writing

How many signs, and how often

The main corpus uses 714 sign codes, and 219 of them occur only once. Small details separate signs that look alike: two short strokes and two tall strokes are different signs, and fish signs carry extra marks. Where one sign ends and its variants begin is a scholarly judgement, so sign lists range from about 400 to over 700 units.

A handful of signs does most of the work. Sign 740, a U-shaped jar-like sign, makes up a tenth of all readable positions and usually ends an inscription (Part IV).

The writing

The texts are very short

Half of all inscribed faces carry three signs or fewer. The average is 3.5 signs, and the longest single face in the corpus (M-314, from Mohenjo-daro) has 17 sign positions.

A two-sign text gives very little context, and the same short texts repeat across many objects. Both facts limit every statistical method, and both appear repeatedly in the results that follow.

The writing

Direction of writing

Most Indus texts read from right to left. Where a line runs out of space, the last signs are squeezed or turned, which shows where the writer ended. Catalogues store each line as it appears on the page, so each checked single line is reversed to obtain reading order; a published example and a labelled photograph of seal M1088 confirm the convention.

Prediction models do almost as well on reversed texts (a gain of 0.775 bits instead of 0.890), so statistics alone cannot settle direction. The evidence for direction is the physical spacing of the signs.

The writing

A century of decipherment attempts

Proposals began within a decade of the discovery. By 1985 Mahadevan counted more than forty published claims of decipherment, none generally accepted. They disagree on the language behind the signs (Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Munda or unknown) and, since 2004, on whether the signs record language at all.

Alongside the readings, scholars built the tools every later study uses: Mahadevan's concordance (1977), the Helsinki corpus and the photographic CISI volumes (1979 onward), and the ICIT database.

The writing

Why the script is unread

  • No bilingual. No inscription has a matching text in a readable script. Indus seals found in Mesopotamia carry no cuneiform translation.
  • Unknown language. Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Munda and lost languages have all been proposed.
  • Short texts. Three or four signs per text give little context.
  • Several hundred signs. More than an alphabet (about 20–40) and fewer than a word script such as Chinese, which suggests a mix of word signs and sound signs; this remains unestablished.

Renaming every sign consistently leaves every count and pattern unchanged, in the same way that swapping every 3 for a 7 in a solved sudoku leaves it solved. In a six-sign test system, all 720 possible renamings score exactly the same. Patterns alone cannot say what a sign means.

The writing

What a reading would require

Each kind of outside fact ties signs to something real, and each fixes something different:

A counted collection with a label

Fixes the value of a number sign. Example: a container of 12 weights labelled with a sign group.

A known material or activity

Fixes the function of a sign or seal. Example: sealings whose backs show they closed grain sacks.

An archive naming a seal's owner

Fixes a name. Example: a Mesopotamian text naming the owner of an Indus seal.

Matching texts in a known script

Fixes sounds and meanings. Example: a bilingual inscription.

A proposed reading must also predict evidence held back from its author, judged by someone who has not seen the answer. Every claim tested here is held to that rule.

Evidence

Sign lists disagree

Each sign list draws its own line between "one sign written differently" and "two different signs". The 1979 Helsinki chart has 687 cells; Carlson's 2024 annotation uses 396 classes linked to 498 Wells numbers; the main corpus has 714 codes.

The links between lists run many-to-many. One annotation class covers two Wells signs (817 and 861), so two signs that appear to swap places may be two catalogue spellings of a single sign. Overlapping classes do not chain: if class A overlaps B and B overlaps C, A and C need not match.

Catalogue object numbers also begin with letters. "M331" is an object from Mohenjo-daro and "H904" an object from Harappa.

Two dictionaries that disagree on whether "colour" and "color" are one word or two give different word counts for the same book. Both counts are correct for their own definitions.

Evidence

The working corpus

No complete, openly licensed, independently coded corpus is available. The analyses use two public exports, pinned at exact versions, keeping only their observation fields.

Evidence census Partly addressed

What has been done about the gap

Classified. Each of the 166 absent entries was assigned to its printed catalogue section, which separates likely inscriptions (91 seals, impressions and tablets; at Lothal, 34 seals and 25 impressions) from pottery graffiti (75). No other site label in the corpus carries any of them.

Entered in the object ledger. Twenty of them, mostly Lothal sealings in the L124–L216 range, now have ledger records as catalogue-only objects, linked to their printed plates. Their texts have not yet been transcribed, and whether each carries signs or only a design is still to be confirmed.

Routes identified. The census names where the missing texts can come from: the CISI vol. 1 plates themselves, a permissioned ICIT export, and ASI museum and archive registers. No request has yet been sent.

What would close it

Transcribing the 91 seal, impression and tablet entries directly from the CISI vol. 1 photographs, with two independent readers and an adjudicator, as was done for the 1979 corpus. This would add their texts to an open record without waiting for outside access.

Evidence census

What the corpus records about each object

Even for inscriptions that are in the corpus, much of what an archaeologist needs is missing. Fewer than half of the faces have a depth or a room, one in five has all three measurements, and none has a calendar date, a photograph link or reuse rights.

Dating therefore works through the layers and periods that objects come from, which is also how the find-site map dates its sites.

Evidence census

Inscribed objects in museum catalogues

Online museum catalogues expose at least 114 original inscribed Indus objects at six institutions. At least 68 join to corpus records through catalogue concordances and excavation numbers. Of the 46 that do not, 31 have no usable key in the museum record.

Bibliography is missing from 101 of the 114 museum records, so most cannot be traced back to an excavation report from the museum page alone.

Making the evidence usable Pilot

One record per object

An object ledger gives each physical object one record that links every number it carries, along with its findspot, dates, holdings, sources and rights.

A pilot of 200 objects across 34 sites (180 in the corpus, 20 known only from CISI vol. 1) added 26 museum accession numbers, 22 excavation-unit pairs and 62 attributed date ranges. 26 museum joins are confirmed, 34 are candidates and 134 questions await specialist review. A validator catches all 26 deliberately planted errors.

The first sample drew its groups in alphabetical order and ran out inside Harappa, so the Lower Indus, 46% of the frame, entered only through museum objects. A second, randomly ordered sample corrects this.

Digitizing the 1979 corpus

The main corpus cannot be redistributed. The 1979 Koskenniemi–Parpola corpus can (CC BY 4.0), but only as page images, where each inscription is a printed row: drawn signs, a number and a five-part classification code.

A reading pipeline finds and cuts out each row, counts the drawn signs, reads the number and code, compares each drawing with the 687-cell chart, and proposes up to five codes per sign.

Its predictions were fixed before scoring. Two independent readers transcribed sample rows from the page images without seeing the predictions, and a third settled their disagreements.

Part III

Methods

QuestionHow can an unread script be studied without guessing its language?

Short answerBy measuring how well its own patterns predict new texts against simpler baselines, by shuffle tests that respect copies and selection, and by trying every method first on constructed systems whose answers are known.

Methods

Predicting the next sign

If sign order follows rules, knowing one sign should help predict the next. A Markov model (or n-gram model) counts how often each sign follows each other sign in known texts and uses those counts to rank likely next signs.

  • First-order looks one sign back; second-order looks two back.
  • Smoothing reserves probability for combinations never seen in training; Kneser–Ney is a refined form.
  • GRU and transformer networks learn sequence patterns from examples.

A phone keyboard suggests "you" after "thank" because it has counted what people type. A Markov model of Indus signs does the same with sign numbers, without knowing what any sign means.

Methods

Measuring surprise in bits

A good model is rarely surprised by the true next sign. Surprise is measured in bits: one bit is the surprise of a fair coin toss, and each bit saved halves the number of equally likely options. Log score, cross entropy and probability loss all name average surprise in bits; lower is better.

Knowing the previous sign saves about 0.89 bits per sign: the right answer becomes nearly twice as likely (20.89 ≈ 1.85 times) as for a model that knows only position, length, site and object type.

Guessing a card from a full deck carries about 5.7 bits of surprise. Learning that it is red removes 1 bit, because half the cards are ruled out.

Methods

Restoration tests

Top-one accuracy is how often the first guess is right; top-five, how often the right sign is among the first five. Calibration asks whether a model that reports 70% confidence is right about 70% of the time. The benchmark hides 8,009 positions in 1,596 distinct texts, one at a time. These gaps are artificial: the length is known and the neighbours are intact, which real broken objects rarely offer.

Methods

Baselines, controls and shuffle tests

Baseline

A simpler method that a new one must beat: a model that knows only a sign's position and frequency, or a rule that always gives the same answer.

Control

A case where the answer should be no. A test for "language" that also says yes for an office-routing log does not identify language.

Shuffle (permutation) test

Shuffle the signs many times in a way that destroys the pattern under test but keeps lengths, positions and sites, rerun the analysis, and see how often chance does as well. The p-value is that share: with 1,000 shuffles and none as good, p = 1/1001.

Bootstrap interval

Resample whole groups of related texts and report the middle 95% of scores, written "0.890 (0.817–0.965)". An interval that crosses zero means the difference could go either way.

Shuffles and resamples must respect how records were made. If two records are copies of one object, treating them separately creates false independence and makes chance look like discovery (Part VI).

Methods

Faces, objects and copies

A face is one inscribed side of an object; an object family groups the faces of one object. Many objects carry exactly the same text: the 5,680 faces contain only 3,504 different sign sequences.

If one copy of a text trains a model and another copy tests it, the model only has to remember. All tests group records that share an object or an identical text and keep each group on one side of every train/test split.

An exam built from questions the students saw in their homework measures memory. Keeping copies together prevents that.

Methods

Constructed systems with known answers

Many studies build an artificial record system with a known answer, hide the answer, and check whether a method recovers it. Success shows the method works under the stated conditions; failure shows where it breaks before it is used on the Indus script.

Sentence-like systems

Each sign depends on the one before, like words.

Administrative forms

Each position has its own role, like the fields of a delivery note.

Number systems

Signs stand for values; containers hold counted items.

Known real systems

Inca khipu (knotted cords with decimal values), totem poles and weather icons.

Methods

  • Each study specifies its data, test, comparison and success criteria before it runs.
  • Results are written to new records and never overwritten; corrections sit beside the original.
  • Failed results are kept, with the reason and the evidence that would justify a retry.
  • Headline numbers are recomputed by independent code and checked against source pages.
  • Every source file and output carries a checksum.

Part IV

What the signs show

QuestionWhat structure do the inscriptions have, and what can that structure tell us?

Short answerSign order follows strong rules that carry over between sites and transcribers, and a hidden sign can be restored about 35–41% of the time. The same kind of structure appears in record systems that are not language, so structure alone cannot say what the script encodes.

Sign order Supported

Sign order carries information

A model that knows the previous sign predicts new texts better than one that knows only position, length, site and object type. 2,107 texts were split into five parts with related texts kept together; each part was predicted by models trained on the other four.

The gain holds for distinct texts only, with look-alike signs merged, for whole sites held out, and for a second, independent transcription. In 1,000 shuffled versions the gain averaged −0.103 bits and never reached the real score (p = 1/1001).

Sign order Simulation

Order alone does not show language

A constructed office-routing system tests this: 3,000 records in which each sign names an office and the next office follows a legal hand-off, with no language involved. The previous-sign model beat slot-filling templates by 0.631 bits on the office system and by 0.748 bits on the Indus texts.

Totem-pole carvings (180 sequences) showed almost no order gain (0.0117 bits, p = 0.356), but 43.7% of their test signs never occurred in training, against 2.17% for the Indus texts. Weather-forecast icons (10,142 forecasts) formed one linked group, so no fair split was possible.

Rule-governed order is what any organised record system produces. The test detects structure; it cannot distinguish language from administration, a point Sproat (2010) made more generally.

Restoration Supported

Restoring a hidden sign

Each of 8,009 sign positions in 1,596 distinct texts was hidden in turn. A model that sees only position gets the first guess right 16.99% of the time. Models that see the neighbouring signs reach 33.8–41.2%; a combination of Kneser–Ney counting and a small neural network does best.

All 205 signs that occur only once are missed, since a model cannot propose a sign it has never seen. Accuracy still rose 1.2–1.7 points between 75% and 100% of the training data, so more transcribed text should help more than larger models. The transformer, which needs far more data, did worst.

Restoration Supported one negative

Accuracy versus calibrated probability

Looking two signs back raises first-guess accuracy from 34.59% to 37.27% (gain 2.68 points, 1.99–3.40) but worsens average surprise from 5.119 to 5.247 bits. When the setting is chosen to minimise surprise, every test fold selects plain first order.

Eighteen pre-specified variants with extra smoothing tried to keep the accuracy gain without the cost. Every fold again selected first order. That family of fixes is ruled out.

Removing the end-of-text component lowers accuracy at the last position from 53.95% to 48.25%; shuffling the middle of each training text drops overall accuracy to 24.52%. Both the endings and the interior order carry information.

Restoration Negative result

Counting missing signs

When a stretch of text is damaged, can a model tell how many signs are missing? One gap of one to three signs was erased inside each text. The best model was 4.76 points worse than always answering "one" (−7.78 to −1.73).

Sign context does not reveal how wide a gap is. A useful predictor would need physical evidence: the measured width of the damaged area, or surviving traces of carving. No real test case exists yet either: none of 17 annotated faces with a single damaged section could be certified as exactly one missing sign.

Sign frequency Exploratory

How concentrated sign use is

A few signs do most of the work. Sign 740 alone is 10.67% of the 18,067 readable sign positions; the top 20 signs cover 48.76%; 100 signs cover 84.4%. The other 613 signs share the remaining 15.6%, and 219 occur only once.

Sign 740 is also positional: 874 of its 1,256 occurrences (69.59%) end an inscription, against 299 expected if position were random. Its use does not vary with site size or type across 17 sites once object type is controlled.

Signs and seals

Testing statements from a lecture

In a 2026 lecture, "Memorizing Meanings: Archaeological Perspectives on Writing in the Indus Civilisation", the archaeologist Dennys Frenez argued that writing was invented locally between the end of the fourth millennium and 2800 BCE, that Indus people chose not to write long texts, and that the corpus should be divided into regional and period sub-corpora.

He described seal motifs as an "unbalanced oligopoly": a few motifs, with the unicorn dominant inside the Indus valley and the "Indus bison" (gaur) outside it. The statements the corpus can test were checked against exact counts, with object type, sample size and copies controlled. Interpretive claims, such as seals working like corporate badges, are recorded as his positions (Frenez 2018, pp. 186–187).

Chronology and region

Why period comparisons at Harappa are difficult

Sign-frequency differences were measured with the Jensen–Shannon divergence, a value in bits where 0 means identical, and period labels were shuffled among whole objects up to 19,999 times to see what chance produces.

The two periods also come mostly from different parts of the site, dug by different teams. A period difference could therefore be a difference between mounds or recording practice. On Mound E alone, the sign-frequency difference is not significant (p ≈ .065). The rise in object types survives this restriction; the rise in text length does not.

Regions differ in four of six pairs under one grouping of objects and two of six when identical texts are grouped too. Because each site lies in one region, a regional effect separate from site cannot be measured.

Seals Exploratory

Seal size and text length

  • Across 1,027 complete measured square seals, size varies with a coefficient of variation of 0.29: seals came in many sizes rather than one standard.
  • Larger seals carry longer texts: 1.16 more signs for each doubling of face area.
  • Texts on unicorn seals average 0.78 signs longer than on seals with rare motifs; with length held fixed, the signs they use do not differ.
  • Among the ten most common signs, only sign 032 differs between sites after correction for multiple tests.

Jamison (2018) and Green (2018) studied seal carving and production; these measurements add the relation between seal size and text length.

Part V

Numbers, objects and connections

QuestionCan physical objects, weights and finds abroad give the script an outside anchor?

Short answerNot yet. A published numerical proposal depends on an unverified premise; the weight system is precise but carries no signs; no bilingual exists; and no dated chain links the script to later Indian writing. The studies identify which objects would settle these questions.

Numbers Supported

A published number proposal, solved exactly

Andreas Fuls proposed values for some Indus number signs from two equations, v = 2S − L and vL + 1 = 7S + 5, where L, S and v are the values of three signs, and selected L = 10, S = 8, v = 6.

The equations reduce to (2L − 7)S = L² + 4, so 2L − 7 must divide 65. That leaves exactly four whole-number solutions.

Only an added assumption that L lies between 8 and 13 selects 10/8/6. That range rests on a bangle count the cited source does not establish, and an argument that S must be even fails (L = 10, S = 9, v = 8 is a counterexample to it). Vessel capacities cannot separate a unit from a stroke value either: one volume fits 40.4 × n and 4.04 × 10 × n equally well.

Numbers Simulation

What physical evidence could fix a number sign

If a container labelled X holds items labelled Y and Z, then X = Y + Z. Independent equations of this kind fix the ratios between values; one counted object fixes the scale.

  • In 24 constructed collections, twelve complete containers fixed the ratios every time and one count fixed the scale.
  • With incomplete contents, 17 of 24 were recovered; seven still allowed two answers, such as (6,8,1,2,4,3) and (12,8,2,4,8,2).
  • Counts reported with understated error gave a confident wrong answer in 21 of 24 worlds.

A real candidate exists: the Susa clay envelope SB 1940 in the Louvre holds three round tokens and three rods.

Numbers Supported

Khipu as a real check

Inca khipu record numbers in knotted cords with known decimal places, so they test number-recovery methods on real records. A published Puruchuco khipu was reproduced in 16 of 20 account columns (total error 16; the best of 10,000 random arrangements had error 738), and constructed decimal worlds were recovered 299 times in 300.

"Keep the answer that satisfies the most equations" can fail when errors repeat: in one case a wrong answer satisfied 7 equations and the truth 6. An independently counted complete inventory fixed most such failures; treating an incomplete inventory as complete produced 15 wrong answers.

Resemblance Pilot

Do South Indian graffiti match Indus signs?

A 2025 Tamil Nadu study reported Indus parallels for about 90% of graffiti marks on pottery. Simple marks of a few strokes resemble each other across unrelated scripts, so a resemblance rate needs a chance baseline.

The 42 Tamil Nadu base signs were described by strokes, loops and other features and compared with 36 Indus signs, 20 Ogham letters (early medieval Ireland) and 32 Tifinagh letters (modern Berber). Later scripts Exploratory

From the Indus script to later writing

If Indus signs passed into later Indian writing such as Brahmi, dated marked objects should form a chain between them. Dated marked material from 3500 to 200 BCE was placed in 100-year intervals and graded for how securely the marks belong to the dated layer and how reliable the date is (65 items from 50 sources).

Every century has some dated marked material in the broad record, but among well-dated, well-associated items the gap is 400 to 1,600 years long, depending on how Pirak and Perumbalai are dated. No dated chain links Indus signs to later scripts.

Later scripts

The two ends of the timeline

The early end

Potters' marks were made before firing in the fourth millennium BCE; at Mehrgarh a failed firing left about 140 marked jars. Seal use appears by the early third millennium, and a sealing at Harappa is attributed to the Kot Diji phase (about 2800–2600 BCE).

Rehman Dheri object 54 is a button seal from Period IA or IB, and the site lies in the Gomal plain rather than northwestern Baluchistan.

The late end

Frenez places the return of writing in the fifth century BCE. The dated Brahmi evidence includes Anuradhapura (about 450–350 BCE), Porunthal (580–520 and 560–410 BCE), Kodumanal, Keeladi and the Ashokan inscriptions (272–232 BCE).

These dates constrain when writing returned, not who reintroduced it. Four test designs specify the evidence that would distinguish transmission from independent invention.

Part VI

Testing reading methods

QuestionIf outside evidence did exist, would standard code-breaking and statistical methods find the right reading?

Short answerOnly under conditions that have to be checked. Mappings can be confidently wrong when the reference comes from a different kind of record; copied claims and copied texts inflate confidence; and the right comparison depends on how the records were made.

Method tests

The code-breaking approach

Suppose a known script had its symbols swapped by a secret substitution. With ordinary text in the original script as a reference, one can search for the swap, the mapping, that makes the coded text look most ordinary. A symbol whose identity is known from outside is an anchor.

The Indus script has no known reference. Practice on constructed systems shows how mappings fail, how many records they need and which outside facts help.

The first lesson is a symmetry: in a six-symbol constructed corpus, all 720 possible renamings score identically. Fixing two identities reduces the 720 to 24.

Method tests Simulation

Confident and wrong

In 24 constructed eight-symbol systems, sentence-like systems were always recovered. Form-like systems whose coded records came from a slightly different process (35% changed) were recovered 0 of 12 times with 40 records and no anchors, and 26 of 96 wrong answers claimed at least 95% confidence.

Accounting for the small size of the reference cut confident wrong answers in unchanged form systems from 10 of 108 to 0, and in changed systems from 29 to 9.

Treating ten catalogue copies of one mistaken reading as ten pieces of evidence gave 46 confident wrong mappings out of 48. Counting the source once gave 11; modelling the copied error directly gave 4.

Ten newspapers repeating one rumour are one source.

Method tests Simulation

What to check, and when to stop

Which sign to verify

Asking about the sign the model is least sure of needed 1.05 fewer checks than random choice (0.70–1.40).

When to stop

Stopping once the model reported 95% confidence produced more wrong stops: 60 of 96 against 44 for random. Uncertainty guides what to ask well and when to stop badly.

Held-back checks

A few extra identity checks after a proposal reached high confidence caught all 60 wrong proposals in the hardest case. With 8 unchecked identities, checking 6 at random lets a wrong mapping pass with probability 1 in 28.

Method tests Simulation

Choosing the right comparison

Graph matching draws each system as a network of which sign follows which and overlays the two networks, like matching two road maps of unlabelled towns. Slot matching compares where each sign sits in a record. Each keeps information the other discards.

Testing both on the reference first, then using the better one, recovered 309 of 384 mappings: almost the 310 achieved when told the true system type.

When records share one length and many symbols, recovery falls. Of 62 remaining failures in the hardest setting, 56 were cases where the scoring itself preferred a wrong answer. An exact identification argument (Allman, Matias and Rhodes 2009) confirms that the full data determine the hidden record types in all 36 tested profiles.

Method tests Simulation

Fair shuffle tests

Do shuffle tests still report "significant" only 5% of the time by chance when the data contain copies? Each case used 1,000 constructed populations, each shuffled 99 times.

Treating copies as independent makes chance look like discovery about three times too often. Removing duplicates also shifts the error rate, and some tables cannot be fitted at all.

A test can also look well behaved because it is blind to the mechanism that matters: with shared recording quality, the original statistic moved only from 3% to 4%, while a statistic built for agreement separated the cases at 94.2% against 1.0%.

  • Vidale (2004, 2005), following Parpola (1986), discussed the "man" and "twins" signs on seals found abroad as possible patronymics.
  • Kenoyer (2020) places inscribed tablets in Harappa Period 3B and wider media in 3C.
  • Ascalone (2022) reported the heavier small-weight group at Dholavira.
  • Sproat (2010) argued that sign-order statistics cannot by themselves identify language; a 2026 preprint built a constructed non-linguistic system that reproduces Indus-like statistics.

What these studies add

Object-type adjustment, whole-object resampling, sample-size controls and chance baselines applied to one documented corpus; measurements of how complete the evidence is; exact audits of published claims; and method tests on systems with known answers.

Prior work

What researchers recommend

A survey of published recommendations collected 56 from 27 sources, covering 21 of 25 named researchers in the field.

  • Recurring advice: analyse by region and period; keep seals, tablets, sealings and pottery apart; build open, shared data.
  • Open dispute: whether statistical regularities can show that the signs record a language. Farmer, Sproat and Witzel argue they cannot; Rao and colleagues argue they can.
  • Highest-ranked action: a ledger linking each object to its sources, date, site and type, which the census and the object ledger have begun.

Conclusions

What would move the work forward

An open corpus

Complete the 1979 digitization, either through a better row-cutting stage or checked readings of every row.

The missing entries

Transcribe the 91 seal, impression and tablet entries absent from the corpus directly from the CISI vol. 1 photographs, with two readers and an adjudicator.

One record per object

Extend the object ledger beyond its 200-object pilot and resolve its review questions.

Archive records

Field registers for Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, and post-catalogue finds at Dholavira, Kalibangan, Farmana and Rakhigarhi.

Fresh text

Newly transcribed inscriptions for the fixed restoration model.

An outside anchor

A counted object tied to an inscription (H904, M-1383), or Lothal sealing backs coded blind to their texts.

The script will be read, if at all, through an object whose use, quantity or owner is known independently of its signs. These studies show which objects to look at and how to test a reading once one is proposed.
