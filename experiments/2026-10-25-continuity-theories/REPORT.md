# From Indus farming communities to later South Asian societies

25 October 2026. Exploratory historical synthesis and three new conjectures. GPT-6, Codex API workspace harness; exact model build unavailable. Verification tier: `submitted`.

Our working theory is that later South Asian societies formed through selective inheritance and recombination. Farming households and specialists carried practical knowledge across changes in settlement, language and political organization. Communities exchanged crops and techniques; speakers adopted some local words; later ritual specialists organized inherited and acquired knowledge within new institutions. This develops the project's [11 October continuity study](../2026-10-11-early-indian-continuity/REPORT.md), with the [crop audit](../2026-10-25-crop-continuity/REPORT.md) as a new starting point. The mechanism has substantial precedents and remains a theory to test.

The crop evidence supports persistence of elements of a practical repertoire with changing proportions. Identifying a language, a ritual lineage or a political institution requires its own observations. We keep those claims in three [conjecture cards](#the-three-conjectures), with subjective starting credences and predictions. None has passed an empirical confirmation test.

## What this suggests about Indus society

The farming repertoire required people to reproduce knowledge of seasonal timing, soils, crop processing and seed selection. Its survival directs attention to the communities that performed and taught those operations. In our model, a household, a local network of cultivators or a specialist workshop could retain useful knowledge when a larger urban institution changed. A seed store, a field and a working technique can continue through several forms of authority.

That account predicts uneven survival. Operations supported by local materials and recurring practice should travel through disruption more readily than ones dependent on distant procurement or large coordinating systems. Our earlier craft study identifies this as a useful comparison. The new crop pilot does not measure the organization of landholding, teaching or authority, so it cannot choose between household autonomy, communal management and supervision by an elite. We need production contexts and operational evidence for those distinctions.

The historical unit becomes a practice with a carrier: a named crop and its processing, a drill and its manufacturing sequence, or a procedure and the people who teach it. A broad cultural label can contain several such histories. Retention of an edible plant and continuity of its cultivation method are separate measurements.

## How the Vedas enter the model

The Vedic texts preserve language, poetic traditions, ritual prescriptions and representations of social life. Jamison and Brereton place the received Rigvedic hymns approximately in the second half of the second millennium BCE, with a proposed 1400–1000 BCE bracket and explicit chronological uncertainty. Their introduction also distinguishes the much older Indo-Iranian and Indo-European ancestry of poetic forms. [Jamison and Brereton 2014, introduction pp. 5 and 15](https://vedisk.sites.ku.dk/files/2021/09/Jamison-Brereton-s.-2-15.pdf).

This gives a concrete historical model. Communities practicing established regional agriculture interacted with speakers carrying Indo-Aryan speech and inherited Indo-Iranian traditions. Their descendants could retain farming operations while changing vocabulary, institutions and explanations. Vedic composition, later collection and subsequent ritual elaboration belong to different stages of that process.

RV 4.57.4–8 names ploughing, draught animals, the furrow and productive fields. The [metrical text](https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/rigveda/RV04) supplies the Sanskrit locus. Our prior chronology review records these verses in Witzel's later-addition list, so their position in a family book does not by itself date them to its earliest stratum. The hymn is a starting place to study how agricultural work enters ritual language. Its common farming features do not yet identify a specific Harappan ancestor.

Witzel's Kuru reconstruction and Jamison and Brereton's discussion propose that later political consolidation collected family traditions and reorganized ritual. That is a source-based historical argument about Vedic institutions. It explains how later complexity can be assembled from older components, while leaving the ancestry of each component open. We do not infer Harappan social ranks, a Vedic caste system or named deities from the crop assemblage.

## Sanskrit and the other language histories

Sanskrit belongs to Indo-Aryan, within Indo-Iranian and Indo-European. Its relationship to Iranian languages, including Avestan, rests on systematic linguistic correspondences. Those comparisons provide a control for proposed local inheritance: an element already reconstructed for Indo-Iranian has an explanation independent of a particular Harappan-to-Vedic route. [Kümmel 2022, sections 14.1 and 14.3](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108758666.014).

| Language history | Relation to this study | Evidence we would use |
|---|---|---|
| Old Indo-Aryan, including Vedic Sanskrit | Inherited Indo-Iranian speech interacting with regional communities | Textual layers, regular correspondences, loan histories and attested meanings |
| Dravidian | A separate language family with proposed prehistoric contact with Indo-Aryan | Comparative reconstruction across branches and independently dated regional archaeology |
| Munda, within Austroasiatic | Another potential contributor to contact histories in central and eastern South Asia | Supported loans, reconstruction, local chronology and alternative donor analyses |
| Unidentified earlier languages | Some transmitted vocabulary may have lost its donor language | Mark unresolved etymologies as unknown; do not force them into a surviving family |
| Middle Indo-Aryan and later regional languages | Further changes and exchanges after the Vedic period | Dated inscriptions, Pali and Prakrit texts, dialect history and later comparative evidence |

Classical Sanskrit, a cultivated and regulated language, coexisted with developing regional Indo-Aryan speech. A single line from Classical Sanskrit through every Prakrit to every modern Indo-Aryan language would misstate that history. Jamison's account treats Middle Indic as a range of related varieties, with some characteristic developments already visible in Rigvedic vocabulary and with continued coexistence alongside Sanskrit. [Jamison 2008, “Middle Indic”](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511486838.006).

Agricultural continuity can therefore cross a language boundary. A family can keep cultivating a crop while descendants adopt a different language, retain a crop name as a loan, or replace that name. Shared words must be tested for borrowing and common inheritance; shared crops alone do not select one of those histories.

## A concrete bridge: horsegram

Horsegram (*Macrotyloma uniflorum*) occurs in the Jognakhera paper's plant assemblage. Fuller and Murphy independently compare its archaeology with historical linguistics. They discuss reconstructed Dravidian *kol-* or *kol-ut*, Sanskrit *kulattha* and Juang *kulto*, following earlier comparative work. They propose transmission of a crop name across language families. Their study also infers separate northwestern and peninsular domestication trajectories from seed measurements. [Fuller and Murphy 2018, online 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10722-017-0532-2), historical linguistics section and abstract.

This is a tractable research lead because plant identity, morphology, chronology, word forms and attestations can be checked separately. The authors' proposed direction of lexical borrowing remains a hypothesis. Their evidence does not make the Jognakhera specimens ancestral to all southern horsegram or locate the donor speech at that site. Multiple cultivation histories and later circulation of the name are competing explanations.

We would begin by reconciling archaeological plant names, including older botanical misidentifications, and collecting dated occurrences. A separate lexical ledger would preserve original forms, meanings, first attestations and proposed sound correspondences. A joint test would ask which routes fit both ledgers, while allowing crop dispersal and name dispersal to occur at different times.

## Several regional histories

| Region or connection | Historical question |
|---|---|
| Greater Punjab, Haryana and the upper Gangetic plain | Which local farming operations continue through settlement changes in the region later described by Vedic texts? |
| Gujarat and Saurashtra | How do millet-rich assemblages, local ecology and connections with other regions change independently of northern trajectories? |
| Deccan and southern India | Which crops and words reflect local development, interaction with the northwest, or later exchanges? Horsegram allows explicit competing routes. |
| Middle and eastern Gangetic regions | How do established rice cultivation, settlement growth and changing speech communities contribute to later northern traditions? |
| Afghanistan, Iran and Central Asia | Which contacts concern crops and materials, which concern population movement, and which concern the inherited linguistic and poetic ancestry of Indo-Iranian? |

These are comparison routes, not demonstrated migration arrows. The earlier Indus farming literature already places summer crops before urban decline, so a proposed later interaction cannot treat every rice or pulse occurrence as a new post-1900 BCE arrival. We should compare dated local sequences before combining them into a subcontinental narrative.

Population genetics contributes an independent record of mixture. Narasimhan et al. 2019 model post-urban movements and ancestry combinations, while explicitly retaining both an Indus-related and a peninsular scenario for Dravidian dispersal. Kerdoncuff et al. 2025 show substantial regional variation and refine ancestry proxies; they also state that the archaeological relation of the “Indus Periphery” proxy individuals to the civilization is uncertain. Those studies constrain population histories. Identifying a language still requires linguistic evidence. [Narasimhan et al. 2019, discussion](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat7487), [Kerdoncuff et al. 2025, population-structure results and limitations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.04.027).

The numbers are our starting judgments about the claims as stated. They are not probabilities estimated from the crop percentages. One of three proposals is a long shot. The [test design](TEST-DESIGN.md) gives acquisition, independence and joint-update rules. The [synthetic bench output](bench-shots-synthetic-v1.json) checks workflow guards only, and supplies no score for these historical claims. No real confirmation material was opened.

The strongest present model is a network of practical inheritance and cultural recombination: older farming and craft communities contributed to later societies whose languages and institutions had their own changing histories. A source-linked crop and operation sequence paired with independently established borrowing histories is the most useful next test.
