# Additional primary anchors for the manuscript revision

This bounded follow-up checks examples raised by the Claude review in F6, F14 and F17. It adds supporting cases to the existing argument and windows. `additional-anchor-sources.json` records twelve sources, exact locators, reading depth, local witness hashes and open questions. Acquired witnesses are local-only. Full institutional pages and selected passages in acquired papers were read; an acquired file is not counted as verified evidence unless its relevant passage was inspected.

## Deep burial, living villages and removed buildings

**Mohenjo-daro, AP01.** Dales's 1965 primary account reports borings that recovered brick fragments, sherds, bangles and ash to 39 feet below the surrounding plain, approximately 11.9 m. Groundwater then lay about 15 feet below the plain. This directly supports a known Indus example in which occupation evidence extends beneath alluvium and groundwater limits access. The measurements describe the project reported in 1965. They establish neither today's groundwater depth nor a phase-specific boundary of continuous buildings. The relevant locator is the pair of paragraphs beginning “One of the first objectives” and “A boring rig,” with the boring-rig caption. [Dales, *Civilization and Floods in the Indus Valley*](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/civilization-and-floods-in-the-indus-valley/)

The publisher's volume contents verify Kenoyer's 2021–22 *Heritage* article, ‘The Integration and Diversity in the Indus Tradition (2600-1900 BCE): Comparing Cities, Towns and Villages,’ pp. 1–29. Its indexed passage mentions occupation beneath the water table, but full-text access failed and the supporting reanalysis was not read. The separate large scanned deep-digging compilation was acquired without locating the relevant chapter. These access paths supply no new chronological claim for integration. [Official volume contents](https://heritageuniversityofkerala.com/VolumeDetails.aspx?VID=9)

**Rakhigarhi, AP02.** Haryana's official inventory places the archaeological site beneath the modern villages Rakhi-Shahpur and Rakhi-Khas. This provides the requested living-village overlay example for the existing Rakhigarhi comparison. The entry is on PDF p.38 under “Ancient Site at Rakhigarhi (ASI).” Its separate claims about total extent, mound count and very early chronology need phase and measurement reconciliation; only the overlay observation is recommended for use. The inventory's publication date was not established. [Haryana Directorate of Archaeology & Museums inventory](https://archaeology.haryana.gov.in/sites/default/files/site-docs/Heritage-Monuments-and-Sites-of-Haryana.pdf)

**Harappa, AP03.** Meadow and Kenoyer's 1997 excavation chapter confirms that removal of rubble for the Lahore–Multan railway contributed to damage, particularly in Period 3C and later deposits. It also documents ancient reuse of baked bricks and construction in mud brick, so missing walls have more than one explanation. Use this as the historical brick-removal example, without supplying an exact 1856 date from recollection. The conclusion is on printed p.170 / PDF p.32. [*Excavations at Harappa 1994–1995: New Perspectives on the Indus Script, Craft Activities, and City Organization*](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1997_Excavations%20at%20Harappa%201994-1995%20New%20Perspective.pdf)

Clay extraction for brick kilns and looting in Balochistan were not independently verified during this bounded follow-up.

## Makran: known settlements and the direction of coastal change

**AP04–AP06.** Uplift and coastal advance deserve emphasis in P21. Page and colleagues' primary abstract reports 1–3 m of earthquake uplift at Pasni and Ormara in 1945. Pasni is within the proposed coastal region; Ormara is farther east. The event demonstrates a local uplift mechanism, while leaving Bronze Age shoreline positions to be established separately. The journal article itself was not acquired; the institutional abstract and bibliographic record were read. [Page et al. 1979, USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70012441)

Normand and colleagues' 2019 study explains how regional uplift, erosion, sediment transport and local normal faults shape Makran terraces. Its field study concerns southeastern Iran and Pleistocene evolution. It supports the process model, while supplying no directly transferable rate or archaeological shoreline age for the Pakistani windows. Relevant passages are the abstract and “Geological Setting” / “Geology at the Coastal Makran.” [*Pleistocene Coastal Evolution in the Makran Subduction Zone*](https://doi.org/10.3389/feart.2019.00186)

Dales's 1962 primary survey narrative explicitly combines continuing uplift, river alluvium and beach deposition to explain the changing positions of Sutkagen-dor and Sotka-koh. He describes Sotka-koh north of Pasni and interprets possible tidal access from a nearer ancient coast, while stating it could not have stood directly on the coast. These are useful known-site anchors and historical hypotheses; their local Bronze Age paleoshoreline was not dated in this account. Read the Sotka-koh discussion and paragraph beginning “Three natural forces.” [*A Search for Ancient Seaports*](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/a-search-for-ancient-seaports/)

The parent has already acquired the official Sutkagen-Dor entry and assigned R48, so it is not duplicated here. The revision can favor raised or inland former surfaces for first assessment of P21, while retaining localized subsidence or inundation as alternatives requiring their own evidence. A coast-wide constant uplift rate would exceed the evidence checked here.

## Northern and foothill examples without new windows

**Manda, AP07.** Jammu's official heritage page describes Akhnoor Fort above an ancient site with Harappan pottery, early historic pottery and Kushana remains, and identifies Manda inside the fort. This is a useful river-footprint and later-construction example. Its undated statement that excavation continues does not verify a 2026 season. Use the descriptive paragraphs about the underlying site, rather than the page's imagined navigation narrative. [District Jammu, *Akhnoor Fort*](https://jammu.nic.in/tourist-place/akhnoor-fort/)

**Ropar, AP08.** The official Rupnagar museum page describes the excavated sequence from Harappan to medieval times and the museum's opening in 1998. It provides a known upper-river settlement and later-period comparison, without proving the present living settlement's archaeological footprint. The first two descriptive paragraphs are the relevant locator. [District Rupnagar, *Archeological Museum*](https://rupnagar.nic.in/tourist-place/archeological-museum/)

**Shortugaï, AP09.** The DAFA institutional account records survey in 1975, tests in 1976 and excavations in 1977–79. Its phases include Mature Harappan and later cultural occupations. The account gives 2.5 ha, 993 m² excavated and nearly 90,000 sherds. These figures illustrate information available from a relatively small excavated area, but lack the volume and recovery data needed for a transferable density. This northern Afghan valley settlement can contextualize long-distance connections; it does not authenticate high-mountain or cave cities. The first three paragraphs and bibliography are the locators. [DAFA, *Shortugaï*](https://www.dafa-afgh.org/recherche/les-operations-de-terrain-entre-1922-et-1982/shortugai/)

**Dholavira, AP12.** UNESCO's official description places the known city on arid Khadir Island. Its integrity section records 13 excavation field seasons between 1989 and 2005, followed by conservation of exposed remains. This adds an island urban comparison and a concrete excavation/conservation example. The inspected entry supplies no dated local Bronze Age shoreline or demonstration of navigable access; such claims require their own evidence. [UNESCO, *Dholavira: a Harappan City*](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1645/)

## Kalibangan containment

**AP10.** J.S. Nigam, an excavation participant, gives Kalibangan as 29° 29′ N, 74° 08′ E on the opening page of “Sothi Pottery at Kalibangan: A Reappraisal,” printed p.7 / PDF p.20. That converts to 29.483333 N, 74.133333 E and lies inside P06, whose west/south/east/north bounds are [74.0, 29.1, 75.2, 29.9]. The acquired scan's opening page was visually inspected. The publisher's contents identify *Puratattva* 26 (1995–96). An older Lal chapter's indexed coordinates differ by several minutes but also fall within P06; the containment result is robust to that discrepancy. This is a published coarse point, not a newly surveyed coordinate. [Nigam article in the ASI library scan](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/82052.pdf), [publisher contents](https://indarchaeology.org/Archives%20Content/Puratattva%20no%2026.pdf)

## Khambhat: original evidence and the association problem

**AP11.** The original NIOT *Episodes* article reports sonar patterns, objects collected using grab and dredge sampling, and gravity/vibro cores containing marine, fluvial and terrestrial facies. Its chronology section reports carbonized wood dated to approximately 9544–9545 cal BP. A fair account must include the cores and distinguish the investigators' habitation interpretation from the observations. The relevant locators are printed pp.16–18, particularly “Geological setting,” the sampling paragraph and “Chronology.” [Kathiroli et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.18814/epiiugs/2003/v26i1/003)

The brief report does not document secure association of that wood with a particular constructed feature and diagnostic occupation assemblage. This is the reviewer's inference from the reported recovery and association evidence. It permits neither dating a city from the wood nor dismissing all submerged archaeological potential. A 2003 geological-journal discussion/reply was located only through a bibliographic index; specific objections cannot be attributed to it without reading the primary text. The record preserves that access gap.
