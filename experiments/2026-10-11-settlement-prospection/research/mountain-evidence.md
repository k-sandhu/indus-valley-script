# Mountain valleys, caves and buried slopes: evidence and survey hypotheses

Prepared 11 October 2026 for the settlement-prospection study. This is a focused source review, with five proposed survey windows. It adds mountain evidence to the parent study; it does not report new field discoveries. Source IDs refer to [mountain-sources.json](mountain-sources.json), which records the actual reading depth and local witness hashes.

## What the evidence supports

The strongest mountain targets are cultivable valley floors, tributary fans and spring-fed foothills connected to known occupations. The existing observations provide much less support for an expectation of undiscovered Indus cities inside caves. Rock shelters can preserve short visits, hearths, animal management and later ritual use, all of which require different sampling from a large settlement mound.

Mehrgarh supplies a direct example of burial beside a mountain river. Jarrige and Lechevallier describe a Neolithic mound whose flanks were progressively covered by Bolan alluvium, with later deposits over those sediments and a river-cut section exposing the buried sequence (1980, printed p.253). The site is an archaeological observation of masking and erosion in the Kachi plain. Its buried Neolithic deposits precede the Indus urban period. A 2025 study models new tooth-enamel dates from 23 Neolithic burials and estimates the cemetery's start at 5223–4914 cal BCE in its preferred model; two samples were excluded to improve model agreement. These dates revise the earlier chronology of Period I rather than dating every fan surface or Bronze Age occupation. The paper discusses recurrent flooding as a possible contributor to rapid accumulation. [M01](https://doi.org/10.3406/paleo.1980.4279), [M02](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-92621-5).

Ghazi Shah links foothill water and Indus occupation. Pakistan's archaeological inventory places the mound beside the Bhit Range and a perennial spring, adjoining the alluvial plain toward Manchar Lake. Its account describes an Amri occupation followed by mixed Amri/Indus and Indus levels. The listed footprint is 160 by 130 m, or 2.08 ha, with an 11 m surviving height; these are inventory dimensions, not a phase-specific settlement boundary. A newer primary report abstract explicitly identifies a **2025 Ghazi Shah excavation** and portable archaeometric analyses. An account ending with Flam's 1980s campaigns therefore misses recent work. The full 2025 report was unavailable in the public metadata inspected here, so its stratigraphy and dates cannot yet be assessed. [M03](https://doam.gov.pk/public/sites/7279), [M04](https://hal.science/hal-05430765v1).

The MAFBI project already surveys and tests the southwestern Kirthar foothills in Sindh-Kohistan, Jamshoro district. Its published program connects that work to Chanhu-daro and questions about the early Indus period and relations with Balochistan. Mountain prospecting is an existing research direction. This study's contribution is a proposed comparison of exposed, buried and eroded surfaces within such landscapes. [M05](https://aibl.fr/fouilles/mission-archeologique-francaise-du-bassin-de-lindus-pakistan/).

For Kech-Makran, Besenval's original account describes low mountain chains, alluvial valleys, irregular storm floods and agricultural soils exposed to erosion. Modern karez irrigation appears in this account as present practice; it supplies no direct date for Bronze Age irrigation. A 2024 archaeobotanical study identifies Miri Qalat's Indus-related Period IV within a longer local sequence. Its case study classifies 12 of 13 preserved Phoenix seeds from Miri Qalat and Shahi Tump as wild forms. Table 2 lists 15 sample IDs and four conventional radiocarbon ages; their relationship to the 13 classified seeds needs reconciliation, and the authors' early-third-millennium BCE assignment needs a calibration check. This selected protohistoric sample cannot establish the whole region's farming regime, but it makes current date groves a poor automatic guide to ancient settlement suitability. The authors also identify possible bias from loss of narrow seed tips. [M06](https://doi.org/10.3406/arasi.1997.1399), [M07](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2024.106052).

Swat provides a useful boundary comparison. Olivieri's deposited thesis associates local agricultural settlements and exchange with a period contemporary with the Late Harappan phase. It discusses Cemetery H-style pottery at Barikot, whose local manufacture or import remains unresolved. Its shelter chronology uses stylistic, superposition and contextual comparisons across a long sequence; it explicitly leaves Swat's direct role in Harappan development unresolved (printed pp.166–167). Such evidence supports tests of contact and local occupation, with a lower expectation of Mature Indus settlement. [M08](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/1497).

## Five proposed survey windows

The windows below are geographic research settings. No candidate parcel, measured boundary, access permission or detection probability has been established. Each needs coordination with the relevant field mission and a join to its unpublished records before selecting new ground. The competing explanations should be fixed before survey results are interpreted.

**1. Bolan outlet–Dhadar–Nausharo, northern Kachi plain.** Test whether abandoned fan distributaries and low interfan surfaces retain occupations masked by younger sediments. The Mehrgarh section establishes that masking occurs here; Nausharo provides a known local-to-Indus settlement comparator in the Bolan plain. [M11](https://doam.gov.pk/public/sites/8927). Compare dated stable terraces with distal fan margins and recently reworked channels. Map buried soils and sediment contacts with cores and exposed sections, then investigate cultural layers only where sedimentary context is retained. The principal rival is a pre-Indus village or sterile fan deposit mistaken for an Indus occupation. A younger surface with no surviving older soil would defeat the local preservation premise. Cultural material redeposited in a channel would support erosion, not an intact settlement beneath the core.

**2. Naing Nai–Bhit Range–Ghazi Shah spring corridor, western Dadu.** Test for small rural or craft occupations at former spring channels and fan-to-plain interfaces beyond the known mound. Water access and the existing Amri/Indus sequence motivate the window. Select paired stable surfaces and sediment-covered margins at comparable walking distance from reconstructed water sources. Record off-mound lithic debris, small ceramic scatters and animal remains as well as mounds. Rival explanations include Amri-period activity, historic irrigation deposits and moved artifacts. Dates outside the target phase, or a demonstration that the watercourse postdates it, would reject an Indus-period association. The 2025 excavation makes collaboration and de-duplication especially necessary.

**3. Southwestern Kirthar foothills, Jamshoro district, including Kai–Naig as a verification subarea.** Compare existing recorded occupations with valley mouths, low slope benches and colluvial pockets that lack visible mounds. This region already belongs to the MAFBI survey program. The narrow additional hypothesis is that some slope-adjacent activity survives below sediment accumulated after occupation. At Kai, reconcile the cave names and individual cavities first: the 2015 government inventory describes a natural cave without architectural features, whereas popular accounts assign divergent ages and origins. A dated hearth or floor would provide a usable occupation claim. Geological cavities, recent smoke, historic structures or undated pottery on nearby slopes would leave the prehistoric cave hypothesis unresolved. The inventory passage alone supplies no authenticated Indus occupation date. [M09](https://rio-heritage.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/volume-i-inventory-of-cultural-property-in-the-province-of-sindh.pdf).

**4. Kech Valley around Turbat, Miri Qalat and Shahi Tump, extending into tributary-fan margins.** Test whether low rural occupations are obscured where tributaries spread sediment onto cultivable valley soils. Avoid making modern palm density the main predictor. Compare palaeochannel proximity, dated soils, slope runoff and surviving field surfaces, with settlement predictions separated by cultural phase. Geophysical anomalies should be tested against natural gravel lenses, historic irrigation and Islamic-period structures. An ancient object in storm sediment could have travelled from a known mound. Intact floors, food residues and independently dated short-lived material beneath the masking layer would discriminate buried occupation from transported debris. The locations of known settlements provide background evidence, not an estimate of the size of any missing one.

**5. Kotah–Kandak valleys and the Barikot terrace interface, Swat–Malakand.** Use this as a low-confidence test of small upland occupations, exchange and shelter use during the later Bronze Age. Treat painted shelters and terrace settlements as separate observation classes. Survey unpainted shelters as controls, because a painting-led sample selects ritual and visibility attributes. Map slope wash and debris fans before choosing subsurface tests. Local Bronze Age communities, later herders, Buddhist use and historic visitors are plausible rivals. Secure target-period occupation beneath a dated sediment layer would strengthen the contact hypothesis; later deposits alone would resolve that tested shelter's chronology without establishing a regional absence. A surface stylistic resemblance would not discriminate import, imitation and mixed deposits.

## Testing landslide and cave burial

This search did not locate a primary demonstration of an Indus occupation sealed beneath a dated landslide in these five windows. Landslide burial remains a mechanism to investigate after local geomorphological mapping. A debris lobe can bury a former bench, scour it away, mix material from uphill, or cover ground that was never occupied. Those outcomes produce different observations. A test should reconstruct the pre-slide surface, date the deposit and any underlying soil, assess sediment fabric and artifact abrasion, and seek coherent occupation features. The likelihood of occupation belongs to the former land surface; the likelihood of preservation belongs to the subsequent event. Distance to a large settlement does not answer either question by itself.

Caves need the same separation of site formation from cultural attribution. Kakar's original survey records occupation debris, smoke-darkened roofs and nearby lithics in northern Balochistan caves, alongside painted shelters. The paper lacks absolute dates and bases its proposed Palaeolithic attribution largely on lithic morphology and depicted fauna. It is useful evidence that mountain cavities contain material worth investigating; its animal pictures cannot date a Bronze Age occupation. [M10](https://doi.org/10.34780/mvc3-3u6b).

The proposed cave protocol would distinguish hearth charcoal from dispersed charcoal, screen for burrowing and slope-derived debris, quantify the sediment examined, and date short-lived samples associated with coherent features. Micromorphology could test whether dung or ash is in place. Negative results should report which deposits were reachable and sampled, since a blocked entrance or sterile upper layer does not inspect the entire cavity. The information expected from this work is a dated occupation history and activities at small sites, rather than a forecast of large artifact counts.

## Source and access record

The search covered primary archaeological publications, mission records, official inventories and geological leads, checked through 11 October 2026. It was focused rather than exhaustive. Repository memory was consulted, including the October 8 excavation retrospective and the registry's warning that the inscription census is a selected object-source cohort. No denominator from that census was used here.

Downloaded PDFs and extracted text remain local under `data/raw/2026-10-11-settlement-prospection/` with `MOUNTAIN_` names. The source register carries canonical URLs instead of transient session URLs. Publisher DOI access failed for Mutin 2025, while the university repository PDF succeeded. HAL article pages returned an Anubis access denial; its public API supplied metadata and the Ghazi Shah report abstract. No inaccessible report was represented as fully read. The Kai inventory was available only as an indexed primary passage at printed p.383. These access differences limit the claims above.

Chronological problems discovered during the search changed the proposed evidence frame: the early Mehrgarh chronology has a 2025 revision; the Kech seed study requires sample and calibration reconciliation; and Ghazi Shah has a 2025 excavation record. No numerical discovery model, field survey or remote imagery classification was run in this subtask.
