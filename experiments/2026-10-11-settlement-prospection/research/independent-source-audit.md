# Independent source audit before manuscript review

Date: 11 October 2026. Reviewer: the Codex research subagent assigned to challenge four central evidence claims. This check is separate from manuscript drafting. No complete manuscript was supplied for this pass, so the findings below concern sources and proposed inferences. A later frozen-draft review will be recorded separately.

I read the repository instructions, existing excavation outlook, experiment protocol, writing rules and redistribution record. I then inspected the acquired primary XML/PDF witnesses and checked publisher material for the excavation counts and coastal claims. Downloaded witnesses remain local. The numerical checks here are arithmetic audits of published counts, rather than new field results.

## 1. The headline excavation fraction has conflicting historical denominators

The University of Lucknow teaching chapter does reproduce 1,022 Mature Harappan sites and approximately 96 excavated, attributing its summary to 2002 sources. Its national subtotals are 406 plus 616 sites and 41 plus 55 excavated. The indexed source passage is on printed p. 41. That attribution should remain attached to the numbers because the chapter's own publication date and reconciliation of the underlying inventories were not established. [Teaching chapter](https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202003241550006972anil_kumar_HARAPPAN_CIVILIZATION%201.pdf).

A primary discrepancy is recoverable from Possehl's own chapter, *Harappans and hunters: economic interaction and specialization in prehistoric India*. Its publisher Introduction reports 1,056 Mature Harappan sites and 96 excavated, citing Possehl 1999, Appendix A. The chapter appeared in the 2002 book *Forager-Traders in South and Southeast Asia*, pp. 62–76; its online publication in 2009 does not update the archaeological count. The publisher extract was read, rather than the entire chapter. [Possehl chapter, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511489631.005](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/foragertraders-in-south-and-southeast-asia/harappans-and-hunters-economic-interaction-and-specialization-in-prehistoric-india/F6870A6F5DBA69082DDF1DC1FEDA3FDB).

The two historical ratios are 9.39% and 9.09%. Their small numerical difference does not resolve why the inventories differ. Gadekar's newer review reports 142 excavated sites across the Early and Mature periods, using Uesugi's compilation with excavations through 2024 added. Its count passage is printed p. 156. The final issue is *Lithic Technology* 51(2), 2026, pp. 153–170; publication online was 18 July 2025. Publisher-indexed passages and metadata were checked, but a complete local PDF was not acquired in this audit. [Gadekar review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01977261.2025.2523610).

**Material correction:** retain the historical counts as attributed observations, explicitly record the 1,056 alternative, and avoid calculating a present excavation percentage by combining 142 with either old denominator. The inventories need a site-level concordance, phase rules, and a common cutoff date before a change in the Mature-only excavation rate can be calculated. A site counted as excavated may contain only a small investigated area, so these ratios also cannot measure the fraction of surviving deposits excavated.

## 2. The Cholistan results establish candidate detection, with selected validation

Orengo and colleagues' *Automated detection of archaeological mounds using machine-learning classification of multisensor and multitemporal satellite data* reports 337 proposed mound clusters after applying a threshold above 0.55. Of these, 71 could be linked with reasonable certainty to legacy sites, including five training mounds and twenty validation mounds. The unmatched remainder is arithmetically 266. The Results section and Discussion, “Villages and Towns: Mound Size Estimates,” report 246 clusters below five hectares, or 72.997% of 337. [Primary article, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2005583117](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7414161/).

The Methods, “Machine-Learning Algorithm,” specify that the 25 reference sites were large, well preserved and readily located in high-resolution imagery. The twenty test mounds were identified in the first classifier iteration, followed by two refinements; the threshold was chosen after visual inspection. Successful detection of this selected set cannot estimate recall for buried deposits, small scatters or all archaeological sites. The paper itself identifies modern seasonal settlements, excavated water ponds and natural clay accumulations as sensor-level rivals. Its combined sensor discrimination is useful evidence, but field confirmation and dating remain necessary for the unmatched candidates.

**Material correction:** label 266 as unmatched remote candidates, and retain “tentative visible mound extent” for the size distribution. The classifier score is not a calibrated probability of an Indus settlement. Legacy coordinates are imprecise, so unmatched candidates may include rediscoveries.

Ganweriwala's area estimates describe different observations. Gulzar reports December 2015 total-station work, with a boundary based on the visible cultural-material spread, and obtains about 66.7 hectares (printed pp. 37–38, PDF pp. 3–4). Her discussion records the earlier 81.5-hectare estimate and its uncertain measurement procedure. Orengo estimates approximately 33 hectares of visible mounded area. The latter paper also cites an approximately 80-hectare legacy estimate. These values neither form a confidence interval nor demonstrate a measured loss of half the original town. Surface scatter, surviving mound, burial, erosion, historical coordinate error and changing definitions require separate tests. [Gulzar, *Harappan Cities: Landscape and Surface Survey at Ganweriwala*](https://easaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Sidra-Gulzar-3.pdf).

## 3. Historical-map checking has a changing sampling design

Green and colleagues' *Re-Discovering Ancient Landscapes* confirms 779 checked historical-map features, 199 associated with extant archaeological sites, and 64 additional sites without apparent mapped-feature associations (PDF pp. 7–14). The 199/779 ratio is 25.55%. The 169 positives not linked to earlier reports may include earlier visits whose coordinates were inadequate. The sites span multiple periods, and the comparison's protohistoric category also includes Painted Grey Ware. [Primary article, DOI 10.3390/rs11182089](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/11/18/2089).

PDF p. 10 discloses an especially consequential selection rule: size-one checking initially stopped after ten negative locations per regional grid unit. In 2018, apparent ponds and dunes were excluded before visits, and the remaining selected candidates were checked. PDF p. 11 defines a site by at least two artifacts or features of verifiable antiquity within five metres; it discusses crop visibility and displaced worn pottery. The Table 1 rates therefore describe a changing checked sample, rather than unbiased probabilities for every mapped feature. The wider survey used prior locations, imagery and informants alongside map features.

**Material correction:** use Table 1 as descriptive evidence of symbol and size differences. Do not transfer its 25.55% or category rates into a national Indus forecast. The 64 off-map sites show a failure of a map-only search to include all encountered evidence, but they cannot quantify regional false-negative rate without a complete and consistently sampled denominator. Preserve a distinction between a negative surface visit, a destroyed feature, a buried feature and an inaccessible location in the proposed survey design.

## 4. Later cities and coastlines need process-specific predictions

The Government of Sindh's Lakhanjo-Daro account provides a concrete urban example: recent construction disturbed the upper deposits in four 2017 trenches, while some lower occupation deposits and craft evidence survived. Deep modern pits also mixed ancient material with garbage. The account supports monitoring redevelopment and existing archaeological exposures around Sukkur; it does not establish that every later city conceals an Indus settlement or provide an audited site-wide area. [Government site account, 3 January 2022](https://antiquities.sindhculture.gov.pk/tour/lakhian-jo-daro-site-sukkur/).

Garcia-Molsosa and colleagues distinguish older alluvium beneath Bronze Age deposits from younger channel fills that cut older deposits (PDF p. 23). This creates two rival outcomes: preservation beneath aggradation, and removal by erosion. In the Multan landscape, several relict river and canal associations are Early Historic or Medieval (PDF pp. 21–22). A visible channel beside a candidate mound needs its own date. [Primary article, DOI 10.1186/s40494-023-00985-6](https://heritagesciencejournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40494-023-00985-6).

Das and colleagues infer a local relative sea-level fall beginning around 4150 ± 230 BP from a trench four kilometres downstream of Lothal. Their Methods apply a marine reservoir correction, and the Discussion calls the reconstructed water level approximate because tidal information is limited. Estuarine infilling and changes in river connection are relevant competing processes when translating environmental proxies into a shoreline history. The result supports looking for former coastlines now inland as well as submerged surfaces. [Das et al. 2022, sections 2, 3 and 5](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021GC010264).

The 2026 Gulf of Khambhat synthesis reinforces the need for localized reconstruction: its indexed abstract distinguishes the two locations meeting full sea-level-index-point criteria from limiting or tectonically affected indicators. I read the publisher-indexed abstract and introduction, not the full article. [Vedpathak et al. 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.quaint.2026.110163](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1040618226000492).

**Material correction:** require dated terrestrial surfaces, occupation evidence in context and reconstructed local relative sea level before assigning offshore anomalies to the Indus period. A rectangular acoustic pattern or an isolated dated object would not resolve natural morphology, displaced objects or a different cultural period. The choice of a mountain, cave or landslide window likewise needs an independently supported occupation or exchange catchment, sediment chronology and a test of local cultural alternatives; topography alone supplies little evidence of an Indus settlement.

## Specific source hazard and remaining questions

The acquired Srivastava et al. 2023 review, *Extremely fast Holocene coastal landscape evolution in the Kachchh Upland*, is useful for tectonic and depositional mechanisms. PDF p. 5 nevertheless calls a sea-level fall around 3000 BP contemporary with Harappan centres dated approximately 2800–1800 BCE. Those time ranges do not coincide. Reuse the process arguments with independently checked chronology, and trace individual shoreline ages to their original dated observations. [Review, DOI 10.1080/17445647.2023.2167617](https://doi.org/10.1080/17445647.2023.2167617).

The unresolved questions that can materially change the manuscript are the concordance behind the historical site totals; the number and dates of field-confirmed Cholistan remote candidates since 2020; the contemporary survival and access of proposed urban parcels; and date/elevation control for each coastal or slope-burial window. Current research activity and a public database's existence should not be described as completion of those checks. The acquired GIOSAN2012_HTML witness is a CAPTCHA page and supplies no usable article evidence; the paper must be cited from a valid reading route if used.

No site-discovery claim, national count correction, measured excavation yield or settlement-area model was produced in this pass. The findings are intended to constrain the forthcoming manuscript and its separate adversarial review.
