# What remains to be found? Excavation limits, hidden landscapes and a testable survey agenda for the Indus Civilization

Complete working manuscript prepared 11 October 2026. Structured narrative review and exploratory survey design. Research and writing used AI assistance. Author names, affiliations and a submission venue have not been assigned. Source observations, calculations, reviews and revisions are preserved in the project. No fieldwork or new satellite-image analysis was conducted for this study.

# # Abstract

The large difference between recorded Indus sites and excavated sites reflects selective archaeological investigation, the cost of preserving exposed remains, access constraints, incomplete publication and a landscape record altered by rivers, agriculture and later construction. The familiar estimate of about 100 excavated sites among more than 1000 recorded locations is a historical summary with inconsistent denominators. A recent review counts 142 excavated Early and Mature Harappan sites through 2024, but a compatible present-day denominator remains unresolved. We examine primary archaeological and environmental studies and recompute published aggregate counts. In Cholistan,337 remotely proposed mound clusters include 71 legacy matches;266 unmatched candidates await secure identity and chronology. A northwest Indian historical-map survey found archaeological evidence at 199 of 779 checked features and encountered 64 further locations without mapped-feature associations. Its changing selection rules prevent using those rates as population probabilities. We assemble 21 coarse survey windows with supporting evidence, competing explanations and falsification tests. They cover leveled rural sites, deposits beneath later cities and alluvium, mountain and piedmont settings, caves and colluvium, former shorelines now inland, and potentially submerged surfaces. Local landform chronology controls these proposals. Constructed excavation-volume examples demonstrate sensitivity of artifact recovery to deposit density and sampling, while providing no civilization-wide forecast. The research agenda combines archival concordance, stratified survey, geomorphology, limited subsurface investigation and improved recovery of environmental and craft evidence. Its deliverables are hypotheses and reproducible audits that can guide field collaboration.

## 1. The question behind the excavation gap

The remaining archaeological potential concerns both places and information. An unexcavated mound may retain a household sequence, a workshop, a burial ground or an environmental record. A heavily excavated city may still have poorly sampled peripheral neighborhoods. A settlement covered by sediment may leave no visible mound. A collection already in a museum may contain plant, residue or manufacturing evidence that its initial study did not recover. These are different opportunities and require different investigations.

For readers asking how much of the Indus Civilization has been investigated, three quantities matter: the number of recorded locations, the number with any excavation, and the area or volume actually investigated within them. A site can enter an excavation list after a small test trench. Its appearance in that list does little to describe how much remains below ground. A mound is a physical observation; cultural attribution and period boundaries require evidence. Several mounds can form one site, and one mound can contain several periods.

We use Early, Mature and Late Harappan as archaeological period labels whose regional definitions vary. The familiar broad Mature interval of about 2600-1900 BCE is a working reference for comparison, rather than a date assigned automatically to a feature. A protohistoric inventory can include Painted Grey Ware or other periods beyond this interval. Local Bronze Age communities in Balochistan and adjacent mountain regions must retain their own cultural classifications. Exchange with an Indus city establishes contact more readily than it establishes the cultural identity of an entire settlement.

This study asks why excavation has remained selective, what can be inferred about the size of incompletely investigated places, which landscape changes could conceal further evidence, and what additional recovery would contribute. It develops geographic tests from published research and interdisciplinary reasoning. The rectangles in the accompanying map are sampling frames drawn for this study. Their archaeological content has not been measured.

## 3. Why excavation remains selective

### 3.1 The headline counts do not share a present denominator

A University of Lucknow teaching chapter reports 1022 Mature Harappan sites and approximately 96 excavated, citing 2002 literature. Possehl's own 2002 chapter gives 1056 Mature sites and 96 excavated, referring to his 1999 appendix. These historical ratios are 9.39% and 9.09%. Their discrepancy remains unresolved because we have not joined the underlying inventories location by location. The book chapter's 2009 online date does not update the archaeological count. Gadekar's review, published online in 2025 and assigned to a 2026 issue, reports 142 excavated Early and Mature sites through 2024. Its period scope differs from the old Mature-only totals. A present fraction requires a common site definition, phase rule, cutoff date and concordance. [R01,R02,R38]

|Observation|Recorded denominator|Excavated numerator|What the number measures|
|---|---:|---:|---|
|Teaching chapter citing 2002 compilations|1022 Mature locations|96|Attributed historical summary|
|Possehl 2002 publisher extract|1056 Mature locations|96|Alternative primary historical summary|
|Gadekar review through 2024|Compatible denominator unavailable|142 Early+Mature sites|Review population across two periods|

The numerical difference between 96 and 142 cannot establish how many Mature-only sites were newly excavated. Nor can a site-count percentage measure excavated hectares. Excavation intensity is poorly represented by a yes/no field: a dated test pit, several seasons and large exposed neighborhoods all count as activity but produce different information.

### 3.2 Excavation creates long-term obligations

Excavation removes deposits to reveal their relationships. Once an intact stratigraphic sequence has been dug, its interpretation depends on records, samples and retained materials. Exposed mudbrick and baked brick also require protection. The 1990 ICOMOS charter advocates survey, limited excavation, retained undisturbed portions and maintenance after exposure. These are professional conservation principles; actual permitting and implementation differ by jurisdiction. [R03]

Moenjodaro's 2026 UNESCO conservation record provides a current concrete example. Salts, capillary groundwater and monsoon exposure affect vulnerable brickwork; the 2022 floods and limited conservation resources form part of the management problem. Funding a new trench therefore implies obligations extending beyond a field season. The evidence supports conservation as a real constraint, although it supplies no common cost-per-hectare estimate for every Indus site. [R04]

Research design also limits excavation deliberately. A team may obtain more useful chronological or economic evidence by sampling contrasting small settlements and obtaining environmental samples than by exposing another large stretch of familiar architecture. Documentation, specialist analysis, storage and publication can consume years after digging ends. The excavation gap consequently includes choices about what to ask and how to preserve information, as well as constraints on what can be funded.

### 3.3 Access, destruction and publication

The 2007 RIHN report says planned Ganweriwala excavation was postponed because of political turmoil. Gulzar'sDecember 2015 surface survey describes desert and border-access constraints. These are dated examples, not a permanent account of the site's status: a 2024 University of Lahore notice documents participation in excavation. Current projects cannot be classified from an older “unexcavated” label alone. [R05,R06,R07]

Agricultural leveling, industrial construction, quarrying and sediment movement can change what is accessible even when a location is known. At Lakhian jo Daro in Sukkur, modern disturbance removed or mixed upper deposits while lower craft contexts survived in some excavation areas. A survey's inability to see pottery on the present surface cannot distinguish destroyed from buried archaeology. Published lists also lag behind field activity and may omit specialist categories, as Gadekar's lithic review illustrates. [R21,R22,R02]

The sources do not support one universal explanation such as lack of interest, a prohibition on excavation or a civilization-wide shortage of permission. They support several locally documented constraints. A budget for future work should include the archive, conservation and analytical stages, alongside discovery and digging.

## 4. How large could the incompletely investigated places be?

### 4.1 Visible area, cultural spread and occupation footprint

Ganweriwala illustrates the measurement problem. Gulzar's 2015 ground survey estimates 66.7 ha using cultural-material spread and total-station mapping. An older estimate is 81.5 ha, with uncertain measurement procedure. Orengo and colleagues estimate about 33 ha of remotely visible mounded area. The three values describe different definitions and evidence. They do not form a statistical interval for the city's original area, and they do not demonstrate that half the city was destroyed between surveys. Peripheral scatters, dune burial, erosion, redeposition and phase differences can all affect the comparison. [R07,R08]

The physical scale of these numbers can be made concrete without assuming a shape. A square with an area of 1 ha has a 100 m side;5 ha corresponds to 224 m;30 ha to 548 m;66.7 ha to 817 m; and 200 ha to 1.41 km. These are square-equivalent dimensions. An actual settlement may be long, irregular or split across separate mounds. Its inhabited area at one date may be smaller than the combined cultural spread accumulated across centuries.

At Kotada in Lodrani, a reported 100×60 m fortified element corresponds to 0.6 ha. It is a structure boundary, not a measured total occupation area. That site's 2024 Morodharo name duplicated an earlier documented locality. Small shifts in naming, period assignment or measured feature type can otherwise produce apparent new discoveries or inconsistent size rankings. [R19]

### 4.2 What the remote mound sample suggests

The 2020 Cholistan study reports 337 proposed clusters,246 under 5 ha and six above 20 ha. The smaller category accounts for 73.0% of proposed visible mounds. Two notable unmatched proposals were approximately 29 ha at Khundowala and 20.5 ha at Mulhiawala. Their visible size warrants follow-up; secure cultural and chronological attribution requires field evidence. A remote size distribution cannot be treated as a distribution of all surviving Indus settlements. Small scatters, deeply buried places and some dune-covered remains have different detection chances. [R08]

Large mounds tend to provide prominent surface and sensor signals. A new very large settlement concealed beneath alluvium or later urban fabric remains physically possible, but its probability cannot be estimated from this sample. The evidence justifies expecting a substantial role for small places among additional detectable candidates. It provides no supported list of unseen cities as large as the best-known urban centers.

### 4.3 Geography helps locate tests more than it predicts size

Proximity to a large city can motivate a search for villages, farms, workshops and exchange routes. It does not fix their size. A nearby settlement could serve a specialized task, predate the city, continue after it or belong to another network. Intensive survey around a city also increases discovery effort, making its surroundings appear richer even if the underlying settlement density is comparable elsewhere.

The 2009 Land, Water and Settlement season illustrates both settlement richness and naming ambiguity. A legacy Masudpur entry represented four mounds with one coordinate; renewed mapping recorded 13 mounds. The wider Rakhigarhi survey recorded 127 all-period locations with overlapping phase memberships. Those phase counts cannot be summed as separate sites. This evidence favors phase-specific rural survey and better concordance before a city-centered area model is fitted. [R29]

A future extent model should predict a distribution of measurable boundaries using independently dated occupation, landform age, access to water and resources, and survey effort. It should be evaluated on withheld regions and checked against small and buried sites. The present study fits no such model. Available regional evidence supplies hypotheses about where to measure, rather than authenticated dimensions for invisible sites.

## 5. Processes that can hide or remove evidence

### 5.1 Later cities and repeated building

Later cities offer two opposing preservation pathways. Deep foundations, utilities, quarry pits and leveling may remove deposits. Shallower building and accumulated urban fill may cover earlier layers while protecting portions beneath them. The surviving evidence can vary across adjacent parcels. Lakhian jo Daro demonstrates this mixture and supports an urban archaeological sampling strategy based on construction history and stratified exposures. [R21]

The test is strongest where earlier pottery, historic mound descriptions or dated excavation already establishes a local Indus presence. In Sukkur-Rohri, archived trench records and former mound extents could guide permitted monitoring of redevelopment, with modern dump deposits and deep disturbed foundations as comparison contexts. For later cities lacking any such evidence, geographic convenience alone yields a weaker hypothesis. Continuity of water access or a route must be demonstrated for the Bronze Age, not inferred from the modern city's success.

An archive-first approach can preserve useful information without opening new excavations. It would reconcile excavation loci with parcel history, separate intact deposits from redeposited finds, and register the depth and date of archaeological surfaces in existing sections. Any future construction monitoring should be planned with the relevant authorities and local specialists. This paper has not inspected contemporary building parcels.

### 5.2 Alluvial burial and flooding

Flooding can cover an occupation surface with sediment, and a migrating river can cut it away. These processes produce similar surface invisibility while leaving different subsurface possibilities. Garcia-Molsosa and colleagues describe older alluvium and paleosols associated with Bronze Age occupation, alongside younger fills that cut earlier surfaces in the southern Punjab landscape. Their spatial comparisons support testing older bar/interfluve margins against younger channel belts around Harappa, Khanewal, Old Beas and Ganji Bar. [R11]

The chronology of a river feature is essential. Singh and colleagues date the major Sutlej abandonment of the studied Ghaggar-Hakra course to shortly after 8 ka, well before urban Indus occupation. The 2021 foothill-flood study documents a different phenomenon, episodic larger flows around 3.9-3.8 ka. A landscape can lose a major Himalayan river and still experience later seasonal flows or floods. These observations motivate flank-to-channel coring and phase-specific occupation mapping; a general “dry river” label loses the temporal detail needed for prospecting. [R15,R16]

Sediment cores should establish whether the target-age ground surface survives, its depth and the deposits above it. Optically stimulated luminescence can date suitable sediment deposition; radiocarbon can date appropriate organic material. Their ages concern sampled material and require stratigraphic interpretation. An old charcoal fragment redeposited by a flood does not date settlement construction. The most informative result could be an intact occupation horizon beneath a dated cover, or evidence that channel scour removed the relevant surface.

### 5.3 Leveled mounds and dune cover

Historical maps can retain evidence of a mound before leveling. In Green and colleagues' northwest Indian survey, symbol types and sizes differed in how often archaeological material was encountered at checked locations. Form-line features yielded 150 positives among 393 checks, shaded features 21 of 154, hachures 26 of 227 and combined symbols 2 of 5. The overall 199 of 779 is 25.55%. These rates describe a changing selected sample: checking of small features initially stopped after negative visits, and later imagery excluded apparent ponds and dunes before field visits. They cannot be used as unbiased probabilities for every old map feature. [R10]

Map-only searching also omits evidence. The same survey encountered 64 additional locations without apparent mapped-feature associations. This supports a comparison stratum away from mapped mounds. It does not supply a complete regional denominator for false-negative rate. The published priorities around Tohana-Agroha, Khanauri-Nabha and Maur-Bathinda-Phul should be treated as follow-up to existing research. [R10]

In Cholistan, the classifier's five training and twenty selected validation mounds were large and well preserved. Its threshold followed visual inspection and its iterative refinement used observed performance. The reported success on those locations does not establish sensitivity to buried sites or low-relief camps. Water ponds, modern seasonal settlements and natural clay accumulations are relevant sensor-level rivals. Field comparison of candidate and noncandidate surfaces must therefore accompany dating. [R08]

### 5.4 Mountains, caves, fans and landslides

Mountain research should begin with habitable surfaces and known connections: valley floors, foothill terraces, spring approaches and routes linking dated occupations. In Kech-Makran, Miri Qalat contains an Indus-period settlement within a much longer local sequence. The Kirthar piedmont already has an active archaeological research history, including mission survey and test excavation around Sindh Kohistan and Jamshoro. These anchors support additional comparisons of stable terrace margins, fan-covered surfaces and ephemeral activity areas. They also make duplication checks necessary before calling a feature overlooked. [R26,R37]

Mehrgarh provides a direct regional burial analogue: Jarrige and Lechevallier describe Neolithic mound flanks covered by Bolan alluvium and exposed by a river-cut section. This is pre-Indus evidence of the process. A 2025 tooth-enamel study revised the chronology of the sampled PeriodIcemetery, estimating its start at 5223-4914 calBCE in a preferred model that excluded two dates. It does not date the Bronze Age surfaces sought here. The known Nausharo sequence supplies a local-to-Indus comparator in the Bolan plain. [R39,R40,R45]

Ghazi Shah's government inventory records Amri and Indus levels beside a perennial spring and the Bhit Range. A primary report abstract explicitly identifies a 2025 excavation there. Its full report was unavailable, so recent activity can be confirmed without assigning new stratigraphy or dates. At Kai, the 2015 government inventory describes a natural cave without architecture or an authenticated Indus date. Kakar's original northern Balochistan shelter survey also lacks absolute dates. These observations favor valley and piedmont tests while leaving cave attribution open. [R41,R42,R43,R44]

A fan may aggrade across an occupied terrace, leaving older ground beneath younger sediment. Colluvial deposits can bury a slope-edge activity area. A landslide can cover an intact surface, deform it, or carry artifacts downslope away from their original context. The occurrence of a landslide therefore supplies a preservation question, without supplying an occupation date. These mechanisms remain proposed tests in the Kech and Bolan-Kachi windows; no buried Indus settlement beneath a newly identified landslide was established here.

Caves and rock shelters have a different expected archaeological scale. Small deposits can preserve visits, fuel, food remains, storage, pastoral use or a burial. Evidence of paintings alone would leave habitation and chronology unresolved. A useful search would compare shelters near independently supported contemporary routes and water sources with accessible shelters away from those routes, record sediment depth and disturbance, and seek intact dated occupation. It must test pre-Indus, local Bronze Age and later occupation as explicit alternatives. Expecting an urban settlement within a cave would require architectural and environmental evidence beyond topography.

Slope morphology, karst geology and least-cost route models could help define future strata. Modern paths and springs need historical qualification, and a transport model should account for seasonal water, terrain and the dated resource network. No route-cost surface, cave inventory or landslide chronology was computed in this study. The strongest near-term mountain opportunity is testing buried or lightly represented activity within known archaeological catchments, while caves remain an exploratory component.

### 5.5 Former coasts, inland paleoshorelines and submerged surfaces

“Now underwater” depends on relative sea level at the location. Ocean level, crustal movement, sediment compaction, delta advance, estuarine infill and erosion all change the relation between land and water. A global sea-level curve cannot by itself identify where an Indus-period ground surface lies today. Early Holocene landscapes submerged before the Indus periods address a different archaeological question.

The 2026 Khambhat synthesis distinguishes formal sea-level index points from limiting and tectonically affected indicators. Its abstract reports that only Lothal and Vadgam meet the full index-point criteria in the reviewed set. Das and colleagues'2022 Lothal-area study interprets a local fall beginning 4150 ± 230 BP and continuing to 3625 ± 200 BP, based on proxies in a trench downstream of the site. Tidal limitations affect the reconstructed elevation, and sediment infill can also reduce navigability. These observations support investigation of former shorelines now inland as well as inundated surfaces. [R32,R34]

Kutch research similarly describes former shallow marine areas that became inland salt flats through interacting tectonic and depositional changes. One chronology statement in the 2023 review equates about 3000 BP with Harappan centers dated 2800-1800 BCE; those intervals do not coincide. We use the geomorphic mechanisms while requiring independently checked ages for proposed archaeological surfaces. [R33]

Biagi's 2024 coastal study identifies shell middens and other low-relief evidence in Las Bela and Lower Sindh, including occupation around former lagoons and stone-built Sonari. Its sequences include different periods and explicitly uncalibratedBP dates. These are evidence for a changing coastal archaeological record; marine-shell dates require reservoir and calibration assessment before comparison with calendar-BCE Indus phases. The study also records industrial loss. [R31]

For offshore prospecting, the first target is a preserved, dated terrestrial surface in a plausible contemporary coastal catchment. Bathymetry and sub-bottom profiling could locate buried landscape units; cores would test sediment environment and age. Side-scan sonar and diver inspection would follow sufficiently supported targets. Rectangular rock patterns, isolated objects and a single old radiocarbon result remain compatible with natural geometry, transport or a different period. Submerged settlement boundaries require associated in-situ occupation evidence. The Makran coastal strip and Khambhat estuary windows are therefore ClassC chronology-first proposals.

## 7. How much additional material and knowledge could excavation recover?

### 7.1 Artifact quantity needs excavated volume and context

Site area alone does not predict finds. Let A be the site's area in hectares, f the fraction opened, h its mean excavated archaeological thickness in metres, d the recoverable fragment density per cubic metre, and r the retained recovery fraction. An illustrative volume isV=10000 Afh, and a count isY = V d r. Thickness includes only the archaeological deposit represented by the density assumption; sterile cover has a different recovery process. Fragment count is also affected by how breakage, sorting and joining are defined.

For a hypothetical 30 ha site with 1% opened through 1 m of relevant deposits,V=3000 m³. At a synthetic recovery fraction of 0.8, assumed densities of 5, 50 and 500 fragments/m³give 12000, 120000 and 1200000 fragments. None of those density values was measured from an Indus site in this study. The arithmetic demonstrates how a 100-fold density difference creates a 100-fold result. It supplies no calibrated estimate for any proposed window.

Harappa offers an empirical scale observation: Dales and Kenoyer's report describes almost one million potsherds over five seasons. It also records variable inscribed-object recovery by season and context. We do not divide those sherds by an invented excavated volume or transfer their density to villages, caves or submerged sites. Seal and inscription recovery cannot be estimated reliably from a generic pottery density or the number of settlements left to investigate. [R30]

### 7.2 Often the additional information is microscopic or relational

An additional pottery assemblage can clarify phase, manufacture, use and movement even when no spectacular object appears. Plant remains, crop weeds, animal bones, shells, manufacturing debris and residues can reveal activities that architecture alone leaves uncertain. Village ceramic research and Rohri/Ongar extraction studies demonstrate why a settlement-only search and an inscription-only measure of value would omit economic evidence. [R22,R23]

Bates and colleagues show information recovered through flotation and analysis of small fractions at northern Indus settlements. Their rice and weed observations illustrate a recoverable archive affected by collection procedures. A 2024 study at Kotada Bhadli recovered lipids from 11 vessel sherds but could not confidently connect the extracted proteins with specific food ingredients. Recent biomolecular work therefore justifies preserved samples, contamination controls and reporting failures alongside successes. It does not guarantee that each technique will work on every collection. [R24,R36]

The practical gain may come from relationships: whether a cemetery and settlement overlap in time, whether quarry activity supplied a particular workshop, whether a paleosol was inhabited before a flood, or whether a village retained occupation during urban contraction. More independent, well-contextualized observations could improve understanding of inscriptions' uses and distributions. They do not authenticate a proposed decipherment by themselves.

A 2024 date-seed study from Kech illustrates the need to audit new analytical cohorts. It classifies a selected 13 seed sample, while Table 2 lists 15 sample IDs and four conventional radiocarbon ages. The relation between those cohorts and a precise calendar phase requires reconciliation. Its reported morphometric result and preservation bias motivate testing modern-date-grove analogies; they cannot estimate the region's ancient crop composition. [R47]

## 8. Turning absence into a testable observation

### 8.1 The observation process

A map of recorded sites combines past occupation with preservation and investigation. For a particular period and region, a conceptual chain is: occupation existed; relevant remains survived; the location received survey coverage; a method detected evidence; material supported a period assignment; and the observation entered an accessible record. The joint probability can be factored into conditional probabilities along that chain. The factorization does not assume the stages are independent.

For a simplified synthetic population with a common effective recording probabilityq, the expected recorded count isN q. A world with 200 sites andq=0.5 and a world with 1000 sites andq=0.1 both give 100 expected records. The same count is compatible with different underlying settlement numbers. Repeating surface survey with independent per-visit detectionp would give 1-(1-p)^k after k visits, but deeply buried evidence can havepnear zero under that method. Changing technique or reaching the relevant surface is then more useful than accumulating similar negative visits.

These illustrations explain why we provide no upper bound on undiscovered sites. A lower bound on detection or preservation, or a validated sampling design, would be needed to obtain one. Actual detectability varies with size, crops, season, sediment depth, image resolution and period. A spatial model should represent occupation and observation separately, using survey effort and dated preservation strata rather than unvisited parcels as reliable negative examples.

### 8.2 A staged design with rejection rules

The first stage should reconcile identities and periods across published gazetteers, MAHSA, excavation reports and historical maps. Alias records retain the original witness and uncertainty. Multiple mounds and multiple phase memberships remain separate fields. New map symbols and machine detections enter a feature register before an archaeological site register. Existing survey teams should be consulted before a rediscovery claim or a new field plan.

The second stage samples contrasting landscape strata with declared effort. Candidate features, comparable map-negative surfaces, recent channel belts, stable interfluves, altered urban parcels and accessible shelters require explicit coverage records. Spatially separated evaluation cells reduce the risk of training and checking a predictor on neighboring manifestations of the same feature. The design must retain inaccessible and unobservable locations rather than code them as absences. Crop and seasonal visibility should be recorded and, where possible, revisited with a complementary method.

The third stage tests age and preservation. Cores and permitted small interventions locate relevant surfaces and assess stratigraphic integrity. Chronology should use material appropriate to the process: a sediment burial age, an occupation-associated short-lived specimen, or a carefully assessed marine-shell age addresses a different question. Blank samples, replicates and reference contexts accompany microfossil, residue and geophysical work. Saline or clay-rich ground may restrict particular geophysical methods; site-specific pilots should determine whether they add information.

The fourth stage expands excavation only when a question and maintenance plan justify it. A buried rural sequence, a craft chain or an exceptional preserved environmental context may deserve priority over a visually impressive but redundant architectural exposure. Records should link intervention area/volume, sieving and flotation procedures, retained samples, finds, dates and uncertainty. Publication of negative and undatable cases allows later teams to change a method without repeating an unexplained failure.

No specific total field cost or schedule is estimated here because deposit depth, ownership, permit requirements and analytical partners are not established. The appropriate first deliverable of collaboration would be a bounded pilot budget tied to a defined region and decision, rather than an excavation promise for all 21 windows.

## 9. Findings and limits

Excavation has remained selective for documented conservation, access, research and recording reasons. Current work continues at sites whose old labels can suggest inactivity. The historical excavation totals are useful evidence of a large investigation gap, while their differing scope and denominators prevent a precise present percentage.

Published surface and remote-sensing studies give measured dimensions for particular features and expose missing records. They support follow-up on small settlements and several sizeable mound proposals. Predictions about wholly buried cities require independently dated landforms, archaeological context and an observation model. Distance from a major city supplies a network hypothesis but cannot determine an unseen site's area or artifact yield.

The 21 windows translate plausible hiding mechanisms into comparisons that can fail. Alluvial, urban and agricultural settings have direct regional archaeological support; mountain/cave and offshore targets vary more in cultural and chronological support. Former coasts may survive inland, beneath sediment or under water. Local relative sea-level and sediment history determine which setting applies.

The proposed tests could add numerous ordinary artifacts, new environmental and craft evidence, better occupation sequences and more contextualized inscription observations. Their total quantity is presently uncalibrated. The immediate next research action is a concordance and sampling pilot in collaboration with existing regional projects, preserving the distinction between a feature detected, an occupation dated and an archaeological interpretation supported.

## 1. The headline excavation fraction has conflicting historical denominators

The University of Lucknow teaching chapter does reproduce 1,022 Mature Harappan sites and approximately 96 excavated, attributing its summary to 2002 sources. Its national subtotals are 406 plus 616 sites and 41 plus 55 excavated. The indexed source passage is on printed p. 41. That attribution should remain attached to the numbers because the chapter's own publication date and reconciliation of the underlying inventories were not established. [Teaching chapter](https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202003241550006972anil_kumar_HARAPPAN_CIVILIZATION%201.pdf).

A primary discrepancy is recoverable from Possehl's own chapter, *Harappans and hunters: economic interaction and specialization in prehistoric India*. Its publisher Introduction reports 1,056 Mature Harappan sites and 96 excavated, citing Possehl 1999, Appendix A. The chapter appeared in the 2002 book *Forager-Traders in South and Southeast Asia*, pp. 62–76; its online publication in 2009 does not update the archaeological count. The publisher extract was read, rather than the entire chapter. [Possehl chapter, DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511489631.005](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/foragertraders-in-south-and-southeast-asia/harappans-and-hunters-economic-interaction-and-specialization-in-prehistoric-india/F6870A6F5DBA69082DDF1DC1FEDA3FDB).

The two historical ratios are 9.39% and 9.09%. Their small numerical difference does not resolve why the inventories differ. Gadekar's newer review reports 142 excavated sites across the Early and Mature periods, using Uesugi's compilation with excavations through 2024 added. Its count passage is printed p. 156. The final issue is *Lithic Technology* 51(2), 2026, pp. 153–170; publication online was 18 July 2025. Publisher-indexed passages and metadata were checked, but a complete local PDF was not acquired in this audit. [Gadekar review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01977261.2025.2523610).

**Material correction:** retain the historical counts as attributed observations, explicitly record the 1,056 alternative, and avoid calculating a present excavation percentage by combining 142 with either old denominator. The inventories need a site-level concordance, phase rules, and a common cutoff date before a change in the Mature-only excavation rate can be calculated. A site counted as excavated may contain only a small investigated area, so these ratios also cannot measure the fraction of surviving deposits excavated.

## 2. The Cholistan results establish candidate detection, with selected validation

Orengo and colleagues' *Automated detection of archaeological mounds using machine-learning classification of multisensor and multitemporal satellite data* reports 337 proposed mound clusters after applying a threshold above 0.55. Of these, 71 could be linked with reasonable certainty to legacy sites, including five training mounds and twenty validation mounds. The unmatched remainder is arithmetically 266. The Results section and Discussion, “Villages and Towns: Mound Size Estimates,” report 246 clusters below five hectares, or 72.997% of 337. [Primary article, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2005583117](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7414161/).

The Methods, “Machine-Learning Algorithm,” specify that the 25 reference sites were large, well preserved and readily located in high-resolution imagery. The twenty test mounds were identified in the first classifier iteration, followed by two refinements; the threshold was chosen after visual inspection. Successful detection of this selected set cannot estimate recall for buried deposits, small scatters or all archaeological sites. The paper itself identifies modern seasonal settlements, excavated water ponds and natural clay accumulations as sensor-level rivals. Its combined sensor discrimination is useful evidence, but field confirmation and dating remain necessary for the unmatched candidates.

**Material correction:** label 266 as unmatched remote candidates, and retain “tentative visible mound extent” for the size distribution. The classifier score is not a calibrated probability of an Indus settlement. Legacy coordinates are imprecise, so unmatched candidates may include rediscoveries.

Ganweriwala's area estimates describe different observations. Gulzar reports December 2015 total-station work, with a boundary based on the visible cultural-material spread, and obtains about 66.7 hectares (printed pp. 37–38, PDF pp. 3–4). Her discussion records the earlier 81.5-hectare estimate and its uncertain measurement procedure. Orengo estimates approximately 33 hectares of visible mounded area. The latter paper also cites an approximately 80-hectare legacy estimate. These values neither form a confidence interval nor demonstrate a measured loss of half the original town. Surface scatter, surviving mound, burial, erosion, historical coordinate error and changing definitions require separate tests. [Gulzar, *Harappan Cities: Landscape and Surface Survey at Ganweriwala*](https://easaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Sidra-Gulzar-3.pdf).

## 3. Historical-map checking has a changing sampling design

Green and colleagues' *Re-Discovering Ancient Landscapes* confirms 779 checked historical-map features, 199 associated with extant archaeological sites, and 64 additional sites without apparent mapped-feature associations (PDF pp. 7–14). The 199/779 ratio is 25.55%. The 169 positives not linked to earlier reports may include earlier visits whose coordinates were inadequate. The sites span multiple periods, and the comparison's protohistoric category also includes Painted Grey Ware. [Primary article, DOI 10.3390/rs11182089](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/11/18/2089).

PDF p. 10 discloses an especially consequential selection rule: size-one checking initially stopped after ten negative locations per regional grid unit. In 2018, apparent ponds and dunes were excluded before visits, and the remaining selected candidates were checked. PDF p. 11 defines a site by at least two artifacts or features of verifiable antiquity within five metres; it discusses crop visibility and displaced worn pottery. The Table 1 rates therefore describe a changing checked sample, rather than unbiased probabilities for every mapped feature. The wider survey used prior locations, imagery and informants alongside map features.

**Material correction:** use Table 1 as descriptive evidence of symbol and size differences. Do not transfer its 25.55% or category rates into a national Indus forecast. The 64 off-map sites show a failure of a map-only search to include all encountered evidence, but they cannot quantify regional false-negative rate without a complete and consistently sampled denominator. Preserve a distinction between a negative surface visit, a destroyed feature, a buried feature and an inaccessible location in the proposed survey design.

## 4. Later cities and coastlines need process-specific predictions

The Government of Sindh's Lakhanjo-Daro account provides a concrete urban example: recent construction disturbed the upper deposits in four 2017 trenches, while some lower occupation deposits and craft evidence survived. Deep modern pits also mixed ancient material with garbage. The account supports monitoring redevelopment and existing archaeological exposures around Sukkur; it does not establish that every later city conceals an Indus settlement or provide an audited site-wide area. [Government site account, 3 January 2022](https://antiquities.sindhculture.gov.pk/tour/lakhian-jo-daro-site-sukkur/).

Garcia-Molsosa and colleagues distinguish older alluvium beneath Bronze Age deposits from younger channel fills that cut older deposits (PDF p. 23). This creates two rival outcomes: preservation beneath aggradation, and removal by erosion. In the Multan landscape, several relict river and canal associations are Early Historic or Medieval (PDF pp. 21–22). A visible channel beside a candidate mound needs its own date. [Primary article, DOI 10.1186/s40494-023-00985-6](https://heritagesciencejournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40494-023-00985-6).

Das and colleagues infer a local relative sea-level fall beginning around 4150 ± 230 BP from a trench four kilometres downstream of Lothal. Their Methods apply a marine reservoir correction, and the Discussion calls the reconstructed water level approximate because tidal information is limited. Estuarine infilling and changes in river connection are relevant competing processes when translating environmental proxies into a shoreline history. The result supports looking for former coastlines now inland as well as submerged surfaces. [Das et al. 2022, sections 2, 3 and 5](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021GC010264).

The 2026 Gulf of Khambhat synthesis reinforces the need for localized reconstruction: its indexed abstract distinguishes the two locations meeting full sea-level-index-point criteria from limiting or tectonically affected indicators. I read the publisher-indexed abstract and introduction, not the full article. [Vedpathak et al. 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.quaint.2026.110163](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1040618226000492).

**Material correction:** require dated terrestrial surfaces, occupation evidence in context and reconstructed local relative sea level before assigning offshore anomalies to the Indus period. A rectangular acoustic pattern or an isolated dated object would not resolve natural morphology, displaced objects or a different cultural period. The choice of a mountain, cave or landslide window likewise needs an independently supported occupation or exchange catchment, sediment chronology and a test of local cultural alternatives; topography alone supplies little evidence of an Indus settlement.

## Specific source hazard and remaining questions

The acquired Srivastava et al. 2023 review, *Extremely fast Holocene coastal landscape evolution in the Kachchh Upland*, is useful for tectonic and depositional mechanisms. PDF p. 5 nevertheless calls a sea-level fall around 3000 BP contemporary with Harappan centres dated approximately 2800–1800 BCE. Those time ranges do not coincide. Reuse the process arguments with independently checked chronology, and trace individual shoreline ages to their original dated observations. [Review, DOI 10.1080/17445647.2023.2167617](https://doi.org/10.1080/17445647.2023.2167617).

The unresolved questions that can materially change the manuscript are the concordance behind the historical site totals; the number and dates of field-confirmed Cholistan remote candidates since 2020; the contemporary survival and access of proposed urban parcels; and date/elevation control for each coastal or slope-burial window. Current research activity and a public database's existence should not be described as completion of those checks. The acquired GIOSAN2012_HTML witness is a CAPTCHA page and supplies no usable article evidence; the paper must be cited from a valid reading route if used.

No site-discovery claim, national count correction, measured excavation yield or settlement-area model was produced in this pass. The findings are intended to constrain the forthcoming manuscript and its separate adversarial review.

# Mountain valleys, caves and buried slopes: evidence and survey hypotheses

Prepared 11 October 2026 for the settlement-prospection study. This is a focused source review, with five proposed survey windows. It adds mountain evidence to the parent study; it does not report new field discoveries. Source IDs refer to [mountain-sources.json](mountain-sources.json), which records the actual reading depth and local witness hashes.

## What the evidence supports

The strongest mountain targets are cultivable valley floors, tributary fans and spring-fed foothills connected to known occupations. The existing observations provide much less support for an expectation of undiscovered Indus cities inside caves. Rock shelters can preserve short visits, hearths, animal management and later ritual use, all of which require different sampling from a large settlement mound.

Mehrgarh supplies a direct example of burial beside a mountain river. Jarrige and Lechevallier describe a Neolithic mound whose flanks were progressively covered by Bolan alluvium, with later deposits over those sediments and a river-cut section exposing the buried sequence (1980, printed p.253). The site is an archaeological observation of masking and erosion in the Kachi plain. Its buried Neolithic deposits precede the Indus urban period. A 2025 study models new tooth-enamel dates from 23 Neolithic burials and estimates the cemetery's start at 5223–4914 cal BCE in its preferred model; two samples were excluded to improve model agreement. These dates revise the earlier chronology of Period I rather than dating every fan surface or Bronze Age occupation. The paper discusses recurrent flooding as a possible contributor to rapid accumulation. [M01](https://doi.org/10.3406/paleo.1980.4279), [M02](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-92621-5).

Ghazi Shah links foothill water and Indus occupation. Pakistan's archaeological inventory places the mound beside the Bhit Range and a perennial spring, adjoining the alluvial plain toward Manchar Lake. Its account describes an Amri occupation followed by mixed Amri/Indus and Indus levels. The listed footprint is 160 by 130 m, or 2.08 ha, with an 11 m surviving height; these are inventory dimensions, not a phase-specific settlement boundary. A newer primary report abstract explicitly identifies a **2025 Ghazi Shah excavation** and portable archaeometric analyses. An account ending with Flam's 1980s campaigns therefore misses recent work. The full 2025 report was unavailable in the public metadata inspected here, so its stratigraphy and dates cannot yet be assessed. [M03](https://doam.gov.pk/public/sites/7279), [M04](https://hal.science/hal-05430765v1).

The MAFBI project already surveys and tests the southwestern Kirthar foothills in Sindh-Kohistan, Jamshoro district. Its published program connects that work to Chanhu-daro and questions about the early Indus period and relations with Balochistan. Mountain prospecting is an existing research direction. This study's contribution is a proposed comparison of exposed, buried and eroded surfaces within such landscapes. [M05](https://aibl.fr/fouilles/mission-archeologique-francaise-du-bassin-de-lindus-pakistan/).

For Kech-Makran, Besenval's original account describes low mountain chains, alluvial valleys, irregular storm floods and agricultural soils exposed to erosion. Modern karez irrigation appears in this account as present practice; it supplies no direct date for Bronze Age irrigation. A 2024 archaeobotanical study identifies Miri Qalat's Indus-related Period IV within a longer local sequence. Its case study classifies 12 of 13 preserved Phoenix seeds from Miri Qalat and Shahi Tump as wild forms. Table 2 lists 15 sample IDs and four conventional radiocarbon ages; their relationship to the 13 classified seeds needs reconciliation, and the authors' early-third-millennium BCE assignment needs a calibration check. This selected protohistoric sample cannot establish the whole region's farming regime, but it makes current date groves a poor automatic guide to ancient settlement suitability. The authors also identify possible bias from loss of narrow seed tips. [M06](https://doi.org/10.3406/arasi.1997.1399), [M07](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2024.106052).

Swat provides a useful boundary comparison. Olivieri's deposited thesis associates local agricultural settlements and exchange with a period contemporary with the Late Harappan phase. It discusses Cemetery H-style pottery at Barikot, whose local manufacture or import remains unresolved. Its shelter chronology uses stylistic, superposition and contextual comparisons across a long sequence; it explicitly leaves Swat's direct role in Harappan development unresolved (printed pp.166–167). Such evidence supports tests of contact and local occupation, with a lower expectation of Mature Indus settlement. [M08](https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/handle/fub188/1497).

## Five proposed survey windows

The windows below are geographic research settings. No candidate parcel, measured boundary, access permission or detection probability has been established. Each needs coordination with the relevant field mission and a join to its unpublished records before selecting new ground. The competing explanations should be fixed before survey results are interpreted.

**1. Bolan outlet–Dhadar–Nausharo, northern Kachi plain.** Test whether abandoned fan distributaries and low interfan surfaces retain occupations masked by younger sediments. The Mehrgarh section establishes that masking occurs here; Nausharo provides a known local-to-Indus settlement comparator in the Bolan plain. [M11](https://doam.gov.pk/public/sites/8927). Compare dated stable terraces with distal fan margins and recently reworked channels. Map buried soils and sediment contacts with cores and exposed sections, then investigate cultural layers only where sedimentary context is retained. The principal rival is a pre-Indus village or sterile fan deposit mistaken for an Indus occupation. A younger surface with no surviving older soil would defeat the local preservation premise. Cultural material redeposited in a channel would support erosion, not an intact settlement beneath the core.

**2. Naing Nai–Bhit Range–Ghazi Shah spring corridor, western Dadu.** Test for small rural or craft occupations at former spring channels and fan-to-plain interfaces beyond the known mound. Water access and the existing Amri/Indus sequence motivate the window. Select paired stable surfaces and sediment-covered margins at comparable walking distance from reconstructed water sources. Record off-mound lithic debris, small ceramic scatters and animal remains as well as mounds. Rival explanations include Amri-period activity, historic irrigation deposits and moved artifacts. Dates outside the target phase, or a demonstration that the watercourse postdates it, would reject an Indus-period association. The 2025 excavation makes collaboration and de-duplication especially necessary.

**3. Southwestern Kirthar foothills, Jamshoro district, including Kai–Naig as a verification subarea.** Compare existing recorded occupations with valley mouths, low slope benches and colluvial pockets that lack visible mounds. This region already belongs to the MAFBI survey program. The narrow additional hypothesis is that some slope-adjacent activity survives below sediment accumulated after occupation. At Kai, reconcile the cave names and individual cavities first: the 2015 government inventory describes a natural cave without architectural features, whereas popular accounts assign divergent ages and origins. A dated hearth or floor would provide a usable occupation claim. Geological cavities, recent smoke, historic structures or undated pottery on nearby slopes would leave the prehistoric cave hypothesis unresolved. The inventory passage alone supplies no authenticated Indus occupation date. [M09](https://rio-heritage.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/volume-i-inventory-of-cultural-property-in-the-province-of-sindh.pdf).

**4. Kech Valley around Turbat, Miri Qalat and Shahi Tump, extending into tributary-fan margins.** Test whether low rural occupations are obscured where tributaries spread sediment onto cultivable valley soils. Avoid making modern palm density the main predictor. Compare palaeochannel proximity, dated soils, slope runoff and surviving field surfaces, with settlement predictions separated by cultural phase. Geophysical anomalies should be tested against natural gravel lenses, historic irrigation and Islamic-period structures. An ancient object in storm sediment could have travelled from a known mound. Intact floors, food residues and independently dated short-lived material beneath the masking layer would discriminate buried occupation from transported debris. The locations of known settlements provide background evidence, not an estimate of the size of any missing one.

**5. Kotah–Kandak valleys and the Barikot terrace interface, Swat–Malakand.** Use this as a low-confidence test of small upland occupations, exchange and shelter use during the later Bronze Age. Treat painted shelters and terrace settlements as separate observation classes. Survey unpainted shelters as controls, because a painting-led sample selects ritual and visibility attributes. Map slope wash and debris fans before choosing subsurface tests. Local Bronze Age communities, later herders, Buddhist use and historic visitors are plausible rivals. Secure target-period occupation beneath a dated sediment layer would strengthen the contact hypothesis; later deposits alone would resolve that tested shelter's chronology without establishing a regional absence. A surface stylistic resemblance would not discriminate import, imitation and mixed deposits.

## Testing landslide and cave burial

This search did not locate a primary demonstration of an Indus occupation sealed beneath a dated landslide in these five windows. Landslide burial remains a mechanism to investigate after local geomorphological mapping. A debris lobe can bury a former bench, scour it away, mix material from uphill, or cover ground that was never occupied. Those outcomes produce different observations. A test should reconstruct the pre-slide surface, date the deposit and any underlying soil, assess sediment fabric and artifact abrasion, and seek coherent occupation features. The likelihood of occupation belongs to the former land surface; the likelihood of preservation belongs to the subsequent event. Distance to a large settlement does not answer either question by itself.

Caves need the same separation of site formation from cultural attribution. Kakar's original survey records occupation debris, smoke-darkened roofs and nearby lithics in northern Balochistan caves, alongside painted shelters. The paper lacks absolute dates and bases its proposed Palaeolithic attribution largely on lithic morphology and depicted fauna. It is useful evidence that mountain cavities contain material worth investigating; its animal pictures cannot date a Bronze Age occupation. [M10](https://doi.org/10.34780/mvc3-3u6b).

The proposed cave protocol would distinguish hearth charcoal from dispersed charcoal, screen for burrowing and slope-derived debris, quantify the sediment examined, and date short-lived samples associated with coherent features. Micromorphology could test whether dung or ash is in place. Negative results should report which deposits were reachable and sampled, since a blocked entrance or sterile upper layer does not inspect the entire cavity. The information expected from this work is a dated occupation history and activities at small sites, rather than a forecast of large artifact counts.

## Source and access record

The search covered primary archaeological publications, mission records, official inventories and geological leads, checked through 11 October 2026. It was focused rather than exhaustive. Repository memory was consulted, including the October 8 excavation retrospective and the registry's warning that the inscription census is a selected object-source cohort. No denominator from that census was used here.

Downloaded PDFs and extracted text remain local under `data/raw/2026-10-11-settlement-prospection/` with `MOUNTAIN_` names. The source register carries canonical URLs instead of transient session URLs. Publisher DOI access failed for Mutin 2025, while the university repository PDF succeeded. HAL article pages returned an Anubis access denial; its public API supplied metadata and the Ghazi Shah report abstract. No inaccessible report was represented as fully read. The Kai inventory was available only as an indexed primary passage at printed p.383. These access differences limit the claims above.

Chronological problems discovered during the search changed the proposed evidence frame: the early Mehrgarh chronology has a 2025 revision; the Kech seed study requires sample and calibration reconciliation; and Ghazi Shah has a 2025 excavation record. No numerical discovery model, field survey or remote imagery classification was run in this subtask.

def recover_positives(tested: int, percent: str | None) -> int:
    """Find an integer compatible with the table's two-decimal precision."""
    if not isinstance(tested, int) or isinstance(tested, bool) or tested < 0:
        raise ValueError("A tested-feature count must be a nonnegative integer")
    if tested == 0:
        if percent is not None:
            raise ValueError("A zero denominator has no defined percentage")
        return 0
    if percent is None:
        raise ValueError("A positive denominator requires a reported percentage")
    target = Decimal(percent).quantize(Decimal("0.01"))
    if not Decimal(0) <= target <= Decimal(100):
        raise ValueError("Percentage outside zero to 100")
    compatible = [
        count for count in range(tested + 1)
        if (Decimal(100) * Decimal(count) / Decimal(tested)).quantize(
            Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP) == target
    ]
    if len(compatible) != 1:
        raise ValueError(f"Expected one compatible integer; found {len(compatible)}")
    return compatible[0]


def finite_nonnegative(value: float, name: str) -> float:
    if not math.isfinite(value) or value < 0:
        raise ValueError(f"{name} must be finite and nonnegative")
    return value


def square_equivalent(area_ha: float, *, area_unit: str = "ha") -> float:
    if area_unit != "ha":
        raise ValueError("Area must be expressed in hectares")
    finite_nonnegative(area_ha, "Area")
    return math.sqrt(10_000 * area_ha)


def excavation_volume(area_ha: float, fraction: float, thickness_m: float,
                      *, area_unit: str = "ha", thickness_unit: str = "m") -> float:
    if area_unit != "ha" or thickness_unit != "m":
        raise ValueError("Use hectares for area and metres for thickness")
    for name, value in (("Area", area_ha), ("Fraction", fraction), ("Thickness", thickness_m)):
        finite_nonnegative(value, name)
    if fraction > 1:
        raise ValueError("Excavated area fraction exceeds one")
    return 10_000 * area_ha * fraction * thickness_m


def synthetic_fragments(volume_m3: float, density_per_m3: float, recovery: float) -> float:
    for name, value in (("Volume", volume_m3), ("Density", density_per_m3), ("Recovery", recovery)):
        finite_nonnegative(value, name)
    if recovery > 1:
        raise ValueError("Recovery fraction exceeds one")
    return volume_m3 * density_per_m3 * recovery


def repeat_detection(probability: float, visits: int) -> float:
    if not 0 <= probability <= 1 or not math.isfinite(probability):
        raise ValueError("Detection probability must lie between zero and one")
    if not isinstance(visits, int) or isinstance(visits, bool) or visits < 0:
        raise ValueError("Visit count must be a nonnegative integer")
    return 1 - (1 - probability) ** visits


def scoped_percent(numerator: int, denominator: int, *, numerator_scope: str,
                   denominator_scope: str) -> float:
    """Require an explicitly shared population before producing a ratio."""
    if numerator_scope != denominator_scope:
        raise ValueError("Numerator and denominator have different recorded scopes")
    if not denominator_scope:
        raise ValueError("A scope is required")
    if not 0 <= numerator <= denominator or denominator == 0:
        raise ValueError("Counts do not define a proportion")
    return 100 * numerator / denominator


def metadata(census: dict, dating: dict) -> tuple[dict, list[dict], list[dict]]:
    sites = census["sites"]
    dated = dating["sites"]
    coordinate_status = Counter(row["coord_status"] for row in sites)
    country_rows = []
    for country in sorted({row["country"] for row in sites}):
        subset = [row for row in sites if row["country"] == country]
        located = sum(row.get("lat") is not None and row.get("lon") is not None for row in subset)
        country_rows.append({"country": country, "entries": len(subset),
                             "accepted_coordinate_present": located,
                             "accepted_coordinate_absent": len(subset) - located})

    yields = [{"area_ha": area, "excavated_area_fraction": fraction,
               "deposit_thickness_m": thickness, "excavated_volume_m3": volume,
               "assumed_fragment_density_per_m3": density, "assumed_recovery_fraction": recovery,

def verification(meta: dict, green: dict, arithmetic: dict, scenarios: dict,
                 input_files: list[dict]) -> dict:
    checks: list[dict] = []

    def check(name: str, condition: bool, detail: str) -> None:
        checks.append({"name": name, "passed": bool(condition), "detail": detail})
        if not condition:
            raise AssertionError(name)

    def rejects(name: str, function, *args, **kwargs) -> None:
        try:
            function(*args, **kwargs)
        except (ValueError, TypeError):
            check(name, True, "The deliberately invalid input was rejected")
        else:
            check(name, False, "The invalid input was accepted")

    check("coordinate_status_partition", sum(meta["coordinate_file"]["coordinate_status_counts"].values()) == meta["coordinate_file"]["entries"],
          "Coordinate-status counts conserve the selected population")
    check("country_partition", sum(row["entries"] for row in meta["coordinate_file"]["country_counts"]) == meta["coordinate_file"]["entries"],
          "Country counts conserve the selected population")
    check("coordinate_presence_matches_accepted_statuses", meta["coordinate_file"]["accepted_coordinate_present"] ==
          sum(meta["coordinate_file"]["coordinate_status_counts"][key] for key in ("census-verified", "census-partial")),
          "Null-coordinate and accepted-status totals agree")
    check("dating_phase_partition", sum(meta["dating_file"]["phase_basis_counts"].values()) == meta["dating_file"]["phase_entries"],
          "Chronological basis counts conserve phase entries")
    check("two_cohorts_remain_distinct", meta["coordinate_file"]["south_asian_entries"] != meta["dating_file"]["site_entries"],
          "No positional or name-based join has merged the selected 85-entry and 86-entry cohorts")
    check("green_published_totals", (green["tested_features"], green["archaeological_positives"]) == (779, 199),
          "The recovered cells reproduce the table's total")
    for dimension in ("by_map_symbol", "by_map_size_class"):
        check(f"green_{dimension}_partition", sum(row["tested_features"] for row in green[dimension]) == 779
              and sum(row["archaeological_positives"] for row in green[dimension]) == 199,
              "Independent row and column aggregations reproduce the total")
    for cell in green["cells"]:
        count, tested = cell["recovered_archaeological_positives"], cell["tested_features"]
        check(f"green_rounding_{cell['map_symbol'].replace(' ', '_')}_{cell['size_class']}",
              0 <= count <= tested and (tested == 0 or abs(100 * count / tested - float(cell["reported_percent"])) < 0.0051),
              "Integer counts obey their denominator and the displayed rounding precision")
    rejects("mutation_wrong_percent", recover_positives, 37, "54.06")
    rejects("mutation_zero_denominator_percentage", recover_positives, 0, "0")
    rejects("mutation_negative_feature_count", recover_positives, -1, "50")
    rejects("mutation_percentage_outside_range", recover_positives, 10, "101")
    rejects("mutation_ambiguous_rounded_count", recover_positives, 100_000, "50")
    rejects("mutation_area_unit", square_equivalent, 30.0, area_unit="acres")
    rejects("mutation_negative_area", square_equivalent, -1.0)
    rejects("mutation_thickness_unit", excavation_volume, 30.0, 0.01, 1.0, thickness_unit="cm")
    rejects("mutation_area_fraction", excavation_volume, 30.0, 1.1, 1.0)
    rejects("mutation_recovery_fraction", synthetic_fragments, 3000.0, 5.0, 1.1)
    rejects("mutation_nonfinite_density", synthetic_fragments, 3000.0, float("nan"), 0.8)
    check("yield_linear_scaling", synthetic_fragments(3000, 50, 0.8) == 10 * synthetic_fragments(3000, 5, 0.8),
          "Changing only density by a factor of ten changes the fragment result by the same factor")
    check("zero_recovery", synthetic_fragments(3000, 50, 0.0) == 0,
          "Recovery zero produces zero fragments")
    check("geometry_unit_conversion", square_equivalent(1) == 100 and math.isclose(square_equivalent(200), math.sqrt(2_000_000)),
          "Hectares convert to square metres before square-equivalent dimensions are calculated")
    check("synthetic_equal_expected_records", len({row["expected_recorded_sites"] for row in scenarios["observation_nonidentifiability_examples"]}) == 1,
          "The distinct constructed true totals share an expected record count")
    check("repeat_zero_detection", repeat_detection(0, 10) == 0,
          "Repeating a method with zero detection probability does not recover hidden deposits")
    check("repeat_monotonicity", all(repeat_detection(0.1, k) <= repeat_detection(0.1, k + 1) for k in range(10)),
          "Detection accumulates under the stated independent-visit assumptions")
    check("remote_unmatched_arithmetic", arithmetic["cholistan_remote_mounds"]["unmatched_clusters"] == 266,
          "The unmatched unit is a remotely detected cluster")
    check("historical_ratios_keep_96_numerator", all(arithmetic[key]["excavated"] == 96 for key in
          ("historical_excavation_ratio", "historical_excavation_ratio_primary_2002")),
          "Each historical ratio uses its source's 96 excavated Mature Harappan sites")
    check("historical_denominators_remain_separate", arithmetic["historical_excavation_ratio"]["mature_harappan_sites"] == 1022
          and arithmetic["historical_excavation_ratio_primary_2002"]["mature_harappan_sites"] == 1056,
          "The secondary and primary counts are retained as distinct source observations")
    check("primary_2002_ratio", math.isclose(arithmetic["historical_excavation_ratio_primary_2002"]["percent"], 100 / 11),
          "96/1056 is approximately 9.09 percent")
    check("recent_review_count_has_no_ratio", arithmetic["recent_review_count_separate_scope"]["excavated_site_count"] == 142
          and arithmetic["recent_review_count_separate_scope"]["computed_excavation_percent"] is None,
          "The Early/Mature review count is not blended into historical Mature-only ratios")
    rejects("mutation_blend_142_with_1022", scoped_percent, 142, 1022,
            numerator_scope="Early and Mature through 2024", denominator_scope="historical Mature teaching chapter")
    rejects("mutation_blend_142_with_1056", scoped_percent, 142, 1056,
            numerator_scope="Early and Mature through 2024", denominator_scope="Possehl 2002 Mature census")
    check("no_empirical_artifact_forecast", scenarios["status"].startswith("SYNTHETIC") and "No source-derived" in scenarios["yield_assumptions"],
          "Output type and assumption records prohibit empirical or civilization-wide forecast interpretation")
    for item in input_files:
        path = REPOSITORY / item["path"]
        check(f"input_hash_{path.name}", sha256(path) == item["sha256"], "Input hash unchanged during computation")
        changed = hashlib.sha256(path.read_bytes() + b"\x00").hexdigest()
        check(f"mutation_hash_{path.name}", changed != item["sha256"], "A one-byte mutation changes the recorded hash")
    return {"status": "passed", "check_count": len(checks), "checks": checks,
            "scope": "Computational checks of declared counts, units, rounding and output type. Archaeological source interpretation and field validation require separate review."}
