# What remains to be found? Excavation limits, hidden landscapes and a testable survey agenda for the Indus Civilization

Complete working manuscript prepared 11 October 2026. Structured narrative review and exploratory survey design. Research and writing used AI assistance. Author names, affiliations and a submission venue have not been assigned. Source observations, calculations, reviews and revisions are preserved in the project. No fieldwork or new satellite-image analysis was conducted for this study.

# # Abstract

The large difference between recorded Indus sites and excavated sites reflects selective archaeological investigation, the cost of preserving exposed remains, access constraints, incomplete publication and a landscape record altered by rivers, agriculture and later construction. The familiar estimate of about 100 excavated sites among more than 1000 recorded locations is a historical summary with inconsistent denominators. A recent review counts 142 excavated Early and Mature Harappan sites through 2024, but a compatible present-day denominator remains unresolved. We examine primary archaeological and environmental studies and recompute published aggregate counts. In Cholistan,337 remotely proposed mound clusters include 71 legacy matches;266 unmatched candidates await secure identity and chronology. A northwest Indian historical-map survey found archaeological evidence at 199 of 779 checked features and encountered 64 further locations without mapped-feature associations. Its changing selection rules prevent using those rates as population probabilities. We assemble 21 coarse survey windows with supporting evidence, competing explanations and falsification tests. They cover leveled rural sites, deposits beneath later cities and alluvium, mountain and piedmont settings, caves and colluvium, former shorelines now inland, and potentially submerged surfaces. Local landform chronology controls these proposals. Constructed excavation-volume examples demonstrate sensitivity of artifact recovery to deposit density and sampling, while providing no civilization-wide forecast. The research agenda combines archival concordance, stratified survey, geomorphology, limited subsurface investigation and improved recovery of environmental and craft evidence. Its deliverables are hypotheses and reproducible audits that can guide field collaboration.

## 1. The question behind the excavation gap

The remaining archaeological potential concerns both places and information. An unexcavated mound may retain a household sequence, a workshop, a burial ground or an environmental record. A heavily excavated city may still have poorly sampled peripheral neighborhoods. A settlement covered by sediment may leave no visible mound. A collection already in a museum may contain plant, residue or manufacturing evidence that its initial study did not recover. These are different opportunities and require different investigations.

For readers asking how much of the Indus Civilization has been investigated, three quantities matter: the number of recorded locations, the number with any excavation, and the area or volume actually investigated within them. A site can enter an excavation list after a small test trench. Its appearance in that list does little to describe how much remains below ground. A mound is a physical observation; cultural attribution and period boundaries require evidence. Several mounds can form one site, and one mound can contain several periods.

We use Early, Mature and Late Harappan as archaeological period labels whose regional definitions vary. The familiar broad Mature interval of about 2600-1900 BCE is a working reference for comparison, rather than a date assigned automatically to a feature. A protohistoric inventory can include Painted Grey Ware or other periods beyond this interval. Local Bronze Age communities in Balochistan and adjacent mountain regions must retain their own cultural classifications. Exchange with an Indus city establishes contact more readily than it establishes the cultural identity of an entire settlement.

This study asks why excavation has remained selective, what can be inferred about the size of incompletely investigated places, which landscape changes could conceal further evidence, and what additional recovery would contribute. It develops geographic tests from published research and interdisciplinary reasoning. The rectangles in the accompanying map are sampling frames drawn for this study. Their archaeological content has not been measured.

## 2. Evidence and analytical procedure

### 2.1 Source selection and reading depth

The review began with the project's preserved 8 October 2026 excavation assessment. Searches covered Cholistan; Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan; Sindh; Gujarat and Kutch; and Kech-Makran and mountain approaches. Archaeological survey and excavation reports were read alongside river morphodynamics, sedimentary stratigraphy, remote sensing, coastal evolution, archaeobotany and recent biomolecular work. Searches for recent publications and field activity extended through 11 October 2026. The protocol was recorded before the new calculations and complete draft; the user's mountain, city-burial and submerged-coast additions were recorded as a scope amendment.

This is a structured narrative review. It has no complete bibliographic search denominator and cannot establish that every relevant report has been found. The source register records the observation, exact locator, publication/field date where established, canonical link, actual reading depth and local witness hash. Full texts were acquired where available. Selected articles were available only as publisher-indexed passages, abstracts or institutional accounts. Their use is limited to observations supported by that access. The full 2026 Khambhat article, for example, was unavailable; its abstract supplies methodological qualifications, not a data table reanalysis. [R34]

The 2025 article *Hidden in Plain Sight* and itsJanuary 2026 correction were checked because current research already integrates historical maps, field survey and the MAHSA database. The correction adds an author; the source register uses the published article's title, rather than the earlier title retained on a repository cover. Historical-map extraction and satellite mound detection are established methods. Our contribution consists of reconciling units, identifying preservation-based comparisons, and specifying tests across the user's proposed hidden settings. [R09,R12,R13,R14,R27]

### 2.2 Units and reproducible calculations

We distinguish archaeological locations, cartographic features, remotely proposed clusters, phase memberships and excavation interventions. A new name, a missing match or a higher feature count does not by itself establish a previously unknown settlement. Positives, negatives, unvisited locations, inaccessible locations and undatable material have separate statuses.

The computations reproduce selected published totals and audit the project's existing metadata. Green and colleagues' Table 1 gives numbers tested and rounded percentages for symbol/size groups. We recover the integer number of positives only when one integer is compatible with the displayed rounding, and verify the row, column and overall totals. This uses the published aggregate table; the unavailable supplementary site rows were not reanalyzed. Calculations report percentages descriptively, with no population confidence intervals or national extrapolation. [R10]

The local inscription-site register has 115 mixed entries and a separate chronology register has 86 selected South Asian site entries. They were assembled for earlier inscription research. The new analysis hashes both inputs and reports their coverage without turning their omissions into archaeological absences. Source-based arithmetic, square-equivalent geometry and synthetic yield scenarios are saved separately. The computational outputs are identified in AppendixB.

### 2.3 Hypothesis classes and geographic precision

ClassA windows follow already documented archaeology, published remote proposals or published survey priorities. ClassB windows compare preservation and visibility where regional archaeology supports the question. ClassC windows require landform chronology or cultural attribution before a settlement search has a well-supported target. These classes describe the type of evidence available. They are neither probabilities of discovery nor an assessment of current field access.

Each window has a named region, a proposed missingness mechanism, a source connection, a competing explanation, a test and a comparison stratum. Bounds are editorial rectangles rounded to 0.1 degrees. They overlap and cannot be summed into an investigated area. Regional evidence supports the question within a window; it does not establish equal suitability in every parcel. Permits, local partnerships, recent development, land ownership and safety remain unassessed.

## 3. Why excavation remains selective

### 3.1 The headline counts do not share a present denominator

A University of Lucknow teaching chapter reports 1022 Mature Harappan sites and approximately 96 excavated, citing 2002 literature. Possehl's own 2002 chapter gives 1056 Mature sites and 96 excavated, referring to his 1999 appendix. These historical ratios are 9.39% and 9.09%. Their discrepancy remains unresolved because we have not joined the underlying inventories location by location. The book chapter's 2009 online date does not update the archaeological count. Gadekar's review, published online in 2025 and assigned to a 2026 issue, reports 142 excavated Early and Mature sites through 2024. Its period scope differs from the old Mature-only totals. A present fraction requires a common site definition, phase rule, cutoff date and concordance. [R01,R02,R38]

|Observation|Recorded denominator|Excavated numerator|What the number measures|
|---|---:|---:|---|

|Possehl 2002 publisher extract|1056 Mature locations|96|Alternative primary historical summary|
|Gadekar review through 2024|Compatible denominator unavailable|142 Early+Mature sites|Review population across two periods|

The numerical difference between 96 and 142 cannot establish how many Mature-only sites were newly excavated. Nor can a site-count percentage measure excavated hectares. Excavation intensity is poorly represented by a yes/no field: a dated test pit, several seasons and large exposed neighborhoods all count as activity but produce different information.

### 3.2 Excavation creates long-term obligations

Excavation removes deposits to reveal their relationships. Once an intact stratigraphic sequence has been dug, its interpretation depends on records, samples and retained materials. Exposed mudbrick and baked brick also require protection. The 1990 ICOMOS charter advocates survey, limited excavation, retained undisturbed portions and maintenance after exposure. These are professional conservation principles; actual permitting and implementation differ by jurisdiction. [R03]

Moenjodaro's 2026 UNESCO conservation record provides a current concrete example. Salts, capillary groundwater and monsoon exposure affect vulnerable brickwork; the 2022 floods and limited conservation resources form part of the management problem. Funding a new trench therefore implies obligations extending beyond a field season. The evidence supports conservation as a real constraint, although it supplies no common cost-per-hectare estimate for every Indus site. [R04]

Research design also limits excavation deliberately. A team may obtain more useful chronological or economic evidence by sampling contrasting small settlements and obtaining environmental samples than by exposing another large stretch of familiar architecture. Documentation, specialist analysis, storage and publication can consume years after digging ends. The excavation gap consequently includes choices about what to ask and how to preserve information, as well as constraints on what can be funded.

### 3.3 Access, destruction and publication

The 2007 RIHN report says planned Ganweriwala excavation was postponed because of political turmoil. Gulzar'sDecember 2015 surface survey describes desert and border-access constraints. These are dated examples, not a permanent account of the site's status: a 2024 University of Lahore notice documents participation in excavation. Current projects cannot be classified from an older “unexcavated” label alone. [R05,R06,R07]

Agricultural leveling, industrial construction, quarrying and sediment movement can change what is accessible even when a location is known. At Lakhian jo Daro in Sukkur, modern disturbance removed or mixed upper deposits while lower craft contexts survived in some excavation areas. A survey's inability to see pottery on the present surface cannot distinguish destroyed from buried archaeology. Published lists also lag behind field activity and may omit specialist categories, as Gadekar's lithic review illustrates. [R21,R22,R02]

The sources do not support one universal explanation such as lack of interest, a prohibition on excavation or a civilization-wide shortage of permission. They support several locally documented constraints. A budget for future work should include the archive, conservation and analytical stages, alongside discovery and digging.

## 4. How large could the incompletely investigated places be?

### 4.1 Visible area, cultural spread and occupation footprint

Ganweriwala illustrates the measurement problem. Gulzar's 2015 ground survey estimates 66.7 ha using cultural-material spread and total-station mapping. An older estimate is 81.5 ha, with uncertain measurement procedure. Orengo and colleagues estimate about 33 ha of remotely visible mounded area. The three values describe different definitions and evidence. They do not form a statistical interval for the city's original area, and they do not demonstrate that half the city was destroyed between surveys. Peripheral scatters, dune burial, erosion, redeposition and phase differences can all affect the comparison. [R07,R08]

The physical scale of these numbers can be made concrete without assuming a shape. A square with an area of 1 ha has a 100 m side;5 ha corresponds to 224 m;30 ha to 548 m;66.7 ha to 817 m; and 200 ha to 1.41 km. These are square-equivalent dimensions. An actual settlement may be long, irregular or split across separate mounds. Its inhabited area at one date may be smaller than the combined cultural spread accumulated across centuries.

At Kotada in Lodrani, a reported 100×60 m fortified element corresponds to 0.6 ha. It is a structure boundary, not a measured total occupation area. That site's 2024 Morodharo name duplicated an earlier documented locality. Small shifts in naming, period assignment or measured feature type can otherwise produce apparent new discoveries or inconsistent size rankings. [R19]

### 4.2 What the remote mound sample suggests

The 2020 Cholistan study reports 337 proposed clusters,246 under 5 ha and six above 20 ha. The smaller category accounts for 73.0% of proposed visible mounds. Two notable unmatched proposals were approximately 29 ha at Khundowala and 20.5 ha at Mulhiawala. Their visible size warrants follow-up; secure cultural and chronological attribution requires field evidence. A remote size distribution cannot be treated as a distribution of all surviving Indus settlements. Small scatters, deeply buried places and some dune-covered remains have different detection chances. [R08]

Large mounds tend to provide prominent surface and sensor signals. A new very large settlement concealed beneath alluvium or later urban fabric remains physically possible, but its probability cannot be estimated from this sample. The evidence justifies expecting a substantial role for small places among additional detectable candidates. It provides no supported list of unseen cities as large as the best-known urban centers.

### 4.3 Geography helps locate tests more than it predicts size

Proximity to a large city can motivate a search for villages, farms, workshops and exchange routes. It does not fix their size. A nearby settlement could serve a specialized task, predate the city, continue after it or belong to another network. Intensive survey around a city also increases discovery effort, making its surroundings appear richer even if the underlying settlement density is comparable elsewhere.

The 2009 Land, Water and Settlement season illustrates both settlement richness and naming ambiguity. A legacy Masudpur entry represented four mounds with one coordinate; renewed mapping recorded 13 mounds. The wider Rakhigarhi survey recorded 127 all-period locations with overlapping phase memberships. Those phase counts cannot be summed as separate sites. This evidence favors phase-specific rural survey and better concordance before a city-centered area model is fitted. [R29]

A future extent model should predict a distribution of measurable boundaries using independently dated occupation, landform age, access to water and resources, and survey effort. It should be evaluated on withheld regions and checked against small and buried sites. The present study fits no such model. Available regional evidence supplies hypotheses about where to measure, rather than authenticated dimensions for invisible sites.

## 5. Processes that can hide or remove evidence

### 5.1 Later cities and repeated building

Later cities offer two opposing preservation pathways. Deep foundations, utilities, quarry pits and leveling may remove deposits. Shallower building and accumulated urban fill may cover earlier layers while protecting portions beneath them. The surviving evidence can vary across adjacent parcels. Lakhian jo Daro demonstrates this mixture and supports an urban archaeological sampling strategy based on construction history and stratified exposures. [R21]

The test is strongest where earlier pottery, historic mound descriptions or dated excavation already establishes a local Indus presence. In Sukkur-Rohri, archived trench records and former mound extents could guide permitted monitoring of redevelopment, with modern dump deposits and deep disturbed foundations as comparison contexts. For later cities lacking any such evidence, geographic convenience alone yields a weaker hypothesis. Continuity of water access or a route must be demonstrated for the Bronze Age, not inferred from the modern city's success.

An archive-first approach can preserve useful information without opening new excavations. It would reconcile excavation loci with parcel history, separate intact deposits from redeposited finds, and register the depth and date of archaeological surfaces in existing sections. Any future construction monitoring should be planned with the relevant authorities and local specialists. This paper has not inspected contemporary building parcels.

### 5.2 Alluvial burial and flooding

Flooding can cover an occupation surface with sediment, and a migrating river can cut it away. These processes produce similar surface invisibility while leaving different subsurface possibilities. Garcia-Molsosa and colleagues describe older alluvium and paleosols associated with Bronze Age occupation, alongside younger fills that cut earlier surfaces in the southern Punjab landscape. Their spatial comparisons support testing older bar/interfluve margins against younger channel belts around Harappa, Khanewal, Old Beas and Ganji Bar. [R11]

The chronology of a river feature is essential. Singh and colleagues date the major Sutlej abandonment of the studied Ghaggar-Hakra course to shortly after 8 ka, well before urban Indus occupation. The 2021 foothill-flood study documents a different phenomenon, episodic larger flows around 3.9-3.8 ka. A landscape can lose a major Himalayan river and still experience later seasonal flows or floods. These observations motivate flank-to-channel coring and phase-specific occupation mapping; a general “dry river” label loses the temporal detail needed for prospecting. [R15,R16]

Sediment cores should establish whether the target-age ground surface survives, its depth and the deposits above it. Optically stimulated luminescence can date suitable sediment deposition; radiocarbon can date appropriate organic material. Their ages concern sampled material and require stratigraphic interpretation. An old charcoal fragment redeposited by a flood does not date settlement construction. The most informative result could be an intact occupation horizon beneath a dated cover, or evidence that channel scour removed the relevant surface.

### 5.3 Leveled mounds and dune cover

Historical maps can retain evidence of a mound before leveling. In Green and colleagues' northwest Indian survey, symbol types and sizes differed in how often archaeological material was encountered at checked locations. Form-line features yielded 150 positives among 393 checks, shaded features 21 of 154, hachures 26 of 227 and combined symbols 2 of 5. The overall 199 of 779 is 25.55%. These rates describe a changing selected sample: checking of small features initially stopped after negative visits, and later imagery excluded apparent ponds and dunes before field visits. They cannot be used as unbiased probabilities for every old map feature. [R10]

Map-only searching also omits evidence. The same survey encountered 64 additional locations without apparent mapped-feature associations. This supports a comparison stratum away from mapped mounds. It does not supply a complete regional denominator for false-negative rate. The published priorities around Tohana-Agroha, Khanauri-Nabha and Maur-Bathinda-Phul should be treated as follow-up to existing research. [R10]

In Cholistan, the classifier's five training and twenty selected validation mounds were large and well preserved. Its threshold followed visual inspection and its iterative refinement used observed performance. The reported success on those locations does not establish sensitivity to buried sites or low-relief camps. Water ponds, modern seasonal settlements and natural clay accumulations are relevant sensor-level rivals. Field comparison of candidate and noncandidate surfaces must therefore accompany dating. [R08]

### 5.4 Mountains, caves, fans and landslides

Mountain research should begin with habitable surfaces and known connections: valley floors, foothill terraces, spring approaches and routes linking dated occupations. In Kech-Makran, Miri Qalat contains an Indus-period settlement within a much longer local sequence. The Kirthar piedmont already has an active archaeological research history, including mission survey and test excavation around Sindh Kohistan and Jamshoro. These anchors support additional comparisons of stable terrace margins, fan-covered surfaces and ephemeral activity areas. They also make duplication checks necessary before calling a feature overlooked. [R26,R37]

Mehrgarh provides a direct regional burial analogue: Jarrige and Lechevallier describe Neolithic mound flanks covered by Bolan alluvium and exposed by a river-cut section. This is pre-Indus evidence of the process. A 2025 tooth-enamel study revised the chronology of the sampled PeriodIcemetery, estimating its start at 5223-4914 calBCE in a preferred model that excluded two dates. It does not date the Bronze Age surfaces sought here. The known Nausharo sequence supplies a local-to-Indus comparator in the Bolan plain. [R39,R40,R45]

Ghazi Shah's government inventory records Amri and Indus levels beside a perennial spring and the Bhit Range. A primary report abstract explicitly identifies a 2025 excavation there. Its full report was unavailable, so recent activity can be confirmed without assigning new stratigraphy or dates. At Kai, the 2015 government inventory describes a natural cave without architecture or an authenticated Indus date. Kakar's original northern Balochistan shelter survey also lacks absolute dates. These observations favor valley and piedmont tests while leaving cave attribution open. [R41,R42,R43,R44]

A fan may aggrade across an occupied terrace, leaving older ground beneath younger sediment. Colluvial deposits can bury a slope-edge activity area. A landslide can cover an intact surface, deform it, or carry artifacts downslope away from their original context. The occurrence of a landslide therefore supplies a preservation question, without supplying an occupation date. These mechanisms remain proposed tests in the Kech and Bolan-Kachi windows; no buried Indus settlement beneath a newly identified landslide was established here.

Caves and rock shelters have a different expected archaeological scale. Small deposits can preserve visits, fuel, food remains, storage, pastoral use or a burial. Evidence of paintings alone would leave habitation and chronology unresolved. A useful search would compare shelters near independently supported contemporary routes and water sources with accessible shelters away from those routes, record sediment depth and disturbance, and seek intact dated occupation. It must test pre-Indus, local Bronze Age and later occupation as explicit alternatives. Expecting an urban settlement within a cave would require architectural and environmental evidence beyond topography.

Slope morphology, karst geology and least-cost route models could help define future strata. Modern paths and springs need historical qualification, and a transport model should account for seasonal water, terrain and the dated resource network. No route-cost surface, cave inventory or landslide chronology was computed in this study. The strongest near-term mountain opportunity is testing buried or lightly represented activity within known archaeological catchments, while caves remain an exploratory component.

### 5.5 Former coasts, inland paleoshorelines and submerged surfaces

“Now underwater” depends on relative sea level at the location. Ocean level, crustal movement, sediment compaction, delta advance, estuarine infill and erosion all change the relation between land and water. A global sea-level curve cannot by itself identify where an Indus-period ground surface lies today. Early Holocene landscapes submerged before the Indus periods address a different archaeological question.

The 2026 Khambhat synthesis distinguishes formal sea-level index points from limiting and tectonically affected indicators. Its abstract reports that only Lothal and Vadgam meet the full index-point criteria in the reviewed set. Das and colleagues'2022 Lothal-area study interprets a local fall beginning 4150 ± 230 BP and continuing to 3625 ± 200 BP, based on proxies in a trench downstream of the site. Tidal limitations affect the reconstructed elevation, and sediment infill can also reduce navigability. These observations support investigation of former shorelines now inland as well as inundated surfaces. [R32,R34]

Kutch research similarly describes former shallow marine areas that became inland salt flats through interacting tectonic and depositional changes. One chronology statement in the 2023 review equates about 3000 BP with Harappan centers dated 2800-1800 BCE; those intervals do not coincide. We use the geomorphic mechanisms while requiring independently checked ages for proposed archaeological surfaces. [R33]

Biagi's 2024 coastal study identifies shell middens and other low-relief evidence in Las Bela and Lower Sindh, including occupation around former lagoons and stone-built Sonari. Its sequences include different periods and explicitly uncalibratedBP dates. These are evidence for a changing coastal archaeological record; marine-shell dates require reservoir and calibration assessment before comparison with calendar-BCE Indus phases. The study also records industrial loss. [R31]

For offshore prospecting, the first target is a preserved, dated terrestrial surface in a plausible contemporary coastal catchment. Bathymetry and sub-bottom profiling could locate buried landscape units; cores would test sediment environment and age. Side-scan sonar and diver inspection would follow sufficiently supported targets. Rectangular rock patterns, isolated objects and a single old radiocarbon result remain compatible with natural geometry, transport or a different period. Submerged settlement boundaries require associated in-situ occupation evidence. The Makran coastal strip and Khambhat estuary windows are therefore ClassC chronology-first proposals.

Three agendas follow from the same register. No numerical scoring weights are imposed, and none of these agendas is a funded field itinerary.

The most speculative windows should be inexpensive to reject at the first stage. If a dated surface is too young, too old, eroded or permanently marine during the target period, it leaves the archaeological candidate set. If intact cultural deposits prove local and contemporaneous but lack diagnostic Indus affiliation, they contribute to regional history with that classification. A failed Harappan prediction can still produce useful chronological evidence.

## 7. How much additional material and knowledge could excavation recover?

### 7.1 Artifact quantity needs excavated volume and context

Site area alone does not predict finds. Let A be the site's area in hectares, f the fraction opened, h its mean excavated archaeological thickness in metres, d the recoverable fragment density per cubic metre, and r the retained recovery fraction. An illustrative volume isV=10000 Afh, and a count isY = V d r. Thickness includes only the archaeological deposit represented by the density assumption; sterile cover has a different recovery process. Fragment count is also affected by how breakage, sorting and joining are defined.

For a hypothetical 30 ha site with 1% opened through 1 m of relevant deposits,V=3000 m³. At a synthetic recovery fraction of 0.8, assumed densities of 5, 50 and 500 fragments/m³give 12000, 120000 and 1200000 fragments. None of those density values was measured from an Indus site in this study. The arithmetic demonstrates how a 100-fold density difference creates a 100-fold result. It supplies no calibrated estimate for any proposed window.

Harappa offers an empirical scale observation: Dales and Kenoyer's report describes almost one million potsherds over five seasons. It also records variable inscribed-object recovery by season and context. We do not divide those sherds by an invented excavated volume or transfer their density to villages, caves or submerged sites. Seal and inscription recovery cannot be estimated reliably from a generic pottery density or the number of settlements left to investigate. [R30]

### 7.2 Often the additional information is microscopic or relational

An additional pottery assemblage can clarify phase, manufacture, use and movement even when no spectacular object appears. Plant remains, crop weeds, animal bones, shells, manufacturing debris and residues can reveal activities that architecture alone leaves uncertain. Village ceramic research and Rohri/Ongar extraction studies demonstrate why a settlement-only search and an inscription-only measure of value would omit economic evidence. [R22,R23]

Bates and colleagues show information recovered through flotation and analysis of small fractions at northern Indus settlements. Their rice and weed observations illustrate a recoverable archive affected by collection procedures. A 2024 study at Kotada Bhadli recovered lipids from 11 vessel sherds but could not confidently connect the extracted proteins with specific food ingredients. Recent biomolecular work therefore justifies preserved samples, contamination controls and reporting failures alongside successes. It does not guarantee that each technique will work on every collection. [R24,R36]

The practical gain may come from relationships: whether a cemetery and settlement overlap in time, whether quarry activity supplied a particular workshop, whether a paleosol was inhabited before a flood, or whether a village retained occupation during urban contraction. More independent, well-contextualized observations could improve understanding of inscriptions' uses and distributions. They do not authenticate a proposed decipherment by themselves.

A 2024 date-seed study from Kech illustrates the need to audit new analytical cohorts. It classifies a selected 13 seed sample, while Table 2 lists 15 sample IDs and four conventional radiocarbon ages. The relation between those cohorts and a precise calendar phase requires reconciliation. Its reported morphometric result and preservation bias motivate testing modern-date-grove analogies; they cannot estimate the region's ancient crop composition. [R47]

## 8. Turning absence into a testable observation

### 8.1 The observation process

A map of recorded sites combines past occupation with preservation and investigation. For a particular period and region, a conceptual chain is: occupation existed; relevant remains survived; the location received survey coverage; a method detected evidence; material supported a period assignment; and the observation entered an accessible record. The joint probability can be factored into conditional probabilities along that chain. The factorization does not assume the stages are independent.

For a simplified synthetic population with a common effective recording probabilityq, the expected recorded count isN q. A world with 200 sites andq=0.5 and a world with 1000 sites andq=0.1 both give 100 expected records. The same count is compatible with different underlying settlement numbers. Repeating surface survey with independent per-visit detectionp would give 1-(1-p)^k after k visits, but deeply buried evidence can havepnear zero under that method. Changing technique or reaching the relevant surface is then more useful than accumulating similar negative visits.

These illustrations explain why we provide no upper bound on undiscovered sites. A lower bound on detection or preservation, or a validated sampling design, would be needed to obtain one. Actual detectability varies with size, crops, season, sediment depth, image resolution and period. A spatial model should represent occupation and observation separately, using survey effort and dated preservation strata rather than unvisited parcels as reliable negative examples.

### 8.2 A staged design with rejection rules

The first stage should reconcile identities and periods across published gazetteers, MAHSA, excavation reports and historical maps. Alias records retain the original witness and uncertainty. Multiple mounds and multiple phase memberships remain separate fields. New map symbols and machine detections enter a feature register before an archaeological site register. Existing survey teams should be consulted before a rediscovery claim or a new field plan.

The second stage samples contrasting landscape strata with declared effort. Candidate features, comparable map-negative surfaces, recent channel belts, stable interfluves, altered urban parcels and accessible shelters require explicit coverage records. Spatially separated evaluation cells reduce the risk of training and checking a predictor on neighboring manifestations of the same feature. The design must retain inaccessible and unobservable locations rather than code them as absences. Crop and seasonal visibility should be recorded and, where possible, revisited with a complementary method.

The third stage tests age and preservation. Cores and permitted small interventions locate relevant surfaces and assess stratigraphic integrity. Chronology should use material appropriate to the process: a sediment burial age, an occupation-associated short-lived specimen, or a carefully assessed marine-shell age addresses a different question. Blank samples, replicates and reference contexts accompany microfossil, residue and geophysical work. Saline or clay-rich ground may restrict particular geophysical methods; site-specific pilots should determine whether they add information.

The fourth stage expands excavation only when a question and maintenance plan justify it. A buried rural sequence, a craft chain or an exceptional preserved environmental context may deserve priority over a visually impressive but redundant architectural exposure. Records should link intervention area/volume, sieving and flotation procedures, retained samples, finds, dates and uncertainty. Publication of negative and undatable cases allows later teams to change a method without repeating an unexplained failure.

No specific total field cost or schedule is estimated here because deposit depth, ownership, permit requirements and analytical partners are not established. The appropriate first deliverable of collaboration would be a bounded pilot budget tied to a defined region and decision, rather than an excavation promise for all 21 windows.

## 9. Findings and limits

Excavation has remained selective for documented conservation, access, research and recording reasons. Current work continues at sites whose old labels can suggest inactivity. The historical excavation totals are useful evidence of a large investigation gap, while their differing scope and denominators prevent a precise present percentage.

Published surface and remote-sensing studies give measured dimensions for particular features and expose missing records. They support follow-up on small settlements and several sizeable mound proposals. Predictions about wholly buried cities require independently dated landforms, archaeological context and an observation model. Distance from a major city supplies a network hypothesis but cannot determine an unseen site's area or artifact yield.

The 21 windows translate plausible hiding mechanisms into comparisons that can fail. Alluvial, urban and agricultural settings have direct regional archaeological support; mountain/cave and offshore targets vary more in cultural and chronological support. Former coasts may survive inland, beneath sediment or under water. Local relative sea-level and sediment history determine which setting applies.

The proposed tests could add numerous ordinary artifacts, new environmental and craft evidence, better occupation sequences and more contextualized inscription observations. Their total quantity is presently uncalibrated. The immediate next research action is a concordance and sampling pilot in collaboration with existing regional projects, preserving the distinction between a feature detected, an occupation dated and an archaeological interpretation supported.
