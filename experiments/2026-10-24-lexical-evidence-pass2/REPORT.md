# Printed evidence for sixteen lexical cases

We completed the frozen sixteen-case review. Original dictionary entries support the intended lexical context in eight increment cases, contradict one generated increment analysis, separate the senses behind six boundary conflicts, and document a noun-label disagreement in the dry-wood case. We selected 35 Tamil Lexicon sense records and 23 distinct DEDR entries as evidence. These source decisions leave the previous extraction, exact candidate subset and scorer flags unchanged. [Results](results-v1.json), [case freeze](FREEZE-v1.json) and [validation](validation-v2.json) record the scope and checks.

## What the source entries decide

The two vowel-folding cases have clear source-specific second nouns. Winslow calls aṭampaṅkoṭi a creeper and āmaṇakkaṅkoṭṭai a castor seed. The relevant Lexicon senses are koṭi creeper and koṭṭai seed or kernel, distinct from the long-vowel kōṭi and kōṭṭai homographs. DEDR 65, query p.7, lists aṭampu and the related aṭappaṅ-koṭi form; DEDR 2050 and 2069 group the creeper and seed senses. These observations support the exact short-vowel alternatives already retained by the audit. They supply no general vowel-length equivalence.

The sampled exact candidates show why sense and origin need separate fields. Winslow defines kampaṅkatir as a millet ear and both varakañcōṟu and kampañcōṟu as boiled grain. The relevant Lexicon entries are kampu² millet, katir¹ sense 5 ear of grain, varaku, and cōṟu¹ boiled rice. DEDR 1194, 1242, 2897 and 5260 group these senses. The original DEDR entries also retain Sanskrit comparisons for the millet words. A Dravidian comparative entry therefore does not by itself certify a word as an unborrowed native picture noun.

For pīrkkaṅkūṭu, the Lexicon prints the boundary and names pīrkku in the etymology, query p.2736. Its Cilappatikāram 3,26 commentary citation is preserved as a printed source locator; we did not inspect that commentary or establish its date. DEDR 4224 supplies the gourd forms. In teṅkantīvu, DEDR 3408 prints both teṅku and teṅkam for the coconut tree. The exact DEDR match tīvu in entry 3268 means sweetness. The island sense needed by Winslow instead has the Lexicon etymology dvīpa, query p.1951. Form-only DEDR membership would attach the wrong sense to that second noun.

One generated candidate fails its source context. Winslow defines pattaṅkam as ten parts. Lexicon pattu¹, query p.2466, is ten, while aṅkam¹, query p.20, is a limb or part with Sanskrit aṅga. The candidate pattaṅ plus kam has no matching part sense in the inspected kam entries. This is a concrete negative control for the increment generator. The existing patt plus aṅkam analysis fits the printed sense; we record that comparison without submitting a new segmentation or changing its flags.

All six boundary-conflict cases separate homographs or senses. The darkness entry karuvari differs from karu-vari dark lines in an eye. The heat sense of vēṉil differs from decoration or beauty, and the Lexicon prints a romanisation discrepancy that remains recorded. Caṅkaniti treasure differs from its plant homograph; caṇṭivāḷam redemption money differs from leg fetters. Aka-vilai grain price differs from aka-v-ilai inner petal. Mā-tavam great penance differs from mātavam spring. Their original labels, etymologies and citations are retained in the local review. The money entry explicitly cites Winslow; these two dictionary witnesses are dependent for that definition.

The mā-tavam case also retains a competing etymological tradition. Winslow derives its starred mā adjective from Sanskrit Mahat, and the Lexicon gives mahat plus tapas for the compound. DEDR 4786 groups mā great with Dravidian forms. We preserve both sources rather than converting the shared written form into a single origin decision.

The dry-wood case has positive material-noun evidence despite the frozen generator's exclusion. Winslow labels cettal a verbal noun but gives dried-fruit and dried-vegetable senses; its compound explicitly names dry murukku wood. The Lexicon labels cettal a noun, query p.1585. DEDR 2770 groups the material sense separately from its dying entry, and DEDR 4981 groups tree names. The flower case likewise has an explicit botanical context. These entries supply a specialist review target for the noun policy. Botanical species, ancestry and the scope of the increment remain qualified.

## Additional grammatical evidence

Eva Wilden's [Grammar of Old Tamil for Students](https://gokulam.net/mirrors/Eva_files/readers/Grammar_of_Classical_Tamil_for_Students.pdf), first edition with a December 2016 preface, gives consonant sandhi on printed pp.17–18, PDF pp.27–28. We inspected both rendered pages. It describes assimilation of final m before k, c and t, with examples, and also describes competing gemination or deletion environments. This supports the nasal-assimilation component of the candidate rule. The inspected passage does not establish a general final-u-to-am substitution. The acquired copy is a mirror of the published grammar, with no verified reuse licence.

E. Annamalai's institutional [“13. Sandhi”](https://tamilcourse.uchicago.edu/node/121), copyright 2017, gives compound examples with added material, including tamarind fruit, and discusses several classes of sandhi. It explicitly leaves detailed compound rules and exceptions outside that lesson. This is useful grammatical context, with limited coverage of the proposed increment distribution.

The three frozen web searches produced these accessible sources and one bounded access failure. The Tamil Digital Library candidate scan reported 81,385,350 bytes, exceeding our 50 MB limit, and was not downloaded. The web reader could not open the Wilden mirror, but direct retrieval succeeded and produced a 1,782,051-byte PDF. The [source manifest](source-manifest-v2.json) keeps both outcomes. We did not inspect the oversized grammar and make no claim about its contents.

## Scope and deciding evidence

All sixteen Winslow blocks bind to their original entries. The source packets contain 116 Lexicon case records and 76 DEDR case records gathered during exact lookup; the selected evidence comprises 35 sense references and 26 case references to 23 distinct DEDR entries. Those counts measure source records, not newly discovered words or independent attestations. DEDR is an additional comparative source and may draw on the same lexicographical tradition. Printed literary citations remain unverified as earliest attestations. The author has no specialist Tamil credentials.

Five author checks pass for the frozen selection and inputs, source hashes and bindings, review accounting, wrong-sense regressions, and grammar bytes and coordinates. The first packet builder stopped on repeated DEDR entry 2054; its repair retains every original same-number witness. A later packet version includes the pre-existing selected members among its source queries. The first validation run had a missing Counter import; both the failure and corrected version are preserved. Full sources, rendered pages, packets and judgments remain physically on D:. No substantive effect or real confirmation was run.

The strongest supported reading is that the outstanding lexical problem is already sense-specific: several compounds have clear printed contexts, one increment candidate is contradicted by its context, and nominal labels and etymological traditions conflict in identifiable entries. A Dravidianist's review of these sixteen records, followed by direct checking of the cited literary and grammatical passages, would decide which source-qualified rules and noun senses can enter a later calibrated comparison.
