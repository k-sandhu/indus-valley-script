# Documentary contexts and dates in six towns

We can now connect named inscriptions and documentary objects to manufacture, house rooms, floor reuse, sealing contacts and a public gateway. These are useful archaeological joins. None yet supplies the independently dated construction-to-documentary-use sequence and matched earlier/later episode needed to test C0018 or C0019. The result is an eligibility audit of a fixed source frame, not a confirmation failure for either conjecture.

The audit contains 32 authored context assessments and 78 date-evidence entries for Farmana, Kanmer, Banawali, Harappa, Dholavira and Lothal. Assessment units overlap: a site cohort and an individual item from that cohort are separate assessments, not additional objects. Seventy-six date entries contain a published radiocarbon determination or TL result; the two Kanmer rice entries preserve calibrated ages from a paragraph that omits their raw determinations. Laboratory records, specimens, use events and independently dated repairs have separate denominators. We have not read or received the architectural agents' new persistence outcomes. The prior town comparison and several sources are familiar, so this is an exploratory audit with a recorded separation of context coding from the new architectural result.

Harappa supplies the best manufacturing context. Kenoyer's *Steatite and Faience Manufacturing at Harappa: New Evidence from Mound E Excavations 2000-2001* describes manufacturing material in the fill of a bounded Trench 54 courtyard. Figure 2 labels workshop[601], wall void[603], brick wall[604], mud-brick stairs[605], wall[606] and platform[613]. Figure 3 identifies five incised steatite tablets, two blanks and eight moulded faience tablets. Unfired and broken pieces, sawn steatite, faience waste and firing-canister fragments support local production. We preserve the 15 illustration units and their accession/lot strings. The surface tablet H2000-4483/2342-01 illustrated separately in Figure 4 stays outside that bounded set. Production debris does not identify the subsequent recipients or their offices, and ceramic Period 3B association does not independently date a workshop construction or repair. See `HAR-TR54-MANUFACTURE` and `HAR-TR54-DOWNSLOPE` in `context-joins.json`; source indices 1–3 and 7–10.

Harappa also has positive room and floor associations. Meadow and Kenoyer's *Excavations at Harappa 1994-1995: New Perspectives on the Indus Script, Craft Activities, and City Organization* reports H95-2598/4666-19 from Trench 10E floor deposits in a room with a hearth, bin and grindstone. H95-2491/4690-01 is described in the prose as from a courtyard floor, while Figure 7.6 retains the broader label floor/fill. H95-2419/4609-01 is an upper-fill seal. We preserve those distinctions. In Trench 9, the inscribed black-slipped jar H95-2661/6640-501 was sunk into a floor and reused as a sump; two pre-firing and two post-firing sets of marks can precede that final installation. A nearby dated hearth does not date each mark or the installation. The single apparently undisturbed above-floor Trench 19 tablet is another positive room lead, but its registration is unresolved within the passage read. These joins give specific targets for excavation-record recovery rather than a claim that all Harappa tablets lack architectural contexts. Locators: printed pp. 148,150–151,154 and 161; PDF indices 9,11–12,15 and 22.

The 22 incised tablets H2218–H2239 remain a strong example of why a close group can mislead an institutional reconstruction. In *The 'Tiny Steatite Seals' (Incised Steatite Tablets) of Harappa: Some Observations on Their Context and Dating*, 16 are reconstructed as a clump, two nearby and four scattered. The deposit is secondary and postdates Wall 296 construction. Its original manufacturing and use locations remain unknown. The source plan preserves approximate finds near Walls 250 and 296; it does not turn a dump into an office operating there. A nearby seal, H96-2796/6876-01, is from a separate street deposit. Shared terminal signs do not supply a shared use event. Source author-PDF pp. 7,12–15, indices 7,12–15. Actual dates in that article come from Mounds AB and F, about half a kilometre from the Mound E excavations, and are retained as rejected date transfers.

A further Harappa candidate shows where a plausible chain breaks. Table 1 in the 1994–1995 excavation report gives A-8047/AA-16461, A-8048 and A-8049 from hearths below Wall 15, and the text infers that the wall was built after about 2470 BC. The Trench 22 narrative places two faience tablets inside the perimeter wall, but Figure 11.4/.6 labels the registered objects H94-2274/5332-01 and H94-2283/5332-02 as Trench 7/8,wash. We preserve the conflict and the underlying-wall dates rather than choosing the location that would complete an association. A-8049 is source-flagged somewhat aberrant and remains in the record. Locators: printed pp. 143–144,147 and 160; indices 4–5,8 and 21.

Banawali has a useful house-level association. Bisht's *Excavations at Banawali: 1974-77*, p. 117, reports a horned-tiger inscribed seal in a mud-brick-paved sitting room. The same house, referenced in Plates 10.11a–b, yielded more than one seal, weights and large jars half embedded in floors. The description lacks a registration, exact subphase and independently related sample. We therefore keep B17/BNL-9201 as an unverified alias candidate. The excavator's rich-merchant interpretation derives from this assemblage and cannot independently validate a merchant interpretation of its writing. *Indian Archaeology 1986-87—A Review*, p. 33, reports seals/sealings and at least three gate/drain rebuilding phases; it does not join a named object to a specific rebuilding. No relevant laboratory determination was located in this bounded Banawali frame.

Farmana identifies four steatite seals and two impressed clay objects. S1 is reported with Structure 1; S2 came from a layer sealing Phase 4 structures, S3 occurs in a layer enclosing Phase 5 structures and S4 in a layer sealing Phase 5 structures. The report itself states that none of the seals was found in a specific context allowing a structural association. We retain both the proximity/phase statements and that explicit qualification. S5 carries reed/bamboo, cloth and finger-contact evidence; S6 has a horizontal suspension perforation and is described as a pendant. Counting both as proven closures would change the functional denominator. Tables 7.2–7.3, p. 371; Figure 7.18 and discussion, pp. 384–385; PDF indices 384,397–398.

The Farmana dating appendix gives 26 AMS results: 21 Harappan, four Gupta and one Late Medieval. Carbonized wood is the material for 25 samples; PLD-15057 is charred material. The source uses OxCal4.1 and IntCal09, with multiple calibrated components preserved here. PLD-12422, sample 08-001, names Trench 1E1/Structure 3N yet dates 1390±20 BP, calibrated AD 616–666 at 95.4%. It defeats an apparent Harappan named-structure date. PLD-12425 shares the Trench 1C1/1C2 vicinity and Phase 5 with S6, but a shared depositional unit and installation relation are not reported. Trench 3 dates belong to Phases 2–3, while S5 is Phase 4. The section/phase figure cannot supply those missing physical links. Tables 13.1–13.2 and Figure 13.1, pp. 832–835; indices 845–848.

Kanmer preserves three same-stamp terracotta impressions with eccentric perforations. The original 2011 article's identity-card analogy is a proposal about use. An indexed Chapter 8 lead reports IIb/layers 8–9 and a room, but the held full article does not provide the registered object-to-room/date crosswalk. The held Chapter 8 viewer shell is not the chapter, and the inherited file named kanmer2013.pdf is publisher HTML rather than the requested article. Both access limits are recorded. We do not promote the indexed room claim into an adjudicated original-use join.

The genuine Kanmer publications provide 16 AMS results and nine conventional wood-charcoal results; repeating a lab ID in two papers adds no independent determination. They also preserve early conventional results and source disagreements, including the layer/calibrated endpoints for BS-2619. Most decisively, Pokharia and colleagues' *Archaeobotany and archaeology at Kanmer, a Harappan site in Kachchh, Gujarat: evidence for adaptation in response to climatic variability*, p. 1836, reports rice PLD-16351 and PLD-16353 with fourth–fifth-century AD calibrated ages despite Mature/Late field associations. The authors propose percolation or reworking. These grains demonstrate that cultural-phase membership alone cannot date an object or construction. The sample-wise tables on pp. 1836–1837 preserve the available grid/layer/depth and determination evidence; none supplies a seal-to-repair chain.

At Dholavira, we apply computable literal-label gates to all 225 previously extracted context-table rows. The derivative keeps every original cell, serial, page and extraction coordinate on D:. It contains 225 unique accession strings, not an adjudicated total of physical objects. Three rows explicitly name a room: serial 83/accession 53254, Castle, Stage IV; serial 140/accession 26514, “Midle Town” as printed, Stage V; and serial 211/accession 49846, Citadel, Stage VI. Grid/depth labels bound those leads without identifying an operating floor or a specific building episode. Serial 140's inscription cell is blank, while the catalogue description says six signs; neither is silently substituted for the other. Of 225 rows,162 have an unqualified stage after whitespace-only normalization,16 contain pit labels, two reservoir labels and 23 explicit secondary/disturbed terms. These flags can overlap and do not measure archaeological absence. Context table pp. 247–260, PDF indices 263 onward; individual row locators are local.

The ten-sign gate panel is the clearest named public-architecture inscription in the fixed frame. Bisht reports fallen inlays on the floor of the western chamber of the Castle north gate and reconstructs a wooden signboard above the passage. This joins an inscription to a gate chamber, while original mounting and emplacement date remain reconstructed or unresolved. The later narrowing of a 3.5 m opening does not date the signboard. The four-sign limestone fragment from an underground Bailey chamber is secondary. The chronology chapter reports DHR93/TL/4 at 2809±461 BCE for Stage III and DHR93/TL/8 at 2302±480 BCE for Stage II; the broad errors and unavailable sample-to-feature relationships prevent their use as gate-panel or room-repair dates. Gate inscription pp. 228–230, indices 244–246; chronology pp. 104–105, indices 120–121; narrowing pp. 586–587, indices 602–603.

Lothal provides physical contact evidence for movable containers and immovable fastenings, plus a concentrated warehouse-associated deposit. L206 is illustrated in a peg/door-fastening model; L164 has cord/wood-related contacts and a tentative box/support reconstruction. Those contacts address application, while their later find deposits do not identify a named recipient or a labour obligation. The passage 41I–41J concentration has competing archive-discard and fire/collapse formation histories. Rao's 65, the later 69 of 90 and Frenez–Tosi's 93 retain different source populations.

We manually transcribed the provenance labels of all 93 rows L124–L216 in Frenez and Tosi's *The Lothal Sealings: Records from an Indus Civilization Town at the Eastern End of the Maritime Trade Circuits across the Arabian Sea*, Table 1, pp. 93–94, indices 30–31. The result is W=35, W1=18, W2=11, O=10 and ?=19. The prose on pp. 71–72 gives W=35, W1=18, W2=11, O=11 and ?=17, whose category sum is 92. We preserve this discrepancy and its unreviewed manual-transcription status. W1 locates material by shared stamp identity; including it in an independent stamp-similarity/location test would make the association circular. W2 names the broad SRG3 trench without a demonstrated warehouse-room deposit. The two table pages and bounded prose witness are available locally for an independent recount.

Kusumgar, Lal and Sarna's *Radiocarbon Dating: Results*, pp. 148–149, provides six actual Lothal charcoal determinations with trench, locus, depth and deposit description. TF-23 is from a mud-wall posthole above Phase IV flood debris; TF-22 is from an ashy layer sealing Phase IIIB flood debris and a wall. These are useful partial sample-to-feature chains. None is from the SRG3 warehouse sealing deposit. The paper's parenthesized ages use a different half-life and are not modern calibrated intervals. Rootlets, waterlogging, old wood and the sample-to-event relation remain explicit limits rather than reasons to transfer a nearby date to the sealing concentration.

The strongest reading supported by these joins is that writing and sealing participated in several spatially distinct activities: specialist manufacture, house-level handling, container or door fastening, reuse and public display. Their distribution makes a single universal institutional function less plausible, but the present record does not establish the political scale of coordination or distinguish shared-work obligations from goods allocation. The deciding test requires registered objects on demonstrable operational surfaces or closures, short-lived independently dated samples that bracket a named construction or repair, and matched earlier/later contexts with comparable recovery. We should first seek that complete chain for Harappa's named floor/room seals and Trench 54 courtyard, Banawali's sitting room and Dholavira's explicitly named rooms; Lothal additionally requires an independent resolution of its deposit formation and provenance denominator. A frozen comparison of documentary change against independently coded repair episodes could then decide whether infrastructure continuity follows household, district or wider coordination, and whether the surviving documents track work obligations or movable goods.
