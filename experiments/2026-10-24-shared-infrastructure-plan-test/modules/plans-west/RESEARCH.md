# Shared infrastructure in the western plan sources

We audited 23 original plan, section and photograph panels for Harappa, Dholavira and Lothal. They show repeated work on shared defences, drainage and circulation, together with clogged drains, altered gate connections and street encroachment. Seven local dimensions can be read from source scales, and one Harappa pair can be registered on the excavation grid. None supplies the complete, comparable route and internal-wall inventories needed to estimate the proposed persistence difference. The P1 effect is therefore unknown and the eligible denominator is zero in this module.

The machine-readable record contains 14 source-use records, 23 panel records, 15 source-defined cases, seven dimension readings and one registration. The cases include a source-eligibility audit and overlapping views of the same architectural areas; they are not 15 independent repair events. Twelve annotated boards and a complete crop gallery remain local on D. [visual-assets.json](visual-assets.json) gives each selected board's path, hash, caption and exact source locator.

## Harappa: retained position and changed access

Area C supplies a genuine location comparison. Kenoyer's *Urban Process in the Indus Tradition: A Preliminary Model from Harappa* shows Period 2 cart-track depressions in natural soil on p.49, Fig.4.12, and the upper street and house exposure on p.54, Fig.4.16. The text places the Period 3 street directly above the earlier route and reports a 5 m width on p.50. We fit each drawing to six excavation-grid crosses, then checked two unused crosses on each image. No street, drain or wall was used as a registration control. Grid-fit root mean square errors are 0.0055 m and 0.0157 m; maximum errors at the held-out crosses are 0.0182 m and 0.0368 m. These describe the fitted drawing coordinates, with original survey and scan error still unknown. The small common geometric window is 1.7 by 4.3 m at E2355.30–2357.00, N1015.40–1019.70.

Board PW-V01 transfers the traced early depressions onto the upper plan using that grid fit. The upper drawing itself labels encroachment paving and a drain. This establishes a location relationship and later change within the corridor. The early exposure supplies no comparable internal-wall population, and the upper plan combines exposed levels. PW-E01 therefore remains descriptive. The six traced line segments are parts of one cart-track set, not six independent episodes.

The Area C sequence includes interruption and renewal. The report describes clogged drains, sewage in the street and decay of a wall and gateway before the Period 3B rebuilding. The rebuilt wall was traced for 73 m, with widths from 5.4 to 11 m and unresolved repairs or tower explanations for its broader parts. During Period 3C, brick paving and drains encroached on the formerly 5 m street. Brick robbing affects its upper levels. These observations are PW-E02 and PW-E03, using pp.52–56, Figs.4.14–4.16. One overall plan and several section faces cannot isolate every building stage as a separate complete exposure.

Mound ET makes the relation between service and access particularly clear. In Meadow and Kenoyer's *Excavations at Harappa 1994-1995: New Perspectives on the Indus Script, Craft Activities, and City Organization*, pp.143–146, the original gateway and three successive western drains precede a central corbelled drain. The last western drain clogged with silty refuse. The new drain partly blocked the older gateway, while an east-west street crossed above the culvert and linked parallel streets along the channel. Brick bonding and construction breaks support three building stages. Board PW-V02 preserves this composite drawing and the altered connections. Drainage renewal and gate access must be evaluated as distinct outcomes within one setting.

The Mound ET enclosure has an earlier chronological bound, but the late drain repair does not inherit it. Table1, pp.142–143, reports A-8047 at 3965±55 bp and A-8048 at 3975+110/−105 bp from hearth deposits below wall [15]. Their reported one-sigma calibrated BC entries are 2558 (2465) 2404 and 2589 (2467) 2320. The source infers construction sometime after ca.2470 B.C. A-8049, from a related below-wall context, gives 3785+145/−140 bp and 2457 (2195, 2152, 2149) 1976 calibrated BC, with the source's single-asterisk warning. PW-E04 preserves all three, their laboratory and sample identities, and the source's calibration convention. This is a terminus post quem for initial enclosure with conflicting determinations, not a direct date of a late 3C repair.

Farther north, Fig.6 on p.153 places curtain wall [296], perimeter [250], streets and Room 92 in plan and section. Wall [296] followed a prepared surface; later street and dumped deposits buried the walls. A late 3C drain cut the earlier Room 92 block. The authors propose a connection to the gateway drain farther south; the intervening connection is unverified. Room hearth dates concern occupation and cannot directly date wall [296] or that channel cut. PW-E06 and board PW-V12 retain the local sequence and the proposed connection separately.

The new HARP images improve source access without creating a valid survival denominator. Trench54's overview combines Periods 2, 3A, 3B and 3C, with wall voids and robbed brickwork. Its detail and west section clarify masonry and loss. The two Trench39 drawings have different extents; one shows a later drain as a reference, while the later image combines 3B drain [8], 3C wall [50] and later fragments. Trench11 shows several 3C levels, and 1999/2001 label excavation seasons. Board PW-V03 and PW-E07 make these source limitations visible.

## Dholavira: rebuilding the boundary, narrowing the approach

Bisht's *Excavations at Dholavira (1989-90 to 2004-2005)* supplies a long construction sequence in the Castle's southern defence. The explicitly schematic Fig.5.1, p.96, distinguishes stages and unexcavated regions. Source prose on pp.95–99 describes an 11 m basal wall, a 2.8 m inward addition in Stage II and a 4.5 m addition in Stage III. Board PW-V06 displays the actual section. The evidence supports successive investment in the same defence; a single section cannot establish a whole-enclosure survival rate.

The East Gate combines surviving geometry with blockages. Fig.6.9, p.116, labels later blocking at both ends of its passage. Our selected interior clear section reads 2.52 m, but the plan does not assign those two blockings to exact stages. The nearby Stage V buildings and Stage VI external steps come from prose on pp.115–116; they cannot be used to date every line in the composite. Board PW-V04 shows the measured line and both source labels. Continued clearance inside the gate does not establish open passage through it.

The North Gate source also combines stages. Fig.6.19, p.121, marks earlier wall plaster and Late Harappan walls. North points down. The report describes gate use from Stage III to VI and narrowing of a 9 m ceremonial approach in Stage V or VI on p.122. The central passage reads about 2.70 m, with a broad 2.24–3.22 m reading range because the source crop is only 419 by 526 pixels. Board PW-V05 keeps the approach change and phase ambiguity visible. The plan does not locate an original display mounting or establish a primary-use deposit relationship for that display.

The Lower Town supplies a named counterexample whose missing plan matters. On p.137, the report describes a Stage V wall encroaching on a formerly 5.25 m north-south street in trench 15x24. It gives no later clear width. Most Lower Town excavation reached only about 1.5 m and one or two phases; three named trenches reached natural soil. Fig.6.26, p.133, is an oblique street photograph with no surveyed link to trench 15x24. Board PW-V07 therefore shows the photograph and the textual observation without marking a particular wall as the encroachment. A phase-keyed plan of that trench would resolve the local width and connectivity change.

The well-and-tank plan on p.144, Fig.6.32, preserves another shared system with unresolved repair dates. It connects the well and two tanks through solid and dashed channels; north again points down. The report places the well in use by Stage IV and into VI, and describes rebuilt access steps as the adjacent broadway rose. Its first construction and the individual channel repairs remain unresolved. PW-E12 and board PW-V08 record the arrangement, source diameters, our drawing reading and those missing stages together.

## Lothal: a usable Phase IV plan inside an incomplete volume

Rao's *Lothal: A Harappan Port Town (1955-62), Volume I* reports the town being raised and rebuilt after floods. Pages 85–87 describe Phases II and III retaining block and house layouts, with encroachment developing in IV. The nine streets and twelve lanes are reported exposed features, with no complete contemporaneous denominator. Pages 86–87 reverse the compass directions assigned to Streets 1 and 6; we preserve the conflict without selecting a bearing. Phase I layout remains poorly recovered.

The held scan lacks the original plate volume, including the whole street-network plate XXXVIII. This does not make every plan unavailable. Fig.17, p.109, is a usable Phase IV Block B plan with houses 171.IVa and 172.IVa, Streets 2/3, a passage, entrances and household drainage. Our passage and northern entrance readings are 1.12 m and 2.21 m. Board PW-V09 shows those exact transects. An earlier matched block plan is still required to measure narrowing through rebuilding.

Fig.16, p.101, illustrates public drain 15A using an isometric view, section and plan. Its damaged masonry and sockets are visible; the wooden-screen function is the source's interpretation. We take no cross-projection length. Fig.19, p.124, is explicitly an isometric reconstruction of the structure called a dock. The text on pp.125–126 orders an eastern inlet and western drain closure after Phase IIIb damage. Boards PW-V10 and PW-V11 preserve these observations and drawing types. They support ordered hydraulic change without supplying two comparable orthogonal plans or settling the basin's function.

We followed direct acquisition routes for the missing plans. The first Internet Archive creator query returned no candidates because the uploader metadata did not use Rao as expected. A broader title query returned 32 items, including the same incomplete original Volume I and Volume II, but no complete original plate volume was acquired. The existing page-image scan remains the inspected source. Its alternate OCR text is corrupt and was not used to infer architecture.

## Strongest reading and deciding test

These western sources support repeated investment in shared settings while access arrangements changed. The strongest examples are the renewed Harappa drains and crossing street within an older gate complex, Dholavira's additions to the same defence, and Lothal's reported hydraulic rerouting. Clogged drains, blocked gate ends and encroached streets show that continuity must be defined as a particular service or connection through a particular episode. Architectural persistence alone cannot select the mechanism that maintained it.

The deciding test needs complete phase-keyed excavation windows containing both shared routes and internal partitions, with source-defined matches, comparable exposure and preservation, and independently dated repairs. Harappa Area C is the immediate documentary target: obtain separate full street levels and the associated internal walls on the same survey grid. Recover the Lothal plates and the Dholavira trench 15x24 phase plans as explicit counterexample targets. Freeze every candidate feature before counting retained, changed, removed and unobservable lengths. If shared routes do not exceed partition persistence by the registered 0.25 contrast across the required independent episodes, the architectural prediction fails. A mechanism involving documentary coordination additionally needs object-to-deposit-to-part-to-episode joins; none is supplied by these geometric readings alone.
