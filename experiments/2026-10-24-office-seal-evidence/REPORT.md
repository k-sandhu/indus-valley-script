# The elephant sealings have a source-linked dispute over what they closed

We recovered an earlier excavation description of L-164 that differs from a later tentative wooden-box interpretation. Rao describes overlapping cords on a flat underside and interprets the support as a jar with a flat stopper. Frenez and Tosi place the same object in a twelve-member elephant-sealing group whose strings they suggest closed polished wooden boxes. The shared physical observation is a cord-fastened flat surface. Identifying that surface as a box remains an interpretation.

This source-work run follows C-0004 and Q-0014 without repeating the earlier comparison of L-161–170 with M-895. We read the repository's orient, add-data and retrospective skills and froze [PROTOCOL.md](PROTOCOL.md) before extraction. The four source witnesses, their hashes and page coverage are in [source-manifest-v1.json](source-manifest-v1.json). Detailed observations, catalogue joins, OCR and images remain in the D: directory named there. [aggregate-v1.json](aggregate-v1.json) describes the versioned ledger: 93 source assertions, twelve elephant-group object aliases and four control-object aliases. These are source assertions, not 93 independent archaeological observations.

## The decisive object link

Rao's *Lothal: A Harappan Port Town (1955–62)*, Volume II (1985), printed p. 319 / PDF 70, names excavation object 1864, reports an elephant and Indus signs on its obverse, and describes overlapping cords on its flat underside. His flat-stoppered-jar interpretation follows that description. *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions 1: Collections in India* (1987), printed p. 373 / PDF 409, identifies L-164 with excavation 1864 and museum registration LTH SRG 1232. We inspected the rendered catalogue row and the original excavation page, preserving the coordinates and identifier relation in the local ledger.

Frenez and Tosi's *The Lothal Sealings: Records from an Indus Civilization Town at the Eastern End of the Maritime Trade Circuits across the Arabian Sea* (2005), printed pp. 78–79 / PDF 16–17, puts L-161–172 under Type 5, “Wooden Surfaces (Boxes?)”. It assigns all twelve impressions to one elephant seal with four signs and describes knotted strings against finely treated wooden surfaces. The authors say the strings seem to have closed boxes, explicitly call the boxes’ shape and size conjectural, and suggest small caskets. They leave open whether the strings were packaging or part of a fastening device. This scope matters: the later box proposal is a tentative published group interpretation, while Rao supplies an individually numbered description for L-164. The ledger retains both. [modal-correction-v2.json](modal-correction-v2.json) points to a corrected local ledger that makes the tentative wording explicit; the original ledger and results remain preserved.

The elephant attribution now has an earlier excavation witness. On printed p. 321 / PDF 72, Rao describes twelve single-impression terracotta sealings with a running elephant below four signs. This corroborates the later cohort description, though that passage does not enumerate its members. The specific 1864 description supplies the stronger individual link. It gives no new determination of M-895's animal. The previous disagreement between unicorn and unidentifiable remains open for specialist inspection.

## Related impressions give a useful control

The original report describes repeated combinations of dies on other sealings. CISI1 p. 373 joins L-189 to 1845, L-190 to 1870 and L-193 to 1888. Rao p. 321 says 1845 carries four impressions, 1870 three, and two impressions occur on both. On p. 322 / PDF 73, he reports two impressions on 1888 repeated on 1845, together with bamboo-mat and cord traces on 1888's reverse. These source-qualified relations document a local repeated-die network on used clay closures. We have not remeasured the dies or assigned their users to offices.

L-146 provides a control for how descriptions of attachment materials change. CISI1 joins it to 13191. Rao pp. 319 and 322 describes woven fabric, reeds or hay and cords. Frenez and Tosi p. 78 describes two small canes tied with string under fine fabric. Both accounts identify a composite attachment. Their descriptions illustrate why material traces, support geometry and proposed container type need separate fields.

## The deposit is less precise than a single warehouse event

Volume I (1979), printed p. 113 / PDF 124, reports 65 sealings in successive layers above burnt bricks at the southern end of the passage between bases 41i and 41j, with ash, burnt reeds and a charred log. Volume II p. 319 reports 69 of 90 sealings in a three-foot-square area at the southern end of the easternmost passage. We preserve both counts and scopes. The inspected pages do not establish whether their inventories are identical.

The accounts also differ over the fire. Volume I uses the charred log and deliberately blocked passage to suggest that the fire was not accidental. Volume II describes accidental burning when the building caught fire. The find observations and these causal interpretations remain separate. Neither account supplies individual placement within those successive layers for every elephant sealing. The later cohort's warehouse assignment therefore supplies a published context association, with the original deposit narrative attached at group level.

Rao p. 321 explains nail marks as surplus clay pressed back after stamping. Frenez and Tosi p. 79 proposes administrative tallies and discusses several possible referents. This disagreement is retained. It prevents nail marks from becoming an observed quantity label for the office conjecture.

## Access, checks and remaining test

We reused the four preserved local PDFs; no source was downloaded. The Rao scans lack useful body text layers, so searches returned no relevant pages. We then followed the later chapter's exact citations and used bounded OCR. Volume I coverage was seven body pages and five initial pages; Volume II was nine body pages and five initial pages. The inspected list, extracted candidate pages and rendered-page hashes are preserved. OCR is a navigation aid; we checked the decisive pp. 319, 321 and 113 and CISI1 p. 373 against page images. We did not inspect reserved confirmation material. No functional reading or credence change is submitted, so this contribution adds source evidence without a new bench claim.

[validation-v1.json](validation-v1.json) records 50 passing author checks and no failures for source hashes, local artifact hashes, ledger structure, cohort coverage and preservation of the conflicting assertions. The check script refuses to overwrite its output. This is submitted author verification. The missing observations are calibrated reverse and side views of L-164 and the elephant cohort, an object-by-object excavation-layer join, and a specialist reading of M-895's remaining motif.

The boldest reading supported here is that the recurring elephant legend marked a repeated cord-fastening operation at Lothal. A specific wooden-box function is disputed, and an office shared across cities remains C-0004's conjecture. The deciding physical test is to trace each sealing's underside and edge junction in calibrated views: a vessel rim or stopper contact would support Rao's reconstruction; continuous polished planar faces meeting at a box corner would support the later reconstruction. A specialist comparison with M-895 would then test whether the same legend crossed a change in emblem as well as a change in die.
