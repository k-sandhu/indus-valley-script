# Backward chronology of South Asian writing and marks

The recovered sources support several dated regional sequences, but they do not supply a diagnostic sign sequence connecting late Indus material to early historic writing. The inventory contains **42 rows**, **18 sources** and **11 attributed continuity or origin claims**. **19 rows** have an additive calendar interval overlapping 3500–200 BCE: **2 historical endpoints (A), 9 excavated phase links (B), and 8 weaker or unresolved associations (C)**. Twenty rows have no selected calendar interval. These counts are in [summary.json](run-2026-10-07-03/summary.json), fields `inventory_rows`, `window_dating_grades` and `rows_without_selected_calendar_interval`.

The longest internal gap changes with the treatment of old absolute chronologies. All A+B phase brackets leave two empty century bins, 800–700 and 400–300 BCE. Restricting A+B to high/moderate calendar strength leaves **six consecutive empty bins, 1300–700 BCE**. This measures missing coverage in the assembled record. The original Rangpur chronology fills much of that interval by estimating elapsed centuries from deposit thickness; it therefore receives a low calendar-strength rating. The definitions were recorded before computation in [PROTOCOL.md](PROTOCOL.md); the three series and every empty run appear in [summary.json](run-2026-10-07-03/summary.json), field `gaps`.

## What the earlier periods contribute

Harappa supplies the clearest local early sequence. Kenoyer reports **13 post-firing and 4 pre-firing Ravi sherds**, followed by **95 marked Kot Diji sherds from unmixed contexts**. The 2010 phase table places Ravi approximately 3700–2800 BCE, Kot Diji 2800–2600, and the Mature phase 2600–1900. The earlier 2006 Ravi bracket starts at 3300 BCE; both assessments remain visible. The 17 sherds are an excavation snapshot, and their phase attribution does not date each sherd within that long interval. See rows `harappa-ravi`, `harappa-kot-diji` and `harappa-mature` in [evidence-table.json](evidence-table.json), and the [2006 paper](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer2006_The%20Origin%2C%20Context%20and%20Function%20of%20the%20Indus%20Sc.pdf), Ravi/Kot Diji sections, and [2010 chapter](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/KenoyerMeadow%202010%20Inscribed%20Objects%20from%20Harappa.pdf), PDF p. 2, Table 1.

After the urban phase, the evidence changes by object class and region. Kenoyer describes geometric/floral button seals in late contexts and few possible late graffiti at Harappa. His Pirak comparison illustrates **two seals**, assigned to Period II and IIIA within an approximately 1800–1300 BCE bracket. These examples permit a test of geometric motif persistence. Their stepped-cross forms have no recovered dated sequence of linguistic combinations. Cemetery H and Jhukar remain separate rows, with tentative graffiti and geometric seal evidence respectively. See `harappa-late`, `jhukar`, `pirak` and `mehrgarh-button-seals`; [Kenoyer's seal overview](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer2009%20Indus%20Seals_0.pdf), printed pp. 10–11 and figs. 2–3.

Daimabad provides **six Phase II inscriptions: two terracotta seals and four potsherds**, plus **one Phase III inscribed sherd**. Sali supplies trenches, layers and house associations. His conventional Phase II bracket is 2000–1800 BCE, while the printed MASCA-corrected alternatives span 2440/2330 to 2140/2120 BCE. Phase III is conventionally 1800–1600, with corrected alternatives 2140/2120–1800. The calculation uses the envelopes of the corrected alternatives, labels their calendar strength low, and keeps the conventional brackets as unselected assessments. Modern recalibration and a residuality audit would determine how these examples constrain survival after the urban endpoint. An approximate dozen additional graffiti are excluded from additive counts because overlap with the inscribed sherds is unresolved. See `daimabad-II`, `daimabad-III` and `daimabad-II-graffiti`; [Sali 1986](https://asi.nic.in/admin/publications/ePublicationReportsDetails/download/49), pp. 206–211, 248 and 504–506.

The original Rangpur report provides a larger count than the later shorthand description recovered in the Tamil Nadu volume: **26 figurative, 188 linear and 8 compound-bearing sherds, totaling 222**. Most are assigned to IIC/III, with fewer in IIA/IIB. Rao's phase estimates extend from 2000 to 800 BCE, but pp. 25–28 infer durations partly from the thickness of occupation deposits. Rojdi's one mentioned marked bowl is placed by ceramic comparison with Rangpur IIB. Lothal B and other late Gujarat examples have images but no separately verified calendar interval in this package. See `rangpur`, `rojdi` and `lothal-late`; [Rao's original report](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/40421.pdf), pp. 25–28, 48 and 128–137, especially p. 130.

At Bet Dwarka, the report describes **two inscribed sherds and one shell seal bearing an animal motif**. The seven-character sherd came from intertidal exploration, the two-character sherd from onshore exploration, and the seal from UW6 layer 2. The authors explicitly lack a definite dated stratigraphic context for the shell objects. Pottery typology and pottery TL cannot independently date those three objects. See `bet-dwarka`; [Sundaresh and Gaur 1998](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/IndOc-Gulf/GujaratBetDwarka-Sundaresh1998.pdf), pp. 79–81, figs. 2–4.

Lal and Thapar furnish illustrated intermediate graffiti at Navdatoli and Prakash. Ahar–Banas/Gilund enters the bridge argument through ceramic comparisons; a graffiti image corpus was not verified. Thapar reports **10 different Prakash marks, four incomplete**, across grey, Malwa and Jorwe pottery. That is a repertoire count; the report does not provide an equivalent complete marked-object census. Its absolute chronology transfers conventional radiocarbon estimates from other sites. The table preserves those limitations and selects no calibrated mark interval for these rows. The Inamgaon cart graffito is one illustrated exemplar assigned approximately to 1500 BCE; its 1500–1400 bin envelope is explicitly an editorial representation of an approximate label. See `navdatoli`, `prakash-I`, `ahar-banas` and `inamgaon`; [Thapar 1967](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/45764.pdf), pp. 21–24 and 66–68, fig. 19; [Kenoyer 2004](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer2004_Wheeled%20Vehicles%20of%20the%20Indus%20Valley%20Civilizatio.pdf), PDF p. 17.

The OCP, Copper Hoard and PGW targets remain explicit acquisition gaps. No opened primary dated marked-object inventory was recovered for the first two. Lal's 1960 statement that graffiti had not yet been noted on PGW is retained as a historical research observation. He also reports successor grey-ware and NBPW graffiti at Sonpur/Rajghat, without enough calendar/context information here to select a date. These four rows contribute zero dated coverage because their required observations are missing. They do not establish a regional absence. See `ocp`, `copper-hoards`, `pgw` and `nbpw`; [Lal 1960](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/18414.pdf), pp. 22–23.

## Early historic dates and the southern record

The Ashokan Brahmi and Shahbazgarhi/Mansehra Kharosthi records supply the two A endpoints in this inventory. Their broad historical envelopes fall in the 300–200 BCE bin. The Kharosthi row names two edict sites; that quantity is excluded from marked-object arithmetic. The source review places formative Kharosthi earlier on historical/palaeographic grounds, while acknowledging the absence of direct formative inscriptions. See `ashoka-brahmi` and `ashoka-kharosthi`; [Salomon 1998](https://academic.oup.com/book/49774), pp. 10–13, 30, 46 and 133.

Anuradhapura is important for early Brahmi, but its opened witnesses support different assessments. Coningham and colleagues' 1996 abstract places the earliest writing at the beginning of the fourth century BCE. Deraniyagala's overview reports a 600–500 BCE assessment with radiocarbon and TL checks. The table stores these separately and selects the 1996 assessment with a C rating because this package did not recover the full sample/specimen table. Ibbankatuwa's dated cemetery context remains unselected because the recovered overview does not join its dates to marked specimens. See `anuradhapura` and `ibbankatuwa`; [1996 publisher abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/abs/passage-to-india-anuradhapura-and-the-early-use-of-the-brahmi-script/DAAA2514FB08E1DDE3FAFF2171AB097B) and [Deraniyagala's account](https://www.vedda.org/deraniyagala.htm), Early Historic and Protohistoric Iron Age sections.

The 2026 Tamil Nadu volumes substantially expand accessible images. Keeladi has **3782 collected graffiti sherds**, including a **2132 documented subset**, and **172 Tamil-Brahmi sherds** counted separately. Kodumanal has **2632 documented graffiti**. These totals should be retained with their collection/documentation scope. Neither total supplies the distribution of marked objects among the dated layers used here. Keeladi's published conventional and calibrated occupation envelopes remain separate; Kodumanal's 408–200 BCE conventional bracket and 480–210 BCE calibrated bracket remain separate. See `keeladi` and `kodumanal`; Rajan and Sivanantham, [Volume I](https://tamildigitallibrary.in/assets/docs/uploads/catalogue_article_file/BOK/upload/2026/03/TVA_TVA_BOK_062969/upload_primary_20260302113351638_20260302111421.pdf), pp. 56–57, 102 and 227.

Porunthal MEG I yielded **two inscribed ringstands** associated with rice in another vessel. Volume I, p. 167 explicitly reports **2440±30 BP, Beta-302854**, and MEG IV **2400±30 BP, Beta-305904**, as uncalibrated conventional ages. Its 490 and 450 BCE labels do not supply calibrated calendar intervals. Both rows remain outside coverage pending recovery of the calibration and grave register. Kilnamandi reports **43 burial graffiti sherds** and a MEG III sample, **3400±30 BP, Beta-666752**, with a **1769–1615 BCE interval at 93.3%**. The aggregate sherd-to-grave allocation was not recovered, so the row receives C. Its **45 habitation graffiti** remain separately undated. Perumbalai's graffiti-bearing phase has four published calibrated sample midpoints spanning 649–499 BCE; the **1028 site-wide documented graffiti** are excluded from numeric object bounds because their layer allocation is unknown. See `porunthal-I`, `porunthal-IV`, `kilnamandi-burials`, `kilnamandi-habitation` and `perumbalai`; Volume I, pp. 69–74, 167 and 181–182.

Sivagalai illustrates why the mark association matters. The volume's early urn determinations include Beta-583593, Beta-583592 and Beta-583594, but p. 295 reports no graffiti in the early urn burials. The claimed early Tamil-Brahmi habitation association uses a different charcoal determination, **Beta-600727, 2560±30 BP**, with a reported calibrated midpoint of 685 BCE. The full interval and registered marked-object join remain missing. Neither the urn dates nor the single midpoint fills a century bin here. See `sivagalai-early-burials` and `sivagalai-habitation`; Volume I, pp. 181–194 and 295.

Source availability is densest in South India by inventory size: **15 rows, 12 with verified images**, but only **4 dated additive rows**. The Indus northwest has **7 rows and 7 dated additive rows**, including **5 B links**. Gujarat has 4 inventory/2 dated rows, the Deccan 5/3, Sri Lanka 2/1, and Central India and the Ganga plain have no selected calendar-mark intervals here. These are acquisition-density measurements, not a ranking of ancient marking activity. The South India and northwest records also do not occupy one geographic transmission corridor. See [summary.json](run-2026-10-07-03/summary.json), `regions`, and [regional-gaps.json](run-2026-10-07-03/regional-gaps.json).

The A-only series has 32 empty bins from 3500 to 300 BCE because no directly joined radiometric marked endpoint was authenticated in this bounded recovery. Earlier excavated marks appear under B. The H/M series has eight empty bins in total, including 1900–1800 and 400–300 BCE as well as the six-bin run. Perumbalai's 649 BCE midpoint bracket first overlaps the 700–600 bin; the six-bin gap length uses whole bins rather than a claim of exact archaeological endpoints.

## What the continuity arguments still need

Lal's original p. 21 gives **47 shared types**, **6 exclusive megalithic types** and **8 exclusive earlier types**. His reported rounded percentages are reproduced by **47/53 = 88.68%** and **47/55 = 85.45%**. The earlier comparison set combines Harappan and post-Harappan Chalcolithic graffiti. His intermediates are real source examples, but their dates, matching tolerances and frequencies are incomplete. Mahadevan's Sulur argument proposes four sign identifications and uses Indus pair frequencies; it reverses the reading direction for one pair, and its tablet comparison places pairs on different faces. The dish's cited first-century BCE museum label also falls outside the present endpoint. The 2025/2026 morphology arguments add many images, yet use several populations and denominators. The 2026 catalogue itself has an unresolved **three-sherd discrepancy**, with 9619+4546=14165 documented versus a printed final total of 14168. See [continuity-claims.json](continuity-claims.json), `lal-bridge`, `mahadevan-pairs` and `tn-morphology`; [Lal](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/18414.pdf), p. 21; [Mahadevan](https://journal.fi/store/article/download/43982/11036), pp. 379 and 382–384; Volume I, pp. 227–229 and 263–301. Arithmetic appears in `lal_arithmetic` and `catalogue_arithmetic` in the frozen summary.

Salomon's origin review distinguishes indigenous proposals associated with Cunningham, Ojha and Pandey, Indus-derived proposals associated with Langdon/Hunter and aspects of Sircar, several Semitic prototypes, and adaptation accounts. Bühler argued for a northern Semitic/Phoenician source; Taylor favored a southern Semitic route. Falk's 1993 account proposed a deliberate Mauryan combination of Greek and Kharosthi; Salomon disputed its Greek vowel derivation and favored Aramaic influence combined with Indian phonological organization. These positions are reported through Salomon, pp. 19–30; the original works were not opened. Kharosthi supplies a related Aramaic adaptation comparison, with different structural evidence. The record needed to discriminate these accounts is a dated formative corpus with declared correspondence rules. It is not supplied by isolated modern shape matches. The missing observations for each attributed claim appear in [continuity-claims.json](continuity-claims.json), and four acquisition-dependent, falsifiable designs appear in [TEST-DESIGNS.md](TEST-DESIGNS.md).
