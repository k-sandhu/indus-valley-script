# Amendment 3: reading the printed legends, and corrections to the normaliser

Written 21 October 2026 (UTC) after the Bloch, Marshall and Mahadevan transcriptions arrived and before any statistic of section 7 was computed on a comparison corpus. It fixes how the normaliser reads the editors' marks, corrects two defects in the code delivered by Codex run C, and records the coordinator's corrections and the extended tag lexicon. The rules are implemented in [edition_rules.py](edition_rules.py) and [normalize_brahmi.py](normalize_brahmi.py); run C's original normaliser and lexicon are kept in [codex/c-statistics/original/](codex/c-statistics/original/).

## How a printed legend becomes the die's text

The editors print restorations, corrections and doubts in the conventions of *Epigraphia Indica*, and the sign units must follow what the die carries. Letters in square brackets were lost to damage and restored by the editor, often from a duplicate impression; they are kept and the record is `restored`. Letters in square brackets with an asterisk are the editor's supplement of letters the engraver omitted or that are illegible; they are dropped. Where such a supplement stands at the very start or end of the legend and holds more than a visarga or anusvāra, it stands for letters lost at the edge, and the record is `damaged-edge`. Round brackets hold Marshall's, Bloch's and Mahadevan's corrections (`Puś(sh)ya`), alternative readings (`(or sometimes nigamā)`) and doubts (`(?)`); they are dropped with their content. Spooner uses round brackets for letters he restores on a worn impression (`(A)mātya`, `Buddhadeva(sya)`, `ku(li)ka`), so in `asi-ar-1913-14` their letters are kept and the record is `restored`, and a space he sets between such a bracket and the rest of its word is removed. When a dropped vowel followed a bare consonant, the consonant keeps its inherent `a`, so Marshall's `K(ā)lañjara` and Bloch's `Sauvarṇṇam[i*]trasya` give `ka la ñja ra` and `ma tra`, not a conjunct. Any question mark sets `reading_doubtful`. Two or more dots, an ellipsis, or a dash that does not end a printed line mark lost text: the words are split there, and the record is `damaged-edge` when no letter precedes or follows the gap and `damaged-internal` otherwise. Full stops, commas, semicolons, colons, slashes, quotation marks, raised dots and stray asterisks are removed.

A hyphen or dash at the end of a printed line of the legend continues the word on the next line. The two parts form one word without the hyphen (`śrī-Dhru-` / `vasvāminī` gives `śrī-dhruvasvāminī`), the word stands in the line where it starts, and the position of the line break inside it is recorded in sign units in the extra field `line_breaks_units`. When the edition gives no line division, the legend is one line. Entries whose impressions carry different legends (Marshall's lumps with a personal seal beside an official's counter-seal, entries 37, 40, 47, 61, 76 and 77 among them) become one record per impression; the impressions share the lump's `object_id` and each has its own `die_id`. Marshall sets brackets apart from their letters, so for `asi-ar-1911-12` a space before an opening bracket that follows a letter, a space after a closing square bracket whose content ends in a bare consonant, and the spaces around `(?)` are removed.

## Transliteration and metadata

All letters are lowered in every source, including Mahadevan's capitals. In the two older-convention reports (`asi-ar-1911-12`, `asi-ar-1913-14`) the aspirate `chh` is mapped to IAST `ch` through a placeholder before `ch` becomes `c`; the literal sequence of Amendment 1 and of run C's code (`chh` → `ch`, then `ch` → `c`) turned every aspirate into `c`. In the three Indo-Aryan sources `ē` and `ō` become `e` and `o`, because Brahmi writes one sign for each, and `ṁ` becomes `ṃ`. Tamil keeps its long and short vowels. In the Indo-Aryan legends `ḷ` before a vowel is the retroflex lateral of a name (`makkutaḷi`), not the vocalic vowel of Amendment 1.

A remark that only compares a form with Prakrit or calls a genitive irregular does not identify the language; `language` is then the editor's collection statement. Bloch and Spooner read and translate the Basarh legends as Sanskrit. Marshall's statement (p. 46) makes pre-Gupta legends Prakrit or mixed and the rest Sanskrit; the period comes from his palaeographic date, written in figures or words (`fourth or fifth century of our era`), the earliest century of a range deciding: B.C. centuries and the first century A.D. `pre-Gupta (early)`, the second and third `pre-Gupta (Kushana)`, the fourth to sixth `Gupta`, later centuries `post-Gupta (later)`, a century without an era being A.D. A date phrased as older or earlier than the Gupta period gives `pre-Gupta (Kushana)`, and `early type`, `early period` or `antiquity` gives `pre-Gupta (early)`. An undated Bhita entry takes the majority class of his statement, Gupta and Sanskrit; an undated Basarh entry takes the editors' date for the collection, Gupta. A pre-Gupta Basarh legend takes Marshall's rule, Prakrit or mixed. For Mahadevan's rows the language is his statement for the row where he makes one, otherwise Tamil.

## Coordinator corrections

The corrections file `data/raw/2026-10-21-head-direction-corpora/review/corrections.json` (local) holds every change the coordinator made to a transcription after checking the page image, with the printed page and the reason; its keys and counts are listed in [FEASIBILITY.md](FEASIBILITY.md). They are spacing corrections where the print sets a bracket apart from its word and the editor's translation shows one word, the period of an entry that refers to another entry's date, the language of Mahadevan rows he marks as Prakrit or Pali, and the exclusion of an object that is not a seal, ring or coin.

## Tag lexicon

Each added line is marked as an addition under this amendment.
