# Which contributions are new enough and strong enough to publish?

Review date: 22 October 2026. Status: source review. We assessed the eight contribution families in the preceding discussion against primary publications, public research records and the project's preserved evidence. This is a publication assessment; it does not introduce a reading or a new confirmation experiment.

We have two substantially developed contributions that can support focused papers: the controlled evaluation of a specified Sanskrit key, and the calibration of reference distributions for copied and selected short records. A source-linked object reconciliation resource is a strong third paper after a bounded accuracy and release audit. Restoration and line-trained segmentation are promising papers with specific validation work remaining. The current evidence supports narrower claims for chronology, rebus tests, seal individuality and fish ordering than the earlier contribution outline suggested.

A new measurement, a careful correction or a reusable dataset can constitute a publishable contribution. We distinguish that from priority for an idea. Finding earlier work on the same idea reduces the scope of our novelty claim; it does not invalidate our measurements.

## Decisions

A means that the scientific core supports a focused paper, subject to normal independent checking and preparation. B means a specified scientific repair or validation is needed. C means useful supporting evidence or a resource component, with insufficient support for the proposed standalone claim. These are our review judgements, not predictions of editorial acceptance.

| Contribution | Defensible addition to the field | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation of the published Sanskrit key | A complete-key, real-Indus evaluation with frequency-matched rearrangements, independent language models, representation-specific redundancy, fitted controls and known-answer power checks | **A: strongest existing empirical manuscript**, with a narrow claim |
| Conditional reference laws | Exact short-record examples distinguishing selection by uniqueness from deduplication, object copying, imperfect observations and the support on which a score is defined | **A: focused applied-methods paper or substantial methods companion** |
| Object identity, source joins and coverage | A versioned reconciliation and uncertainty layer across existing corpora, excavation tables, museum identifiers, photographs and later published finds | **B, close: prioritize as a data paper** |
| Restoration benchmark | An evaluation that isolates objects and related texts, measures predictive gain against contextual baselines and compares modern estimators | **B: complete the corrected evaluation first** |
| Line-trained segmentation | Cross-validated prediction of physical divisions, with a separate readable-script calibration and a frozen unit inventory | **B: promising computational-epigraphy paper** |
| Harappa chronological differences | Within-trench measurements of length, inventory and graphic-variant changes in the retained Mound F population | **B: archaeological chronology and influence checks needed** |
| Rebus and interlock evaluation | Tests on unselected sign pairs, matched lexical alternatives and cross-dictionary sensitivity | **B: repair the matching and parser before a publication claim** |
| Seal-text identity and circulation | A reproducible count of repeats, spatial distribution and text–motif relations, with particular plate checks | **C alone; potentially part of a comparative study** |
| Fish ordering and stroke contexts | Additional quantified constraints, sensitivity analyses and corrections to source counts or object references | **C alone; a short specialist note may be possible** |
| Numerical decoding and reference-model selection | Known-answer experiments about required observations and model mismatch | **C for a new Indus interpretation; B for a broader methods study with external cases** |

## 1. What the literature removes from our novelty claims

Recurring segments, terminal classes and computational structural analysis have a long history. Koskenniemi's 1981 paper explicitly combines segmentation with syntactic classification and studies pairwise constraints. Yadav and colleagues' 2010 study develops n-gram structure and recurring combinations. Khanna and Merriam's 2025 article proposes distributional sign and pair classes. These sources precede a general claim that we discovered reusable units or a formulaic structure. [Koskenniemi 1981](https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/207893149/indus_syntactic_1981.pdf), [Yadav et al. 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009506), [Khanna and Merriam 2025](https://www.ijcaonline.org/archives/volume187/number64/khanna-2025-ijca-926075.pdf).

Physical line arrangement is also prior evidence. Bhaskar's 2024 article discusses when line breaks can be joined and when they affect adjacency, with an explicit discussion of Mahadevan's 1977 treatment. This makes our choice to train a predictor on physical divisions a possible methodological addition, while requiring a direct account of which divisions carry which sort of boundary evidence. [Bhaskar 2024, section 3](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/MarkersAndAgenciesOfAnisotropy.pdf).

A particularly close public predecessor is Daniel Bourdeau's Cyphersolver project. We inspected its Indus page at commit `1c73645947deba694cb82d42dba967319e8df5a5`, whose recorded commit date is 1 October 2026. That snapshot already discusses line breaks, seal individuality with Linear B and Ur III comparisons, stratigraphic differences, and chance baselines for Tamil compounds. It is an unreviewed project record; this audit does not validate its results. It is sufficient evidence that those broad approaches were publicly documented before our 12–22 October packages. Its alternative transcriptions must not be counted as independent physical observations. The mutable page's September date alone would not have established priority for each result. [Pinned page](https://github.com/dbourdeau/cyphersolver/blob/1c73645947deba694cb82d42dba967319e8df5a5/docs/indus.html).

Names, titles and administrative functions are established hypotheses. Parpola discusses short seal texts in terms of personal names and occupations; Mukhopadhyay argues for taxation, licensing and control functions, explicitly using identical inscriptions at distant settlements. Our integrated model can organize new tests, but these functions are not new proposals from this project. Publication of a hypothesis does not establish its archaeological truth. [Parpola 2008](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/indus-writing.pdf), [Mukhopadhyay 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-02320-7).

## 2. Sanskrit-key evaluation: a defensible paper now

The contribution is a controlled evaluation of one released key on actual Indus inscriptions. It tests what the key fixes after optional vowels and phoneme pooling, measures how much the dictionary rule admits, compares the assignments with frequency-matched rearrangements, and asks whether a known correct key would be detectable under the same scoring procedure.

The revised [manuscript](../../papers/006-sanskrit-key-calibration/manuscript.md) reports 1,859 distinct complete inscriptions. Its dictionary emulation accepts approximately 92–100% of random consonant strings in the stated length range. The key's language-model score lies about 1.2–2.4 standard deviations above rearrangements under the tested representations, while the synthetic correct keys lie 27–72 standard deviations above theirs. Its order-only comparison does not show the corresponding advantage. These are different experiments; their standard deviations must not be treated as a common effect-size scale.

The broad criticism of dictionary fitting and unicity arguments is already public. Raghavendra's August 2026 preprint supplies a structured non-language, multilingual lexical stress tests, grouped evaluation and a direct unicity counterexample. The Panini Protocol gives a public demonstration of forcing Sanskrit readings onto other material. Our paper must lead with the narrower full-key evaluation and its added controls. [Raghavendra 2026, appendices B–C](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02999v1), [Panini Protocol](https://ugrashravas.github.io/paniniprotocol/).

The [adversarial review](../2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/sanskrit-key/review/REVIEW.md) found sensitivity to projection, deletion-based optimization and an overly broad interpretation of dictionary emulation. The revised manuscript incorporates these qualifications. Before submission, trace every abstract number to the final endpoint, pin the exact versions of the preprint and key, and obtain a Sanskrit specialist's check of the projection and lexical acceptance model. The central conclusion concerns the discriminating support for this submitted key under these tests.

Suggested contribution statement: **We evaluate a complete published Indus sign key against matched alternatives and known-answer controls, showing which apparent fits survive independent scoring and which acceptance rules fail to discriminate.**

## 3. Conditional reference laws: publish as an applied statistical contribution

Winkler and colleagues address dependent observations through exchangeability blocks; Hemerik and Goeman derive exact random-permutation tests. Those foundations support our application to inscription records. [Winkler et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26074200/), [Hemerik and Goeman 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6405018/).

Our [paper 002](../../papers/002-conditional-reference-laws/manuscript.md) supplies explicit finite record laws and preserved counterexamples relevant to inscription corpora. In its declared simulations, record-wise shuffling of literal copies rejects 15.9% at a nominal 5% threshold; object-wise shuffling gives 5.4%. The imperfect-face comparison is 11.0% against 5.0%. The exact enumeration distinguishes conditioning on unique strings from deduplicating a larger sample. Its feasibility examples show why a statistic's undefined cases belong in the analysis.

This is enough material for a focused applied-methods paper. The original contribution is the specific derivation, examples and calibration results. A statistics reviewer should check the derivations and the relation to selective inference and occupancy sampling. The package already contains independent audits, but this review did not rerun the large simulation grids. A broad theory-paper claim would require a substantially wider theorem and literature comparison.

Suggested contribution statement: **We derive and test the reference laws induced by copying, deduplication and fitting restrictions in short inscription datasets, with exact examples and reproducible calibration failures.**

## 4. Object reconciliation: the strongest resource opportunity

ICIT already provides an archaeological and epigraphical relational database. Fuls's 2023 conference abstract reports object metadata, images, sign coding, direction and analytical tools. We cannot claim the first integrated Indus database or first linkage of inscriptions to archaeology. [Fuls 2023, abstract 76](https://2023.caaconference.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2023/04/abstract_book_v13.pdf), [ICIT project](https://epigraphica.de/indus/menueindus.htm).

Our addition is a transparent reconciliation layer that retains conflicting readings, identifies the source of each field and measures what remains missing. The [CISI basic-data package](../2026-10-13-cisi-basic-data-full/README.md) covers 3,682 printed rows and 3,680 join-eligible entries; 2,602 join uniquely. Across the 4,695 provisional object families, museum-number coverage is 52.2% when uncertain digits count, 44.3% when at least one number is definite, and 42.5% when the whole cell is unbracketed. These definitions must accompany any headline.

The [later-find inventory](../2026-10-21-post-catalogue-finds/README.md) identifies 301 definite post-catalogue inscribed objects from 21 sites in its searched sources. Strict identity rules leave a missing-object range of 55–291; post-outcome context joins narrow it to 55–224. Neither number means that all 301 are absent from every prior corpus. The excavation-table joins reuse upstream transcription work and must credit it.

The resource needs a versioned field schema, object-versus-face identifiers, published uncertainty categories, a stratified independent accuracy audit, and a clear comparison with the named upstream versions. CISI 2 retains 2,870 unadjudicated disagreements; row completeness does not establish field accuracy. A paper can release these alternatives honestly, or lead with the adjudicated subset. Provide redistributable derived tables and an acquisition recipe for restricted inputs. This audit adds no new object records.

Suggested contribution statement: **We provide a source-linked reconciliation of Indus object identities and metadata, preserve unresolved alternatives, and quantify the coverage and remaining gaps of specified corpus versions.**

## 5. Restoration and segmentation: valuable, with separate validation needs

Rao and colleagues already demonstrated missing-sign prediction in 2009 and used modified Kneser–Ney smoothing; the source also removed repeated inscriptions in its working corpus. Markov restoration, smoothing and simple deduplication are therefore prior methods. [Rao et al. 2009, methods and supplementary information](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rao/PNASIndus.pdf).

Our restoration contribution is the evaluation design: physical dependencies, transitive identity links, nested tuning, contextual reference models and comparisons across estimators. The [modern-restoration review](../2026-10-06-modern-restoration/REVIEW.md) and [report](../2026-10-06-modern-restoration/REPORT.md) expose remaining contamination. Sixteen short strings connect roughly 9.3% of queries through full-corpus aliases; near-variant overlap affects a broader 25.2% set. Reported deletion sensitivities are useful diagnostics. They are not a complete refit with corrected groups. Complete that refit for every compared model and quantify paired uncertainty before using the headline model ranking. A synthetic known-position deletion experiment also needs to be distinguished from performance on genuinely damaged inscriptions.

For segmentation, we replayed the saved [bench endpoint](segmentation-replay.json). It exactly reproduces 27 of 34 required line divisions, recall 0.7941, and a cut rate elsewhere of 0.0765; precision and F1 are explicitly null. Their difference is 0.7176. The [study](../2026-10-20-seal-legend-units/REPORT.md) reports an interval of 0.586–0.838 for that difference. Its [Ur III calibration](../2026-10-20-seal-legend-units/run-2026-10-20/sumerian-summary.json) gives word-boundary F1 0.618 against 0.557 for cutting everywhere, but branching entropy reaches 0.692. The new predictor therefore passes its declared calibration without becoming the best word segmenter on that readable cohort. Our replay checks scoring and hashes, not model training or the archaeological validity of the labels.

The primary plate-order subset contains only 33 faces. It needs epigraphic adjudication of direction and line order, uncertainty over independent object components, and a clear sensitivity analysis for the premise that a physical line break marks a reusable linguistic or formulaic unit. Compare directly with the prior line-break approaches and explain the different method rankings on line divisions and readable word boundaries. The frozen inventory's prospective test on unread seals would provide stronger evidence of transfer. The current 79% measures required-line recall.

## 6. Chronology: the effect may be publishable, but its clock needs checking

Kenoyer and Meadow already document chronological changes in inscribed objects and seal manufacture, including the late use of long rectangular seals. The existence of change is established prior work. [Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, especially pp. 8–10 of the accessible manuscript](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/KenoyerMeadow%202010%20Inscribed%20Objects%20from%20Harappa.pdf).

Our [Harappa study](../2026-10-20-harappa-legend-periods/REPORT.md) adds controlled measurements on 173 Mound F seals in six trench frames: complete text length rises from about 4.0 to 4.8 to 5.3 signs by stratum thirds, and length, inventory and variant tests survive the specified multiplicity correction. The decisive risk is the conversion from Vats's strata to HARP period labels in the export. The within-trench rank analysis reduces some problems, but does not independently verify that conversion, context integrity or chronological comparability.

A publishable paper needs an archaeological audit of the assignments and a presentation that separates relative stratigraphy from absolute period dates. It also needs leave-one-trench checks: variant drift loses significance without Trench I. Closing-stock change has a 90% interval from about −0.19 to +0.10; the declared ±0.25 rule permits quite substantial change. It supports the specified bounded result, not a strong claim of an invariant grammar.

## 7. Rebus tests: potentially useful, presently too fragile

The narrow new question is whether published word assignments predict compound formation on sign pairs omitted from the published examples, under an explicit lexical comparison. Existing public work already tests chance Tamil matches. Our specific unselected-pair and dictionary-transfer designs may add evidence, but priority for random rebus controls is unavailable.

The [unselected-pair run](../2026-10-22-rebus-blind-templates/REPORT.md) gives nine hits among 38 adjacent pairs against 4.97 expected; the ratio is 1.81 with an interval of 0.93–2.72, and 6.7% of whole random keys reach nine hits. Passing a rule that the ratio is below two does not establish equivalence to chance. The [retrospective](../2026-10-22-rebus-blind-templates/RETROSPECTIVE.md) records a frequency proxy that counts all Sangam words beginning with a one-letter stem. This affects templates contributing five hits, and the aṟu lookup also resolves to a different headword through a fallback.

The [Winslow run](../2026-10-22-rebus-winslow/REPORT.md) fails the frozen cross-dictionary prediction. Its zero baseline partly follows from a parser that misses compounds and changes the replacement pools. The paper needs exact matching of tokens or lemmas, a checked Tamil compound parser, analyses that keep shared sign assignments dependent, and a newly frozen evaluation after the repairs. Retain the failed endpoints. The favourable 0.44% four-link result from the earlier base-rate study also belongs in the paper; selecting only negative results would distort the record.

A hypothetical calculation that seven hits become likely after approximately 28 trials describes a selection mechanism. It does not reconstruct Parpola's actual search history or establish that his results arose that way.

## 8. Supporting findings and methods

The [seal-identity study](../2026-10-12-seal-text-identity/REPORT.md) adds reproducible measurements and particular source checks. Its primary locality test gives p = 0.31; a later length-matched comparison cannot replace that endpoint. The same-motif result is almost entirely unicorn-driven, and the absence of duplicate long texts needs controls using known names and titles matched on length and vocabulary. Earlier literature and the pinned public project already discuss repeated text across sites and individual seal legends. The current package can support a comparative paper; it does not by itself identify persons or offices.

The [fish-context programme](../2026-10-20-fish-context/PROGRAM-REPORT.md) quantifies ordering and stroke-context constraints and records failed predictions. Parpola's fish readings and their associated sequences are prior work; our ordering count is an additional measurement. A source correction such as the H-902/H-912 mismatch may justify a specialist note if the correction changes an argument and all identities are checked. One surviving dependency component in the H-912 comparison cannot decide a general stellar or quantity interpretation. [Parpola 2009](https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/127256488/Parpola_A_2009._Hind_leg_fish_in_the_Indus_script._Scripta1_37_76.pdf).

[Papers 003](../../papers/003-physical-observations/manuscript.md) and [004](../../papers/004-reference-model-selection/manuscript.md) offer carefully recorded known-answer experiments. Additive scale ambiguity, assignment algorithms, model selection, decipherment with external references and latent-mixture identifiability are established. Knight et al. and Luo et al. are relevant decipherment precedents; Allman, Matias and Rhodes supply the latent-identifiability result. The new work is the particular controlled comparison and failure catalogue. Real readable-script cases and weaker assumptions would strengthen a standalone methods paper. The Puruchuco replication is a control, not a new khipu reading. [Knight et al. 2006](https://aclanthology.org/P06-2065.pdf), [Luo et al. 2019](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1303/), [Allman et al. 2009](https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.5032).

## Publication order and limits of this review

Prepare the Sanskrit-key paper and the focused conditional-law paper first. In parallel with manuscript preparation, finish the bounded accuracy audit and release plan for the data resource. Then complete the restoration refit and the segmentation adjudication. Pursue the chronology and rebus papers after their stated scientific repairs. Treat seal and fish results as supporting analyses until a sharper discriminating comparison is available.

We inspected primary sources and the repository's protocols, aggregates, amendments and reviews. We independently replayed one saved segmentation scoring endpoint and checked its provenance. We did not rerun every model, independently transcribe plates, or validate every calculation in the cited literature. The full 1994 Parpola book and Wells's book-length treatments were not exhaustively checked; precise priority for every proposed unit remains open. The search register records this scope.

**Boldest supported assessment:** the project can contribute publishable, reproducible tests of proposed readings and data-dependent structural claims. The deciding test is whether the narrowed claims survive source adjudication, the specified dependency corrections and independent replay from documented inputs.
