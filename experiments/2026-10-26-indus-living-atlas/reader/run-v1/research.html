CHANGING MARKERS · SOURCE REVIEW

People and practices after the Indus cities

26 October 2026. Source review. GPT-6, Codex API workspace harness; exact model build unavailable. Verification tier: submitted.

The proposed explanation fits an established archaeological pattern: communities continued working, farming and exchanging goods while the urban system lost some of its most recognizable features. The change took different forms in Punjab, Sindh, Gujarat and the upper Ganges basin. Our review adds a source-linked account of those differences and of the processes that make later activity harder to identify. The most useful extension is to measure ordinary occupation and urban markers separately in the same dated deposits.

We reviewed twelve primary publications or excavator accounts and recorded sixteen observations in evidence.json. Each row gives the original title, authors, date, URL, page or section, chronology, interpretation and source dependence. The source register distinguishes complete local witnesses, preliminary reports and web text. This is a bounded review; it does not estimate regional population totals or an empirical probability of continuity.

What can continue

Historical question Evidence that could answer it
Do people remain in a region? Dated domestic activity, food production and burials across the transition; ancestry and mobility measurements for particular people
Does a practice continue? Its materials and operational steps in dated production or use contexts
Does a settlement move? Dated old and new locations within a surveyed catchment, with comparable recovery and sufficiently narrow chronology
Is one plot continuously occupied? An occupation sequence whose potential breaks are resolved by stratigraphy and independent dates
Does an institution continue? Repeated arrangements of production, exchange or administration, including diagnostic intermediate evidence
Does a language or writing system continue? Dated linguistic or graphic evidence that identifies a transmission relationship
Can we detect the activity? Evidence that the relevant deposits survive, lie within the sampled depth and were recovered by a suitable method

These questions have different answers at the same place. Farming can continue while an administrative system changes. People can move while their regional population persists. A town can return to an old mound after centuries. We therefore avoid using one continuity label for every historical scale.

The project's 11 October continuity study and 25 October crop review already support selective practical inheritance as a model to test. Meadow and Kenoyer had described the distinction between shared urban traits and older regional farming traditions in 1997. Here we compare marker changes with their archaeological visibility, including regional differences concealed by a single transition date. Meadow and Kenoyer 1997, p. 139.

Regional sequences

Punjab. Harappa's excavators describe regional exchange and craft activity in Cemetery H levels, approximately 1800 to perhaps 1500 BCE, while script and standardized weights cease there. Their provisional phase table has a different uncertain endpoint from the prose. The practical result is local activity after urban reorganization, with the final chronology still requiring context-level resolution. Kenoyer and Meadow 2004, pp. 202–203, E02.

Sindh. At Chanhu-daro, the original excavation report distinguishes later Jhukar seals or amulets, many round, from the familiar inscribed seal forms. Mackay records no clear script in this particular group. He also describes brick removal and disturbed upper deposits. We retain the relative sequence without adopting its legacy ethnic interpretations or assigning a new calendar date to the assemblage. Mackay 1943, pp. ix and 143, E04–E05. Modern identification must allow changed forms and exclude residual older objects before claiming continued manufacture.

Gujarat. Dholavira makes the transition particularly clear. Bisht's preliminary report assigns Stage VI to about 1950–1800 BCE. After a short desertion, a smaller settlement retained rectangular inscribed seals and cubical chert weights while pottery and housing changed. A longer break precedes Stage VII, assigned about 1500–1450 BCE, with circular houses and absent urban attributes. These are provisional excavator phase assignments. Bisht 2015, §§5.6–5.7, pp. 103–104, E06–E07. Stage VI spans 1900 BCE; its envelope alone cannot date an individual seal or weight after that year or exclude residual objects. The report's persistence claim calls for primary contexts and absolute dates to establish each marker's final use.

Haryana and the upper Ganges connection. The familiar Bahola crop comparison retains summer and winter crops while their proportions change. The Jognakhera paper supplies posturban crop dates, including rice at 1517–1428 cal BCE, with the sample-to-phase joins still incomplete. Petrie and Bates 2017, Table S23, Swaroop et al. 2026, chronology, E13–E16. These are exposed observations from the preceding project audit.

A separate chemical analysis adds household evidence. Suryanarayan and colleagues studied 172 pottery fragments at seven sites. The small comparisons spanning urban and posturban contexts, chiefly Alamgirpur and Masudpur VII, did not detect a change in the reported lipid isotope variables. Product mixtures, small samples and incomplete local reference fats limit the interpretation of particular foods. This supports a test of practical retention, with equivalence still to be measured. Suryanarayan et al. 2021, §4.4, E12.

Alamgirpur also preserves a useful disagreement. Its excavators report possible ceramic overlap and no clear stratigraphic gap, yet state that available AMS dates suggest a protracted gap; seven dating submissions failed. The report therefore leaves calendar continuity unresolved. Burj supplies a separate case with an explicit occupation break. Singh et al. 2013, pp. 53–54, E14–E15. These cases belong in any comparison sample alongside apparently continuous sequences.

Why some continuities become hard to see

There are two mechanisms to test. An activity can persist while its diagnostic objects change, or its deposits can become inaccessible to a particular survey. The first concerns ancient behavior; the second concerns the surviving record and our observation of it.

Weight history illustrates the first mechanism and its limits. Kenoyer's specialist chapter relates urban and Early Historic metrology but explicitly identifies the intermediate relationship as unfinished research. The interval to about 600 BCE needs dated evidence. Dholavira's later weights qualify a broad disappearance statement without filling that entire interval. Kenoyer 2010, pp. 117 and 120, E03.

Landform history illustrates the second. Giosan and colleagues distinguish exposed, well-preserved parts of upper Sindh from southwestern alluvial settings where later sediment could cover earlier settlements. Their study supplies a spatially specific burial mechanism. It does not establish how many settlements lie beneath the deposits. Giosan et al. 2012, “Settlement Dynamics in the Harappan Domain”, E08–E09.

Neogi and colleagues recover buried soils and former raised settlement settings at four northwest Indian sites. Modern agriculture and landscape change obscure some of that local topography. These sampled profiles show why a flat present surface can be a poor guide to ancient occupation. A buried soil's presence and a buried settlement's presence remain separate observations. Neogi et al. 2020, discussion and supplement, E11.

Survey history adds another filter. Green and Petrie find uneven coverage and regional differences in settlement distributions. Their proposed northeastern reoccupation requires revisiting older records and surveying gaps. A map of changing site counts cannot by itself identify the people who moved. Green and Petrie 2018, Discussion and Conclusions, E10.

Later towns over older deposits are a further hypothesis to investigate through permitted construction archives, cores and sections. A modern town's name, proximity to an ancient mound or long-lived sacred reputation cannot resolve a buried occupation sequence. The atlas should record the resolution of the actual sequence and any hiatus for each place.

Assessment of the theory

Version of the explanation Present assessment
People and local practices persisted through urban reorganization Supported in named posturban sequences; the regional balance of retention, movement and population loss remains uneven
Diagnostic urban objects changed or stopped being deposited Supported, with different endpoints by region and object class
All Indus writing and cubical weights stopped everywhere in 1900 BCE Dholavira's reported persistence into a reduced settlement challenges this conventional cutoff; exact last-use dates remain unresolved
Burial, erosion and survey history conceal some activity Supported mechanisms with local observations; hidden totals remain unmeasured
Every former city remained occupied on the same plot Contradicted by reported breaks, including Dholavira; unresolved elsewhere
A writing tradition moved entirely onto perishable material A testable possibility without direct support in this reviewed sample
The same language, political structure or self-identified civilization continued unchanged Requires evidence beyond the practical and settlement observations reviewed here

The next study measures ordinary occupation, marker occurrence and detection separately. It reserves independent sites, includes interrupted sequences, freezes chronological and recovery rules before confirmation, and gives each proposed continuity a rejection condition. The present source and integrity checks establish a usable evidence register. They do not replace independent archaeological review.

The boldest reading supported here is that the Indus urban system fragmented while regional communities continued and changed their practical traditions; its archaeological boundary partly records the end of a shared urban material system. The deciding test is a recovery-controlled set of independently dated household and production sequences across that boundary, with the final appearances of seals, writing, weights and building forms measured in the same deposits.

LIVING TOWNS · SOURCE REVIEW

Living towns and Indus-period occupation

Harappa and Rupnagar are the two living-settlement candidates in this register whose local archaeological sequences overlap the Mature Indus period. Harappa's modern town covers part of the ancient site, making the buried history beneath its streets a direct target for continuity research. Rupnagar's published site card records a break after its Harappan phase. We found no complete, source-audited sequence demonstrating uninterrupted occupation from the Indus period to the present in these sixteen candidates. That is the result of this bounded search, with the missing intervals identified for each place.

The evidence table compares sixteen candidates in India, Pakistan, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, plus Dholavira and Mohenjo-daro as abandoned-city comparisons. towns.json supplies the dates, evidence places, modern civic identities, spatial relations, gaps and source IDs for the atlas. sources.json records twenty-four sources and their access depth. The protocol fixed the candidate list and coding rules before the new search. This is a purposive comparison of relevant places, not an exhaustive ranking of South Asia's oldest towns.

What counts as persistence

We distinguish the date of first habitation at a place, its growth into a city, later occupation in the same area and continuation of the same bounded settlement. Each requires different observations. A modern municipal boundary can contain several ancient settlements, and a name can survive a relocation. A sacred place can remain in use while residential settlement contracts or moves. The register identifies the place to which each date belongs and puts earlier neighbouring evidence in a separate field.

The comparison intervals are 2600–1900 BCE for Mature Indus and 1900–1300 BCE for later occupation. Overlap denotes contemporary habitation; it does not assign Harappan culture to every contemporary settlement. BCE values are positive numbers with the older bound first. Broad archaeological phases, approximate onsets and uncalibrated radiocarbon results retain their different meanings. A no overlap label describes the reviewed local sequence; earlier deposits can remain unexamined. All main map pins are geographic index points with 5 km stated precision, suitable for a regional view. They cannot locate trenches or establish a shared ancient and modern footprint.

The most informative places

Harappa offers the strongest next test of occupation concealed by a living town. Kenoyer's 1991 excavation chapter places approximately one quarter of the archaeological site beneath the modern town, on printed p. 57. The acquired chapter supports that spatial relation directly. James and colleagues' 2025 study reviews occupation from the fourth millennium through about 1300 BCE and uses a broad statement about continuous occupation. Neither the analysed chronology nor that statement supplies the missing later occupation series. Harappa remains a strong candidate with a specific gap: the dated history between the Late Harappan phases and the modern town.

Rupnagar makes the continuity problem testable. The ASI site card, item 146, dates Period I to 2000–1400 BCE and the following Painted Grey Ware period to 1000–700 BCE, and explicitly identifies a break. A broader town-scale history could still find contemporary occupation in another part of Rupnagar. That possibility needs a mapped sequence outside the excavated mound.

Vadnagar and Peshawar illustrate long occupation that starts later. The Vadnagar nomination describes seven successive cultures beneath the living town; the published research abstract reports a settlement sequence beginning about 2754 calibrated years BP. We retain an approximate 800–750 BCE anchor. The proposed 1400 BCE extension needs the primary dated samples and their contexts. At Gor Khatri in Peshawar, the 2026 State Party submission reports about 2300 years of stratified occupation. Both places can demonstrate substantial later persistence without filling the Indus-to-modern interval.

How the other candidates change the question

Taxila's early history belongs to a serial landscape with Saraikala, Bhir, Sirkap and Sirsukh. Bhir's sixth-century BCE city date is kept apart from Saraikala's prehistoric phases. At Srinagar, Burzahom establishes third- and second-millennium BCE habitation in the region; the city's own beginning and the intervening settlement links remain unresolved. The older history of Pandrenthan also requires its own spatial record. These are promising landscape histories, with several places to join.

Varanasi's ASI Rajghat catalogue, Introduction pp. xi–xii, reports the older c. 800 BCE chronology and suggests extending it back by 400–500 years. We preserve both assertions. Nearby Akatha, Ramnagar and Sarai Mohana require separate dated records. Mathura's annual excavation report assigns its local earliest Period I to the sixth–fourth century BCE. Ujjain's report dates the earliest fortified phase at Garh-Kalika to c. 700–500 BCE. A ceramic tradition's general age cannot replace the excavator's local chronology. The exact annual-report pages are recorded in the source register.

The Patna cuttings show why the scale of a continuity statement matters. The 1955–56 report, printed p. 22, records a CE 600–1600 gap across four sampled areas. Occupation elsewhere in the city needs to be mapped before the whole city can be described as abandoned during that interval. At Rajgir, the primary report's 245 ± 105 BC result uses an old radiocarbon convention. We retain it as reported and leave the calendar onset unfilled. Multan's old fort sequence remains an acquisition and dating problem in this review; nearby early sites supply a regional context.

Lumbini separates habitation from sacred-place use particularly clearly. The primary shrine study dates timber structures under the temple to the sixth century BCE. A UNESCO project account reports a c. 1300 BCE village a few hundred metres south. The older village lies near the younger edge of our later comparison interval; its occupation and its link to the modern municipality remain to be established. For Tilaurakot, the 2025 ICOMOS evaluation, pp. 133–134, records eighth-century BCE timber settlement, sixth-century urban development and gradual abandonment beginning in the first–second century CE. The Kapilavastu identification is a separate historical question.

At Anuradhapura, direct inspection of Coningham and colleagues' 1996 report, printed p. 79, gives 840–460 BCE for ASW2 Period K. The paper separately cites hunter-gatherer activity on the broader low mound as early as c. 4000 BCE. Those observations leave the intervening millennia unresolved. The UNESCO description records the capital's abandonment after 993 CE, while excavation and sacred-use histories have different limits. At Mahasthan, the published landscape and eastern-rampart sequence begin in the late fourth–third century BCE. Later habitation and religious use persist across changing occupied areas. Those dates belong to Mahasthan, separately from nearby Bogura city.

What the register adds

The main addition is a set of explicit joins and missing joins between ancient evidence and modern settlement. It adds no inscribed objects, faces, museum accessions or newly measured dates. The files preserve source-specific chronologies, known gaps and conflicting scopes so later work can add the missing evidence without rewriting the earlier record. DATA-UPDATE.md proposes the shared coverage changes for maintainer integration.

Twenty-eight requests in the first acquisition saved twenty-four local responses and recorded four failures. The supplementary acquisition obtained the three annual-report scans from public research deposits and a primary institutional bibliography. Some DOI responses were redirect or login pages; the register identifies them as such. Rights-unverified PDFs, pages and local page renders remain under D:. The validator checks source links, chronological consistency and hashes. Its result does not establish uninterrupted habitation or independent archaeological review.

The boldest supported reading is that a living settlement can cover part of a known Indus city and make its later history difficult to recover: Harappa provides the direct spatial case. The deciding test is a mapped series of dated occupation surfaces from about 1300 BCE through Early Historic and medieval periods beneath the modern town, obtained from existing excavation and construction records, permitted non-invasive survey and accessible stratigraphic sections. Comparable records outside Rupnagar's excavated mound would test whether its documented gap was local relocation or a wider break.
