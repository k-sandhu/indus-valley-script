# People and practices after the Indus cities

26 October 2026. Source review. GPT-6, Codex API workspace harness; exact model build unavailable. Verification tier: submitted.

The proposed explanation fits an established archaeological pattern: communities continued working, farming and exchanging goods while the urban system lost some of its most recognizable features. The change took different forms in Punjab, Sindh, Gujarat and the upper Ganges basin. Our review adds a source-linked account of those differences and of the processes that make later activity harder to identify. The most useful extension is to measure ordinary occupation and urban markers separately in the same dated deposits.

We reviewed twelve primary publications or excavator accounts and recorded sixteen observations in [evidence.json](evidence.json). Each row gives the original title, authors, date, URL, page or section, chronology, interpretation and source dependence. The [source register](sources.json) distinguishes complete local witnesses, preliminary reports and web text. This is a bounded review; it does not estimate regional population totals or an empirical probability of continuity.

## What can continue

| Historical question | Evidence that could answer it |
|---|---|
| Do people remain in a region? | Dated domestic activity, food production and burials across the transition; ancestry and mobility measurements for particular people |
| Does a practice continue? | Its materials and operational steps in dated production or use contexts |
| Does a settlement move? | Dated old and new locations within a surveyed catchment, with comparable recovery and sufficiently narrow chronology |
| Is one plot continuously occupied? | An occupation sequence whose potential breaks are resolved by stratigraphy and independent dates |
| Does an institution continue? | Repeated arrangements of production, exchange or administration, including diagnostic intermediate evidence |
| Does a language or writing system continue? | Dated linguistic or graphic evidence that identifies a transmission relationship |
| Can we detect the activity? | Evidence that the relevant deposits survive, lie within the sampled depth and were recovered by a suitable method |

These questions have different answers at the same place. Farming can continue while an administrative system changes. People can move while their regional population persists. A town can return to an old mound after centuries. We therefore avoid using one continuity label for every historical scale.

The project's [11 October continuity study](../../2026-10-11-early-indian-continuity/REPORT.md) and [25 October crop review](../../2026-10-25-crop-continuity/REPORT.md) already support selective practical inheritance as a model to test. Meadow and Kenoyer had described the distinction between shared urban traits and older regional farming traditions in 1997. Here we compare marker changes with their archaeological visibility, including regional differences concealed by a single transition date. [Meadow and Kenoyer 1997, p. 139](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer1997_Excavations%20at%20Harappa%201994-1995%20New%20Perspective.pdf).

## Regional sequences

**Punjab.** Harappa's excavators describe regional exchange and craft activity in Cemetery H levels, approximately 1800 to perhaps 1500 BCE, while script and standardized weights cease there. Their provisional phase table has a different uncertain endpoint from the prose. The practical result is local activity after urban reorganization, with the final chronology still requiring context-level resolution. [Kenoyer and Meadow 2004, pp. 202–203](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer2004_Fifty-five%20years%20of%20Archaeological%20Research%20in%20P.pdf), E02.

**Sindh.** At Chanhu-daro, the original excavation report distinguishes later Jhukar seals or amulets, many round, from the familiar inscribed seal forms. Mackay records no clear script in this particular group. He also describes brick removal and disturbed upper deposits. We retain the relative sequence without adopting its legacy ethnic interpretations or assigning a new calendar date to the assemblage. [Mackay 1943, pp. ix and 143](https://www.panjabdigilib.org/webuser/searches/displayPageContent.jsp?CategoryID=1&ID=2920&Searched=W3GX&page=1), E04–E05. Modern identification must allow changed forms and exclude residual older objects before claiming continued manufacture.

**Gujarat.** Dholavira makes the transition particularly clear. Bisht's preliminary report assigns Stage VI to about 1950–1800 BCE. After a short desertion, a smaller settlement retained rectangular inscribed seals and cubical chert weights while pottery and housing changed. A longer break precedes Stage VII, assigned about 1500–1450 BCE, with circular houses and absent urban attributes. These are provisional excavator phase assignments. [Bisht 2015, §§5.6–5.7, pp. 103–104](https://archive.org/details/dholavirareportpreliminaryjan2015_860_K), E06–E07. Stage VI spans 1900 BCE; its envelope alone cannot date an individual seal or weight after that year or exclude residual objects. The report's persistence claim calls for primary contexts and absolute dates to establish each marker's final use.

**Haryana and the upper Ganges connection.** The familiar Bahola crop comparison retains summer and winter crops while their proportions change. The Jognakhera paper supplies posturban crop dates, including rice at 1517–1428 cal BCE, with the sample-to-phase joins still incomplete. [Petrie and Bates 2017, Table S23](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10963-017-9101-z), [Swaroop et al. 2026, chronology](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00334-026-01140-4), E13–E16. These are exposed observations from the preceding project audit.

A separate chemical analysis adds household evidence. Suryanarayan and colleagues studied 172 pottery fragments at seven sites. The small comparisons spanning urban and posturban contexts, chiefly Alamgirpur and Masudpur VII, did not detect a change in the reported lipid isotope variables. Product mixtures, small samples and incomplete local reference fats limit the interpretation of particular foods. This supports a test of practical retention, with equivalence still to be measured. [Suryanarayan et al. 2021, §4.4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7829615/), E12.

Alamgirpur also preserves a useful disagreement. Its excavators report possible ceramic overlap and no clear stratigraphic gap, yet state that available AMS dates suggest a protracted gap; seven dating submissions failed. The report therefore leaves calendar continuity unresolved. Burj supplies a separate case with an explicit occupation break. [Singh et al. 2013, pp. 53–54](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283348204_Recent_Excavations_at_Alamgirpur_Meerut_District_A_Preliminary_Report), E14–E15. These cases belong in any comparison sample alongside apparently continuous sequences.

## Why some continuities become hard to see

There are two mechanisms to test. An activity can persist while its diagnostic objects change, or its deposits can become inaccessible to a particular survey. The first concerns ancient behavior; the second concerns the surviving record and our observation of it.

Weight history illustrates the first mechanism and its limits. Kenoyer's specialist chapter relates urban and Early Historic metrology but explicitly identifies the intermediate relationship as unfinished research. The interval to about 600 BCE needs dated evidence. Dholavira's later weights qualify a broad disappearance statement without filling that entire interval. [Kenoyer 2010, pp. 117 and 120](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer%202010%20Measuring%20the%20Harappan%20World.pdf), E03.

Landform history illustrates the second. Giosan and colleagues distinguish exposed, well-preserved parts of upper Sindh from southwestern alluvial settings where later sediment could cover earlier settlements. Their study supplies a spatially specific burial mechanism. It does not establish how many settlements lie beneath the deposits. [Giosan et al. 2012, “Settlement Dynamics in the Harappan Domain”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3387054/), E08–E09.

Neogi and colleagues recover buried soils and former raised settlement settings at four northwest Indian sites. Modern agriculture and landscape change obscure some of that local topography. These sampled profiles show why a flat present surface can be a poor guide to ancient occupation. A buried soil's presence and a buried settlement's presence remain separate observations. [Neogi et al. 2020, discussion and supplement](https://doi.org/10.1017/qua.2019.70), E11.

Survey history adds another filter. Green and Petrie find uneven coverage and regional differences in settlement distributions. Their proposed northeastern reoccupation requires revisiting older records and surveying gaps. A map of changing site counts cannot by itself identify the people who moved. [Green and Petrie 2018, Discussion and Conclusions](https://doi.org/10.1080/00934690.2018.1464332), E10.

Later towns over older deposits are a further hypothesis to investigate through permitted construction archives, cores and sections. A modern town's name, proximity to an ancient mound or long-lived sacred reputation cannot resolve a buried occupation sequence. The atlas should record the resolution of the actual sequence and any hiatus for each place.

## Assessment of the theory

| Version of the explanation | Present assessment |
|---|---|
| People and local practices persisted through urban reorganization | Supported in named posturban sequences; the regional balance of retention, movement and population loss remains uneven |
| Diagnostic urban objects changed or stopped being deposited | Supported, with different endpoints by region and object class |
| All Indus writing and cubical weights stopped everywhere in 1900 BCE | Dholavira's reported persistence into a reduced settlement challenges this conventional cutoff; exact last-use dates remain unresolved |
| Burial, erosion and survey history conceal some activity | Supported mechanisms with local observations; hidden totals remain unmeasured |
| Every former city remained occupied on the same plot | Contradicted by reported breaks, including Dholavira; unresolved elsewhere |
| A writing tradition moved entirely onto perishable material | A testable possibility without direct support in this reviewed sample |
| The same language, political structure or self-identified civilization continued unchanged | Requires evidence beyond the practical and settlement observations reviewed here |

The [next study](TEST-DESIGN.md) measures ordinary occupation, marker occurrence and detection separately. It reserves independent sites, includes interrupted sequences, freezes chronological and recovery rules before confirmation, and gives each proposed continuity a rejection condition. The present source and integrity checks establish a usable evidence register. They do not replace independent archaeological review.

The boldest reading supported here is that the Indus urban system fragmented while regional communities continued and changed their practical traditions; its archaeological boundary partly records the end of a shared urban material system. The deciding test is a recovery-controlled set of independently dated household and production sequences across that boundary, with the final appearances of seals, writing, weights and building forms measured in the same deposits.
