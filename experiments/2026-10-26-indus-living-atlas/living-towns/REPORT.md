# Living towns and Indus-period occupation

Harappa and Rupnagar are the two living-settlement candidates in this register whose local archaeological sequences overlap the Mature Indus period. Harappa's modern town covers part of the ancient site, making the buried history beneath its streets a direct target for continuity research. Rupnagar's published site card records a break after its Harappan phase. We found no complete, source-audited sequence demonstrating uninterrupted occupation from the Indus period to the present in these sixteen candidates. That is the result of this bounded search, with the missing intervals identified for each place.

The [evidence table](TABLE.md) compares sixteen candidates in India, Pakistan, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, plus Dholavira and Mohenjo-daro as abandoned-city comparisons. [towns.json](towns.json) supplies the dates, evidence places, modern civic identities, spatial relations, gaps and source IDs for the atlas. [sources.json](sources.json) records twenty-four sources and their access depth. The [protocol](PROTOCOL.md) fixed the candidate list and coding rules before the new search. This is a purposive comparison of relevant places, not an exhaustive ranking of South Asia's oldest towns.

## What counts as persistence

We distinguish the date of first habitation at a place, its growth into a city, later occupation in the same area and continuation of the same bounded settlement. Each requires different observations. A modern municipal boundary can contain several ancient settlements, and a name can survive a relocation. A sacred place can remain in use while residential settlement contracts or moves. The register identifies the place to which each date belongs and puts earlier neighbouring evidence in a separate field.

The comparison intervals are 2600–1900 BCE for Mature Indus and 1900–1300 BCE for later occupation. Overlap denotes contemporary habitation; it does not assign Harappan culture to every contemporary settlement. BCE values are positive numbers with the older bound first. Broad archaeological phases, approximate onsets and uncalibrated radiocarbon results retain their different meanings. A `no` overlap label describes the reviewed local sequence; earlier deposits can remain unexamined. All main map pins are geographic index points with 5 km stated precision, suitable for a regional view. They cannot locate trenches or establish a shared ancient and modern footprint.

## The most informative places

Harappa offers the strongest next test of occupation concealed by a living town. Kenoyer's 1991 excavation chapter places approximately one quarter of the archaeological site beneath the modern town, on printed p. 57. The [acquired chapter](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Harappa1986-90_04_Kenoyer-UrbanProcess_1.pdf) supports that spatial relation directly. James and colleagues' [2025 study](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2024.196) reviews occupation from the fourth millennium through about 1300 BCE and uses a broad statement about continuous occupation. Neither the analysed chronology nor that statement supplies the missing later occupation series. Harappa remains a strong candidate with a specific gap: the dated history between the Late Harappan phases and the modern town.

Rupnagar makes the continuity problem testable. The [ASI site card](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/84884.pdf), item 146, dates Period I to 2000–1400 BCE and the following Painted Grey Ware period to 1000–700 BCE, and explicitly identifies a break. A broader town-scale history could still find contemporary occupation in another part of Rupnagar. That possibility needs a mapped sequence outside the excavated mound.

Vadnagar and Peshawar illustrate long occupation that starts later. The [Vadnagar nomination](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6629/) describes seven successive cultures beneath the living town; [Sarkar et al. (2024)](https://repository.ias.ac.in/141198/) report a settlement sequence beginning about 2754 calibrated years BP in their abstract. We retain an approximate 800–750 BCE anchor. The proposed 1400 BCE extension needs the primary dated samples and their contexts. At [Gor Khatri in Peshawar](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/7035/), the 2026 State Party submission reports about 2300 years of stratified occupation. Both places can demonstrate substantial later persistence without filling the Indus-to-modern interval.

## How the other candidates change the question

Taxila's early history belongs to a [serial landscape](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/139/) with Saraikala, Bhir, Sirkap and Sirsukh. Bhir's sixth-century BCE city date is kept apart from Saraikala's prehistoric phases. At Srinagar, [Burzahom](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/5917/) establishes third- and second-millennium BCE habitation in the region; the city's own beginning and the intervening settlement links remain unresolved. The older history of Pandrenthan also requires its own spatial record. These are promising landscape histories, with several places to join.

Varanasi's [ASI Rajghat catalogue](https://asi.nic.in/admin/publications/ePublicationReportsDetails/download/51), Introduction pp. xi–xii, reports the older c. 800 BCE chronology and suggests extending it back by 400–500 years. We preserve both assertions. Nearby Akatha, Ramnagar and Sarai Mohana require separate dated records. Mathura's annual excavation report assigns its local earliest Period I to the sixth–fourth century BCE. Ujjain's report dates the earliest fortified phase at Garh-Kalika to c. 700–500 BCE. A ceramic tradition's general age cannot replace the excavator's local chronology. The exact annual-report pages are recorded in the source register.

The Patna cuttings show why the scale of a continuity statement matters. [The 1955–56 report](https://asiaurangabadcircle.com/assets/files/publications/1955-1956.pdf), printed p. 22, records a CE 600–1600 gap across four sampled areas. Occupation elsewhere in the city needs to be mapped before the whole city can be described as abandoned during that interval. At Rajgir, the primary report's 245 ± 105 BC result uses an old radiocarbon convention. We retain it as reported and leave the calendar onset unfilled. Multan's old fort sequence remains an acquisition and dating problem in this review; nearby early sites supply a regional context.

Lumbini separates habitation from sacred-place use particularly clearly. [The primary shrine study](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X00049899) dates timber structures under the temple to the sixth century BCE. A [UNESCO project account](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/earliest-buddhist-shrine-south-asia-discovered-lumbini-buddhas-birthplace-nepal) reports a c. 1300 BCE village a few hundred metres south. The older village lies near the younger edge of our later comparison interval; its occupation and its link to the modern municipality remain to be established. For Tilaurakot, the [2025 ICOMOS evaluation](https://whc.unesco.org/document/221034), pp. 133–134, records eighth-century BCE timber settlement, sixth-century urban development and gradual abandonment beginning in the first–second century CE. The Kapilavastu identification is a separate historical question.

At Anuradhapura, direct inspection of Coningham and colleagues' 1996 report, printed p. 79, gives 840–460 BCE for ASW2 Period K. The paper separately cites hunter-gatherer activity on the broader low mound as early as c. 4000 BCE. Those observations leave the intervening millennia unresolved. The [UNESCO description](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/200/) records the capital's abandonment after 993 CE, while excavation and sacred-use histories have different limits. At [Mahasthan](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6671/), the published landscape and eastern-rampart sequence begin in the late fourth–third century BCE. Later habitation and religious use persist across changing occupied areas. Those dates belong to Mahasthan, separately from nearby Bogura city.

## What the register adds

The main addition is a set of explicit joins and missing joins between ancient evidence and modern settlement. It adds no inscribed objects, faces, museum accessions or newly measured dates. The files preserve source-specific chronologies, known gaps and conflicting scopes so later work can add the missing evidence without rewriting the earlier record. [DATA-UPDATE.md](DATA-UPDATE.md) proposes the shared coverage changes for maintainer integration.

Twenty-eight requests in the first acquisition saved twenty-four local responses and recorded four failures. The supplementary acquisition obtained the three annual-report scans from public research deposits and a primary institutional bibliography. Some DOI responses were redirect or login pages; the register identifies them as such. Rights-unverified PDFs, pages and local page renders remain under D:. The validator checks source links, chronological consistency and hashes. Its result does not establish uninterrupted habitation or independent archaeological review.

The boldest supported reading is that a living settlement can cover part of a known Indus city and make its later history difficult to recover: Harappa provides the direct spatial case. The deciding test is a mapped series of dated occupation surfaces from about 1300 BCE through Early Historic and medieval periods beneath the modern town, obtained from existing excavation and construction records, permitted non-invasive survey and accessible stratigraphic sections. Comparable records outside Rupnagar's excavated mound would test whether its documented gap was local relocation or a wider break.
