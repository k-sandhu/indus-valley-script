# Enclosures, streets and rooms change on different schedules

The eastern and Gujarat cases support a history in which a settlement can retain its street framework while households occupy former open space, repair walls into a lane, or change the use of a gate. They also preserve episodes of larger reorganization. At Banawali, enclosure followed an open settlement, and a subsequent rebuilding introduced a divided town and radial streets. At Kanmer, a fort and aligned houses followed a small earlier occupation, then later buildings filled an open space without changing the prevailing orientation. These are different sequences, each recoverable from named architectural parts and local stratigraphy.

We register 118 source-qualified assertions from ten held excavation-author or project reports across seven towns. The [observation register](observations.json) preserves the phase label, source and page, evidence class, dating basis, uncertainty and missing reason for each assertion. Its [quantitative subset](quantitative-table.json) retains the values and units with the area, feature or excavation operation to which each applies. A count of rooms in a partly excavated complex remains a count of exposed rooms. Several successive room sets cannot be added as if they stood together.

## Replanning can adopt a curving boundary

Banawali supplies the clearest sequence of larger reorganization in this module. Bisht describes an initially open IA settlement, an IB boundary wall that needed strengthening, and an IC phase in which earlier houses were razed and replaced. The widened inherited fortification shaped the newly divided town. The lower-town streets radiated from points in the enclosure, while the citadel retained rectilinear sectors. The later Period II rebuilt houses again and maintained the broad inherited outline through six or more building phases. This is direct evidence against treating a radial layout as a sufficient indicator of gradual uncoordinated growth. Its shape was part of an explicit rebuilding account. The selected passages are printed pp. 15–20 of [Bisht's paper in *Puratattva* 29](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/85200.pdf).

The report also distinguishes access and sanitation. Five streets converge at the east-gate piazza, and the gate's drain was rebuilt five times. The older IAR 1986–87 summary records at least three rebuilding phases in the gate tie-wall and drain and three bathing platforms along 20 m of citadel Street 11. The increased phase count is a change in published coverage. We preserve each report date. Cart ruts occur in one outer street near the citadel apex; the proposed restriction of traffic elsewhere remains the excavator's inference from exposed surfaces and entrances.

Kalibangan likewise defeats a single alignment label. The IAR 1967–68 account joins the earliest lower-city enclosure with the beginning of Harappan occupation, records nine corresponding building/road levels, and observes that houses nearer the perimeter follow the perimeter while those nearer the streets follow the streets. The source leaves the precise explanation open. Local adjustment and possible changes of street alignment are both discussed on printed p. 44. The broad lower-city plan therefore needs a geometric model with more than one reference line. Thapar's original scan describes four established N–S arterials and three E–W streets, with an indication of a fourth. The east–west lanes are staggered and do not cross every north–south arterial. A diagram of an ideal square grid would erase that distinction.

## Framework retention accommodates local construction

Kanmer's central open space is a particularly useful test case. The IIa report describes a partly exposed 7 × 5 m space, with successive earlier floors shifting toward it. In IIb the space was filled by structures. The orientation stayed the same while buildings widened. The report also observes later III buildings against or on the rampart and removal of fortification stone for housing. These relations distinguish at least three things: a retained directional framework, occupation of previously open ground, and changed treatment of the enclosure. The observations are on pp. 363–365 of [Kharakwal and colleagues' original report](https://nichibun.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/1259/files/symp_016__357__355_376__357_378.pdf). The source does not establish whether open-space occupation was piecemeal, centrally ordered, negotiated, or one concerted rebuilding. Those alternatives need a finer construction sequence.

Farmana records comparable local changes with more detailed feature names. The East Area Lane 1 acquired a 1.50 × 1.43 m bathing platform at its western exposed end (p. 70, Fig. 5.38). Structure 11A later occupied the western end of East Area Lane 2, whose earlier width was 1.90 m (p. 78). In the Central Area, Structure 3I acquired a 55 cm external strengthening wall exposed for 2.10 m into street space (p. 117, Fig. 5.88). These are three source-local instances; similarly numbered lanes in different areas remain distinct. They fit the same report's account of continued broad orientation between IIA and IIB without requiring every boundary or access route to remain fixed. The report's interpreted eight-household Complex 3 is a useful candidate for further access analysis, but eight families were not directly counted. The surviving room, hearth, pit and entrance evidence should remain available independently of that social attribution. [Final excavation report](https://archive.org/details/excavationsatfarmana200608vshindeetal_212_v).

At Surkotada, the IA rampart was reused in IB with reinforcement; the residential entrance shifted, and the later IC rebuilding used substantial stone defences. Within IC, an intercommunicating passage was subsequently partly blocked. These observations permit a history of changing circulation without supposing that the whole settlement changed its orientation or government at each repair. The early and late lane widths in this module refer to different source contexts. They cannot be treated as a before/after narrowing of the same lane until a plan identity join is made. The main witnesses are [Joshi's report](https://archive.org/details/masi87excavationsatsurkotadaandkutchjagatpatijoshi_841_t), printed pp. 44–50 and 58, and *Indian Archaeology 1971–72*, pp. 13 and 16–17.

## Materials and named structures need their own comparison

Farmana and Rakhigarhi rely mainly on mud brick for walls and use fired brick in wet installations. Banawali's defences are moulded brick in mud mortar, while its bathing platforms and drainage use fired brick. Kanmer's enclosure and houses combine local stone and clay; Shikarpur changes from predominantly mud-brick construction in the first excavated phase toward greater use of local stone in the second. These are source descriptions of allocation and change. They do not provide comparable fractions of total wall volume. A material similarity score should first compare the same function, phase and available geological resources, then ask which similarities remain.

Counts require equally careful units. At Rakhigarhi the draft describes ten small blocks in an L-shaped structure called a granary and seven rooms in a neighboring complex called a shopping arcade. We register the observed blocks and rooms, and separately retain the proposed functions. Barley in accumulated cell earth is relevant evidence, but the report does not by itself settle primary storage versus later fill. At Kalibangan, massive platforms and burning installations likewise remain architectural observations alongside the excavator's ritual interpretation. This makes the table useful to later reassessment of building functions. [Nath's Rakhigarhi draft](https://archive.org/details/excavationsatrakhigarhi19972000amarendranath_457_Z), pp. 118–121.

Shikarpur demonstrates why excavation coverage changes the comparison. Nine small trenches in the first renewed season establish successive materials and repair relations but do not yield a street-network census. The report explicitly could not reach the southern fortification base. Its approximately 3.4 ha mound is a different quantity from the nominal 225 m² laid out for trenches, and actual exposed area varies with depth. The observed thin upper structures could reflect changed investment, destruction, sampling or a combination. [Bhan and Ajithprasad's first-season report](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Excavations-at-Shikarpur-2007.pdf), pp. 3–6.

## Dating and inscription joins that change the inference

The Kalibangan 1975 date ranges and Surkotada 1990 arithmetic BC dates cannot be plotted as equivalent to modern calibrated BC intervals. Farmana provides a further lesson inside one volume. Shinde's chronology chapter proposes an early start and an IIA–IIC timetable. Osada's conclusion says the sampled lower pottery and AMS evidence instead point to Mature Harappan occupation, while leaving earlier settlement possible. The AMS chapter specifies OxCal 4.1 and IntCal09. We retain that disagreement and the laboratory ranges. No new occupation-duration model has been fitted.

One Farmana sample is especially consequential for joining buildings to inscriptions. Table 13.1 on p. 832 identifies PLD-12422 as Trench 1E1/Structure 3N. Table 13.2 on p. 834 assigns the sample to Gupta, with a rounded age of 1390 ± 20 BP and a 95.4% range of AD 616–666. This result requires a deposition and sampling relation; the shared room identifier cannot date the Harappan construction or all finds assigned to that room. Similar care applies to the steatite seal reported from different floor levels in one Kalibangan room, the matching Kanmer impressions, and Shikarpur's Phase I sealings. An administrative office cannot be identified by counting inscriptions and attaching a building-function name without checking coeval deposition and recovery.

## Coverage and reproducibility

All ten source assets were already held. The source register recalculates their SHA-256 hashes and gives both logical checkout paths and physical D: paths. No third-party source, plan or image is exported in this module. PDF text extracts and inspected page renders remain under `D:\projects\indus_valley_script\data\raw\2026-10-24-urban-form-and-inscription-ecology\eastern\`, with a derivative manifest. `build_register.py` rebuilds the two registers and quantitative subset, validates source references and unique assertion identifiers, and reports the seven-town coverage. This is an exploratory source integration and an author check, with no fresh confirmation test or meaning assignment.

The strongest supported reading is that durable urban frameworks and changing local construction were concurrent parts of Indus town histories. Larger rebuilding could preserve a curving inherited layout, while continued directional order could accommodate the loss of open space or a change in gate access. The deciding prospective test is to map and date the same named streets, open spaces and gates through successive construction episodes at Banawali, Kanmer and Farmana. Freeze the feature identities, restoration mask, phase joins and exposure area before measuring access loss, plot subdivision and orientation. Separately sampled construction dates would distinguish gradual household accretion from a coordinated rebuilding that merely produces the same final plan.
