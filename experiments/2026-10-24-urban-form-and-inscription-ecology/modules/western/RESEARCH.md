# Western and southern towns: rebuilding was part of planning

The five towns preserve different sequences of expansion, regularization, maintenance and street occupation. Their similarities become clearer when we compare named architectural episodes. Harappa absorbed settlement beyond earlier walls. Dholavira removed a residential extension to create a large public ground, enlarged its enclosure, and later allowed buildings to occupy street space. Mohenjo-daro's reconstructed house plans became more closely aligned with its streets. Lothal retained much of its street framework across rebuilding before encroachments appeared. At Chanhudaro, newly exposed early mud-brick quarters already combine streets, houses and specialized production. These sequences establish that planning continued through the life of settlements. A grid visible in the latest plan cannot stand for their entire occupation history.

We recorded 126 assertions from eleven sources in [observations.json](observations.json). Every assertion retains a source ID, locator, phase, evidence status, dating basis and uncertainty. Ten source records have checked local SHA-256 hashes. Kenoyer's 2010 chapter was read through the web PDF; its direct download returned HTTP 403, so its local path and hash remain empty with that reason. The new acquisitions and failures are recorded in `data/raw/2026-10-24-urban-form-and-inscription-ecology/western/acquisitions.json`, physically on D:. Acquired PDFs, HTML, archive OCR, extracted text and rendered source pages remain local-only. The module contains authored observations and pointers to them.

## Dholavira: coordinated clearance and later encroachment

Bisht's preliminary January 2015 report provides the most explicit architectural sequence in this module. The first walled settlement lies beneath the later castle. A section across its southern wall records an 11 m basal width and about 6.5 m surviving height. Stage II adds 2.80 m of brick masonry on the inside and repeated clay plastering. A residential extension develops to the north. Stage III adds about 4.5 m inward to the earlier defence and converts the nucleus into the castle. The northern housing is cleared for the multipurpose ground, a bailey is added to the west, and a walled town is founded farther north. Following damage, repairs accompany addition of the lower town and eastward enlargement of the outer enclosure. These are source phase relations, not new wall measurements. See `W-DHO-006` through `W-DHO-014` and Bisht pp. 95–100, Figs. 5.1–5.3.

The sequence supplies a concrete case of settlement growth followed by large-scale rearrangement. It does not reveal how the Stage II households originally chose their plots, so we leave their degree of individual coordination open. The existence of earlier housing beneath a later planned space is stronger evidence than a judgment that an irregular plan simply looks organic.

The lower town preserves the reverse direction. Fourteen small streets are reported, with lanes around 1.2–1.7 m wide and two by-lanes 0.90 m wide. The report describes encroachments probably belonging to later Stage V. One example is explicit: a Stage V wall occupies part of an originally 5.25 m wide north-south street in Trench 15x24. House-front platforms also reduce available street width. Their proposed uses for sitting, entertainment or trade remain alternatives. Short drains discharge to soakage jars near the sub-streets. These observations support changes in access and maintenance at the neighborhood scale; they do not by themselves prove a collapse of political authority. See `W-DHO-017` through `W-DHO-025`, pp. 134–137.

Materials and water provision distinguish Dholavira's local solution. Its early construction combines bricks and dressed or undressed stone. The lower town uses rubble and ashlar masonry in mud mortar, with some mud bricks. Reservoirs are fitted around the built-up divisions and the sloping ground. Page 158 revises the earlier reservoir-area estimate from about 17 ha to about 10 ha within a roughly 49 ha city. The report also says on p. 33 that the fortified settlement occupies about half of more than 70 ha of ruins including the cemetery. We preserve both descriptions as an unresolved area-scope discrepancy. A future area calculation needs a mapped polygon for each definition, rather than selecting a convenient city size.

The report's chronology is an approximate model: Stages I to III span about 3000–2500 BCE, IV about 2500–2100, V about 2100–2000, VI about 1950–1800, and VII about 1500–1450. Stage VI follows a short gap and contracts into part of the earlier city, with some walls reused and a new northern limit. Stage VII follows a longer gap of unknown duration and uses circular houses. This is a sequence with interruptions and changes of scale. Adding the endpoints into one uninterrupted occupation duration would erase that evidence.

## Harappa: growth across boundaries, then new boundaries

Harappa developed on a low Pleistocene terrace between the Ravi and ancient Beas systems. The excavation project's mature settlement estimate is around 150 ha. Its Kot Diji settlement already exceeded 25 ha and had streets, houses and perimeter walls built with mud bricks of 1:2:4 proportions. The earlier Ravi architecture includes wooden posts and plastered reed walls. The changing material record therefore belongs in the history of urban formation, rather than being treated as a fixed town attribute. The revised manuscript cited as `W-S03` retains its own earlier chronology, including a Ravi start earlier than 3900 BC; that value is a historical source assertion, not our preferred calibrated boundary.

The clearest growth relation comes from Kenoyer's 2021–22 synthesis, p. 9. People lived outside the Mound E wall before the city enclosed the expanded settlement. Mound F, north of Mound AB, followed a comparable sequence. The same passage leaves walls around Area J, western habitation on former dump areas, and modern Harappa Town unresolved. These are distinct boundary histories within one settlement. See `W-HAR-014` through `W-HAR-016`.

The growing city also maintained differences between narrow access points and broader internal movement. Kenoyer's 2010 chapter gives a 2.8 m southern Mound E gateway and a 5 m street inside it, and an eastern gateway around 2.6 m wide. These dimensions support controlled passage at an entrance while circulation remains wider inside. They do not identify who controlled that passage. The same chapter records changes from approximately 7×14×28 cm fired bricks in selected Period 3B gateway structures to about 5×12×24 cm in late structures. These measurements show that shared brick proportions did not require constant absolute dimensions. Reuse of old fired bricks further complicates a date inferred from brick size alone.

The Late Harappan sequence includes houses and workshops occupying street space. The proposed overcrowding or refugee explanation is separate from the observed encroachment. The source chronology extends occupation beyond the mature 2600–1900 BCE phase into transitional and later occupations. We preserve these intervals and uncertainty about the terminal date; 1900 BCE is not a universal abandonment boundary.

## Lothal: rebuilding preserves some rules and changes others

Rao's p. 85 explicitly leaves the Phase I village's street plan unknown because mud buildings were destroyed and excavation was limited. The village already had a substantial mud bund. We can therefore observe flood protection while leaving the internal planning question unanswered. After damage, Phase II builders strengthened the perimeter and raised housing on mud-brick platforms. Seven blocks were traced, with houses on platforms approximately 4–12 ft high. The report says layouts were retained through reconstruction during Phases II and III.

The street account on p. 87 makes the later transition specific. It reports no street encroachment before Phase IV, encroachment in IV with attempts to retain alignments, and a different building pattern in V. Soakage jars and occasional small brick-bat drains continue during Period B. Civic facilities could persist while construction and access changed. Within the Acropolis, baths at the back of houses caused many drains to follow lanes; that differs from Jansen's description of street-facing drainage facilities at Mohenjo-daro. The relationship between house arrangement and drain placement is an appropriate comparison across the two towns.

The basin called the dock belongs to Phase IIa in Rao's account. Later work changes its inlet arrangements and closes drains through the western wall. Its function and its construction date require their own evidence. Rao's c. 2350 BC date is produced within an older model using cross-dating, radiocarbon anchors and assumed building-phase durations. It is not a dated foundation cut. The scan also lacks the plate volume, and pp. 86–87 reverse the stated directions of Streets 1 and 6. The prior town-phase study preserves the unresolved matching problem. We have not used either prose direction to assign a measured bearing.

## Mohenjo-daro: a changing neighborhood and a difficult city chronology

Jansen's well estimate and drainage discussion establish substantial investment in repeated water access and waste removal. The figure of at least 700 wells is an extrapolation from known well distributions. The Great Bath's roughly 1,700 m² building footprint differs from its approximately 12×7 m pool. Gypsum mortar and bitumen waterproofing belong to that particular structure. They should not become a blanket description of domestic building materials.

Jansen p. 190 supplies a useful way to reconstruct episodes. Interconnected drains can associate bathing floors and door sills even where their absolute heights differ. Three successive drain rebuilding phases in the DK-G deep excavation show that growth was uneven within a small area. A site-wide horizontal level cannot be assumed to represent one moment.

Green, Alam and Petrie's 2026 reanalysis supplies five DK-G South samples. Their published Gini coefficients decline from 0.39 to 0.23; median residence areas run 161.39, 117.06, 112.69, 98.02 and 141.13 m². Later houses align more closely with streets. The approximate century labels were assigned from an assumed occupation span, rather than independently dated. The same article leaves alternative residence groupings open. Table 1 prints `.044` for DK-G North while the prose gives `0.44`; its count relationships also remain unresolved. [table1-conflicts.json](table1-conflicts.json) preserves those discrepancies. The chart-ready [residential series](mohenjo-daro-residential-series.csv) contains the five published phase rows, with their sample sizes and date-model note.

## Chanhudaro: early urban organization beneath the familiar fired-brick plans

The old three-mound topography is affected by erosion and incision. It does not establish three contemporary administrative sectors. Mackay traced a large wall outside the principal exposure but explicitly left its function unresolved. Cultivation and a tomb enclosure stopped further tracing. An unexposed continuation cannot become evidence that the town had no substantial boundary works.

The newer excavation-team article by Marchand and colleagues reports about 4,200 m² of early mud-brick architecture exposed by 2020, expanded to 6,500 m² by 2022. At least seven blocks lie around a main street 5–7 m wide. Building A has twelve storage cells, while other complexes combine courts, houses and production. Tools, debitage and manufacturing equipment support the craft attribution. These are contextualized first-Indus-period quarters, placed around 2500–2300 BCE in the body text, with 2600/2500 as the broader initial bound in the abstract. The underlying radiocarbon determinations were not printed in the inspected account.

Didier and collaborators' July 2026 conference abstract reports still earlier mud-brick levels at Mound I, as well as a substantial retaining wall with a fired-brick facing. These preliminary observations enlarge the occupation sequence. They require the full sections, dates and construction relations before we can compare their layout with the exposed first-Indus-period quarters. The current evidence already makes Chanhudaro an inappropriate example of a town known only to begin as a finished fired-brick plan.

## Strongest supported reading and deciding test

The strongest reading is that an enduring street or enclosure framework could organize repeated local rebuilding while remaining open to later enlargement, clearance and occupation of public space. Harappa's new enclosures and Dholavira's residential clearance show coordinated interventions after settlement growth. Dholavira and Lothal then show street occupation within established layouts. Increasing alignment in one Mohenjo-daro neighborhood adds a different trajectory. This evidence supports several forms of coordination operating at different times and scales. It cannot identify their governing institutions from plan geometry alone.

The deciding prospective test is a matched sequence of adjoining blocks and streets in at least two towns. Before exposing new results, freeze the mapped excavation extent, alternative household groupings, wall-to-floor and drain joins, restoration mask, and phase assignments. Measure street width, door access, block boundaries, building alignment and the proportion of street surface occupied by later structures for each episode. Independently date short-lived material sealed by construction or resurfacing where possible. If apparent regularization and encroachment disappear when walls are assigned to coherent episodes, the sequence reading fails. If major circulation lines persist while plots and house fronts change, the measurements will distinguish durable shared infrastructure from a town constructed once to a final plan.
