# Recommendations from Indus researchers

This survey records **33 recommendation rows from 31 source records**. Its clearest shared direction is to join sign sequences to dated objects, regions, object types and archaeological contexts. The repository can act on that direction with its existing source-linked records, but it has not yet built the common register needed for a complete regional and chronological corpus. `recommendations.csv` is the sortable table; `recommendations.json` retains structured author lists and detailed access labels.

The YouTube page identifies Dennys Frenez’s talk as “Ongoing decipherments : Writing in the Indus Civilization.” The page and YouTube timed-text endpoint were throttled in the live search, and no transcript, event date, venue details or timestamps could be obtained. The four recommendations from the talk in the table therefore rest on the user’s notes and remain attributed to that summary, without invented timestamps. Frenez’s publications provide separately checkable recommendations on archaeological context, external finds and seal use: “The Lothal Sealings: records from an Indus Civilization town at the eastern end of the maritime trade limits across the Arabian Sea” (Frenez and Tosi 2005), “Private Person or Public Persona” (Frenez 2017), and “Seal and sealing with Indus script” (Frenez et al. 2022). These sources support contextual work on seals, sealings and external finds, but the search did not verify publications stating the reported ?unbalanced oligopoly,? a deliberate rejection of neighboring writing conventions, or every claim about indigenous invention in the same terms. Those remain the user?s notes on the talk.

## What researchers recommend

### Data and corpora

Wells and Fuls explicitly stress maintaining and updating a comprehensive corpus and sign list. Mahadevan’s concordance, the multi-volume *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions*, ICIT and regional graffiti databases show this as sustained work rather than a one-off proposal. Corpus improvement is widely shared. Published datasets still differ in record units, sign inventories, dating fields and access terms, and the project has not established one integrated object-to-image-to-context register. Wells’s 2015 preface further says regional variation and possible multiple languages need chronological control.

Frenez’s reported call for dated regional corpora fits the same direction. The present data can support a limited first register because the dataset landscape audit covers 5,680 Lipi records, 4,695 provisional families and 19,946 token positions, while the context audit identified 22 Harappa short-record objects and their 66 faces. These counts describe existing annotations; they do not represent independently verified objects or complete site coverage. The records still need reconciled identities, dates and source-specific missingness.

### Context and chronology

Kenoyer’s work on early pottery marks calls for better collation and publication across sites and periods. Wells argues chronology is needed to evaluate regional variation. Ameri and colleagues frame seals through production, use, identity and the social lives of their users and makers. Petrie’s settlement-contemporaneity study supplies a wider archaeological approach to chronology. Frenez’s published seal and sealing studies connect object form and find context with administrative practice. Together these recommendations support recording excavation phase, site, object class, physical use and observation source alongside signs.

Frequency-context recommendations are directly testable at descriptive scale when an object and site denominator are known. The requested adjustment for site size and type requires better denominators: excavation area, recovered assemblage, collection history and relevant artifact classes. No available source in this survey establishes a common exposure denominator for all sites. The sign-740 example remains the user’s summary of the lecture, not a verified number from a transcript.

### Linguistic approaches

Rao, Yadav, Vahia and Mahadevan recommend or demonstrate computational analysis of ordered sequences, prediction of missing signs, and structural testing. These proposals concern observable sequence properties. Mukhopadhyay proposes joining positional/co-occurrence regularities to document type, iconography and archaeological context; her semantic and administrative readings are hypotheses, not consensus. Farmer, Sproat and Witzel dispute that short, repetitive sign strings and statistical regularities establish a linguistic writing system. Sproat’s comparative work asks whether linguistic statistics distinguish language from non-linguistic systems. This is an active dispute, not a settled choice between language and non-language.

The repository already runs held-out restoration, sequence comparisons and non-linguistic controls. The 2026 contribution assessment recognizes Yadav et al. 2010’s deduplicated held-out restoration as a precedent, so new work should describe its exact object grouping, model comparison and target population rather than claim that prediction is new.

### Computational method

The published computational agenda includes more corpus-driven statistical and machine-learning work (Rao et al. 2009), missing-sign prediction (Yadav et al. 2010), and testing structural claims against comparison systems (Sproat 2014). The project has acted on these directions. Its current work also records where statistics fail to discriminate language from nonlinguistic administrative codes. No recommendation found here justifies assigning sounds or meanings from model fit alone.

### Collaboration and access

Frenez’s lecture summary asks for interdisciplinary teams. The research record reviewed here shows this in joint archaeological and computational publications, corpus projects, sealing studies and cross-institutional field projects. A specific field-wide team structure or shared annotation protocol was not established by this search. A practical project-level step is to prepare a reviewable object register with source image, transcription alternatives, dating, context and separate reviewer fields, then seek specialist review of a bounded block.

Dataset reuse is constrained by access and rights. The earlier landscape audit found that ICIT access is requested rather than bulk-open, and a single permission-cleared, openly downloadable, integrated corpus was not established. A dataset plan and schema exist, but no new integrated corpus has been populated. Keep third-party images and bulk rows local as described in `knowledge/provenance/REDISTRIBUTION.md`.

### New excavation and science

Rao’s 2026 interview says longer texts or bilingual inscriptions from future excavations could change the decipherment situation, and identifies a possible number-system investigation using stroke marks and archaeological weights. This depends on future excavation or a securely joined, independently measured object. The project has conditional numerical audits and defined acquisition targets, but they are not new excavation evidence. Green’s carving-topography approach and Ameri’s production/use agenda require microscopy, 3D surfaces, wear traces or experimental reference collections not present in current datasets.

External finds merit a distinct register because their find context, date, sign sequence and local imitation or adaptation all affect interpretation. Parpola’s borderlands corpus and Frenez’s Oman evidence supply published examples. Farmer, Sproat and Witzel’s account challenges whether the existing brief signs prove literacy, making external finds important to document while their status remains evaluated case by case.

## Priority actions with current data

1. **Populate a small context-linked object register from existing source audits.** Join current object IDs, surfaces, source images, alternative transcriptions, object classes, findspot and date fields, retaining missingness reasons. The schema and pilot plan already define these entities. This creates a base for regional, chronological and frequency comparisons and is feasible from local records, though some context fields will remain missing. Start with the 22 Harappa short records and selected Lothal, Susa, Oxus and South Indian source audits.
2. **Produce dated, regional, object-type frequency tables with explicit denominators and missingness.** Existing catalogue counts make sign frequencies testable. Separate deduplicated text counts, objects, impressions, surfaces and site/context coverage. Do not treat site-size adjustment as possible until exposure or assemblage denominators are observed.
3. **Run a bounded comparison of Tamil Nadu and Indus pottery graffiti under a declared morphology rule and matched chance baseline.** Current books, source records and regional analyses can define a candidate study. This would test graphic similarity; it cannot by itself establish later cultural transmission. The underlying 2025 matching procedure and complete specimen records need source review first.
4. **Add external finds to the same register while retaining a separate regional label and status.** Existing Oman, Susa and Oxus audits offer source-backed objects. Record candidate pseudo-inscriptions and contested joins separately, then compare their sequences and dates with core-site records.
5. **Prepare a specialist review block for interdisciplinary collaboration.** Existing ambiguity tables make a compact packet possible: object image locator, transcription alternatives, context, dating basis, and the exact question for archaeologists, epigraphers and computational researchers. This can improve data quality before a larger corpus effort.

Work requiring new excavation, bilingual discoveries, fresh 3D scans, toolmarks or residue tests remains a longer-term acquisition agenda. The research roadmap already ranks independent physical observations and context-linked samples as prerequisites for stronger external claims.

## Coverage and source limits

The requested roster is represented in the table, but several named scholars have no recommendation attributed because this search did not establish a precise primary-source recommendation and locator: Ratnagar, Possehl, Vidale, Bisht and Heather Miller. Those rows document the gap explicitly. Some other recommendations use article abstracts, snippets, or bibliography records, each labeled in `sources.json`; the reviewer should verify their exact page/section before treating them as a precise attribution. “Agreement” means a source supporting a similar methodological direction was located. It does not mean the field voted on a recommendation.

The verified publication years in this source set range from 2000 to 2026; some bibliography records still need direct date verification. No recommendation is stated as a field consensus unless more than one independent source supports a similar methodological direction. Semantic readings, language-family claims and the script-status question remain contested.
