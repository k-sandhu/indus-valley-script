# Round seals: catalogue joins, image checks, a shared-tradition test and the "twins"-initial count

Run of 12 October 2026 under the [frozen protocol](PROTOCOL.md) (hashes in [FREEZE-2026-10-12.json](FREEZE-2026-10-12.json)) and a [dated amendment](AMENDMENT-2026-10-12-before-computation.md) written before any test statistic was computed. Code hashes were recorded before the first run in [CODE-HASHES-before-run.json](CODE-HASHES-before-run.json). Tradition results come from [run-2026-10-12/tradition-summary.json](run-2026-10-12/tradition-summary.json), twins results from [run-2026-10-12/twins-summary.json](run-2026-10-12/twins-summary.json), and checks marked exploratory from [run-2026-10-12/exploratory.json](run-2026-10-12/exploratory.json), computed after the outcomes. Claude Opus 5.5 did the work; a Claude subagent served as the blinded second reader. This follows up Study B of the published-claim tests ([report](../2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/foreign-finds/REPORT.md)), whose files were read and not changed.

## Findings

**Round-seal texts share a sign repertoire, and do not share sign order.** For each of 28 circular seals found in West Asia, South Asia or without provenance, a Kneser–Ney bigram trained on the other round seals was compared with bigrams trained on the same amount of South Asian seal text, matched text by text in length, damage and edge structure. The round-seal model predicts the held-out seal 1.28 bits per scored transition better (95% interval 1.76 to 0.82 bits). In 1,000 null groups built from South Asian seals with the same structure, the difference ranged from −0.37 to +0.29 bits between the 5th and 95th percentiles, and no draw reached the observed value (p = 0.001, the floor for 1,000 draws). This meets the protocol's rule for a shared tradition. A unigram model, which uses sign frequencies and ignores order, reproduces 1.22 of the 1.28 bits. The share of a held-out seal's sign-to-sign transitions that also occur on other round seals exceeds the matched share by 2.8 percentage points (interval −0.1 to +6.5), which the null reaches in 27% of draws. Under the protocol's decision rule the shared element is therefore sign inventory. The Mohenjo-daro copper tablets, used as a positive control, show what a shared sequence tradition looks like with this measure: random sets of 29 tablets give a similar total advantage (median 1.32 bits) with a transition excess of 25 percentage points.

**The West Asian and South Asian circular seals do not predict each other.** A model trained on the 16 West Asian circular seals predicts the 9 South Asian circular seals 0.64 bits worse than matched South Asian models (Holm-adjusted p = 0.98). A model trained on the South Asian circular seals predicts the West Asian ones 0.41 bits better (interval 0.85 to 0.04), almost entirely through sign frequencies, with Holm-adjusted p = 0.080. Neither direction meets the decision rule. The West Asian circular seals among themselves give the strongest internal advantage, 1.59 bits (interval 2.35 to 0.86, Holm p = 0.003), again with no excess of shared transitions (−0.3 percentage points). Study B found both sets atypical under South Asian statistics. By this test they are two atypical sets that do not form one sequence tradition, which agrees with Study B's overlap count E4.

**The repertoire is weighted towards human figures and short strokes.** In an exploratory attribution, the "twins" sign 091 supplies 0.42 of the 1.22-bit inventory advantage from only 7 occurrences, the single human figure 090 0.24, the single stroke 001 0.24, sign 140 0.18 and the twelve-stroke group 055 0.13. Stroke-count signs together supply 0.34 bits. The 120 sign tokens of these round seals are 5.8% 091 against 0.03% in the matched South Asian seal pool, 6.7% 090 against 1.0%, and 9.2% 001 against 1.4%; the jar sign 740 falls from 10.0% to 3.3%. Merging all stroke-count signs into one symbol leaves an advantage of 1.09 bits, so the result does not rest on stroke-numeral coding alone.

**Four of the 22 West Asian texts with a known start begin with two human figures side by side, confirmed by two independent readers.** Reader 1 compared each Lipi code string, rendered in Lipi's Wells-coded font, with Laursen's drawings, Gadd's photographs and CISI photographs. Reader 2, given the images without codes and mixed with South Asian texts, classified the sign at each end of every inscription. The two readers agreed on every one of 28 items: 6 begin with the paired figures and 22 do not. The four West Asian texts are the Luristan seal, Karzakan seals nos. 8 and 11 and the Kalba seal. Among 2,122 unique complete South Asian texts, 34 begin with a human-figure sign; images exist for 15, of which the Kalibangan sealing K-69 and the Mohenjo-daro seal M-798 begin with the pair. The comparison 4 of 22 against 2 of 2,122 gives a one-sided Fisher p of 1.2 × 10⁻⁷. Admitting Lipi's readings for the three unimaged South Asian texts coded 091-initial gives 4 of 22 against 5 of 2,122 (p = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶). Two of the four West Asian seals are ones Laursen regards as pseudo-inscriptions: on Karzakan no. 11 a "relatively clear" twin sign stands before strokes that imitate writing, and on the Kalba seal the pair is the only sign. Without them the count is 2 of 19 (p = 0.00044). The paired figures also appear second on Gadd's no. 18, third on the Saar seal and, as two large figures, on the Ra's al-Jinz copper seal, which Lipi codes as one illegible sign.

**Twenty-five of Laursen's 27 Indus-inscribed Gulf Type seals are in the Lipi export, and their joins are recorded with evidence.** [joins.csv](joins.csv) joins 31 Lipi records to Laursen's Table 1, Gadd's catalogue, Laursen's Fig. 10 or a museum record. Fifteen joins rest on an identifier or citation: burial-mound numbers (BBM 18839, 14569, 20362), excavation numbers (U.8685, U.17649), Marshall and Mackay seal numbers embedded in Lipi's excavation field, Laursen's naming of M-1369 and M-1370, site codes and the Louvre record of the Susa cylinder. Sixteen rest on matching motif, outline or damage together with sign count and layout, five of them also on being the only candidate at their site. Laursen nos. 6 (a Qala'at al-Bahrain fragment) and 19 (the lower half of Gadd no. 5) have no Lipi record, and no. 28 bears a Linear Elamite inscription. The four unprovenanced round seals and the two-line unprovenanced seal are Laursen nos. 23–27, among them Gadd nos. 17 and 18 in the British Museum. Laursen's citation "pl. XCVI no 500" for his seal no. 5 is Mackay's seal number, which Lipi stores with M-415; CISI M-500 is a different two-sided circular seal outside Laursen's list.

**Only the Indus Valley seals have CISI identifiers.** Laursen's Group 1 joins to C-32, M-416, M-1369, M-417 and M-415, each confirmed by the CISI photograph filed under that siglum, and his Fig. 10k is M-1370. CISI 3.3 (2022) states that the Gulf, West Asia and new Dholavira finds belong to a final volume 3.4, which a search did not find in print, so the West Asian and Dholavira seals have no CISI number.

**The image checks found two outright code differences and several structural problems.** The 30 circular faces with a witness carry 137 Lipi sign positions: 63 agree with the witness, 46 agree with uncertain detail, 24 are uncertain, 2 differ and 2 are not shown. On the Susa circular seal the reading-initial sign that Lipi codes as illegible is drawn clearly as Wells 092, and on the Girsu seal an illegible position is the looped sign 200. The structural problems matter more than the substitutions. Lipi's sign order for Failaka no. 12 and Qala'at al-Bahrain no. 7 is the mirror image of the impressions, which on the Failaka fragment moves the reading start onto the break. Lipi codes the imitation strokes of Karzakan no. 11 as stroke numerals. Failaka no. 13 shows about seven signs on two levels where Lipi codes four. The Louvre's impression of the Susa cylinder shows a large hatched sign exactly where Lipi places the text's start and end. Of the 13 West Asian texts with a known start that both readers saw, Lipi's first sign agrees with the witness in 10, is uncertain in one (the worn Janabiyah seal) and falls at the opposite end of the impression in the two mirrored texts. The Susa circular seal is outside that set because Lipi codes its first sign as illegible; the drawing shows Wells 092 there.

## Relation to the earlier results and published accounts

Study B showed that round-seal texts are unpredictable under South Asian statistics and that the excess lies in the ordering of common signs. This package finds that the round seals resemble one another in which signs they use and not in how they order them. The two results fit together: a held-out round seal is poorly predicted by South Asian sign orders and equally poorly by the orders on other round seals, while its choice of signs (more human figures and short strokes, fewer jars) resembles theirs. The copper-tablet control shows that the transition measure detects a formulaic tradition at this sample size, so the absence of shared transitions among round seals is informative about recurring formulae and short repeated sequences, though not about longer-range regularities a bigram cannot see.

These observations bear on the accounts listed in Study B. Names or words in another language written with Indus signs predict a skewed repertoire if certain signs were chosen for their sound; recurring name elements or a fixed formula would also produce shared transitions, which are absent here. Imitation of Indus writing by carvers who did not read it predicts a repertoire weighted towards easily copied signs and no consistent order, which matches; removing the four texts identified as pseudo-inscriptions (S2) leaves the inventory effect unchanged, so if imitation is the explanation it extends beyond the texts Laursen recognised. A transcription convention that maps unfamiliar marks onto common codes would also concentrate the repertoire on strokes and figures, and the image checks found one such case. The cross-regional tests do not support treating the South Asian circular seals as products of the same sequence tradition as the Gulf seals, although the two sets use some of the same signs. The twins sign is concentrated at the start of West Asian texts. On two of the four seals it is the sign Laursen reads most clearly on a seal whose other marks he regards as imitation, a pattern consistent with its use as a recognisable emblem as well as with Vidale's and Laursen's patronymic reading. Parpola's earlier count, reported by Laursen, of four Near Eastern and three Indus Valley texts beginning with the twins sign is close to the counts here. None of these tests identifies a language, a sound value or a meaning.

## Methods

The input is Study B's normalized Lipi export (5,680 faces, Lipi commit `fc8813a5`, hashes checked at run time) parsed with Study B's `ffcore.py`. The T1 population holds every single-line SEAL:C text found in West Asia, South Asia or without provenance, with the duplicate Dilmun witness of Ur U.17649 removed: 16 West Asian, 9 South Asian (10 faces) and 4 unprovenanced objects, of which 28 have scorable events. The protocol's count of 43 round faces was a miscount; the export has 42.

The internal model for a held-out object is Study B's interpolated Kneser–Ney bigram over a fixed vocabulary of 713 symbols, trained on all other population texts except identical code strings; an implementation check against Study B's model class gave identical surprisals. The matched pool holds the 1,382 unique complete South Asian non-round seal texts. Each of 50 matched sets replaces every population text with a random pool text of the same length, carrying its damage mask and edge flags, so token and event counts are equal; the held-out object's partner is dropped. Δ is the internal surprisal minus the mean matched surprisal per scored event, averaged over an object's faces and then over objects. The unigram companion uses absolute-discounted target counts with the same smoothing; the transition measure counts sign-to-sign events attested in training. The null replaces the population with structure-matched pool texts and repeats the whole procedure (1,000 draws per test); intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps over objects (10,000 draws). T3a and T3b train on one region's circular seals and score the other's. Positive controls apply leave-one-out to random subsets of 29 texts with matched sets of their own lengths, against a 500-draw null built for the first subset.

For the twins test, a text's reading-initial sign is at the right end of the impression for right-to-left texts and at the left for Lipi's two left-to-right texts. A West Asian start counts as confirmed only when both readers identify two human figures side by side. Of the 22 West Asian texts, 13 were read by both readers. The Susa cylinder received its Louvre witness after Reader 2's set was built and was read by Reader 1 only; the other eight are non-round objects without a local witness. The amendment describes these nine together as non-round, which is wrong for the cylinder. None of the nine is coded 091-initial, and all count as unconfirmed. Reader 1 classified the 15 imaged South Asian texts; the 19 unimaged ones count as not 091-initial in the primary test, and the three among them that Lipi codes 091-initial enter the sensitivity. [verify.py](verify.py) re-parses the faces, rebuilds T1's matched windows from the saved selections and recomputes T1, its per-object values and intervals, the Holm adjustments and the Fisher tests without importing the analysis code or `ffcore.py`; all 14 checks pass ([verify-2026-10-12.json](verify-2026-10-12.json)).

## Limitations

The West Asian round seals number 16 and the South Asian circular seals 9, so the cross-regional tests have wide intervals, and the T3b point estimate (−0.41 bits) may reflect a real but small shared repertoire that this sample cannot confirm. Laursen's figures are drawings made after earlier publications, and Gadd's plates are 1932 halftones, so the checks test Lipi against published witnesses rather than against the objects. Reader 1 saw the Lipi codes while comparing; Reader 2 was blind to codes and to the hypothesis being tested but is a language model reading the same images. The nine non-round West Asian texts with a known start had no local witness, and the British Museum's photographs of the Ur seals could not be retrieved. The inventory measure depends on the granularity of the Wells sign list; merging stroke signs leaves the result, but other merges were not tried.

## Next tests

1. Obtain photographs of the Bahrain and Failaka seals (Kjærum 1983 and 1994, Al-Sindi 1999) and of the British Museum and Penn Museum Ur seals, then repeat the checks for the opening signs that rest on drawings.
2. Simulate imitation: generate texts by copying signs with the frequencies a Gulf carver would see on imported Indus seals, and ask whether the repertoire and ordering statistics measured here distinguish such texts from names written with chosen signs.
3. Test position and repertoire together on Gulf seals found since 2010, where the twins sign and the stroke-rich repertoire make specific predictions for new finds.
4. Check whether the 140–592 pair or sign 592 recurs on Mohenjo-daro objects outside the Lipi export, as a test of a local circular-seal formula.
