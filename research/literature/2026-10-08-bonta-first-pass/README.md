# Steven C. Bonta: recent work and lecture review

Reviewed 8 October 2026. This is a first-pass source review. It identifies Bonta's recent lecture series, explains the proposed route to readings and records several claims worth checking. It contains no new corpus computation or lexical matching experiment.

## Recent sources

The researcher publishes as **Steven C. Bonta**. His [YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@IVC_Decipherment_Steven_Bonta) contains twelve numbered lectures, uploaded between 26 October and 13 December 2025. The first ten appear as eleven months old in the channel view on the review date. Expanded watch-page descriptions give the dates below. Upload order differs from lecture order.

The series develops the proposal in [*A Partial Decipherment of the Indus Valley Script: Proposed Phonetic and Logographic Values for Selected Indus Signs and Readings of Indus Texts*, second edition](https://www.academia.edu/120435855/A_Partial_Decipherment_of_the_Indus_Valley_Script_Proposed_Phonetic_and_Logographic_Values_for_Selected_Indus_Signs_and_Readings_of_Indus_Texts_2nd_ed_) (2024). The [first edition](https://www.academia.edu/105163460/A_Partial_Decipherment_of_the_Indus_Valley_Script_Proposed_Phonetic_and_Logographic_Values_for_Selected_Indus_Signs_and_Readings_of_Indus_Texts) appeared in 2023. A separate [presentation text](https://www.academia.edu/118880153/Towards_a_Partial_Decipherment_of_the_Indus_Script_An_introduction_to_methodology_language_and_reading) identifies a talk at Aror University in April 2024 and labels itself a draft not for citation. The 2025 videos therefore explain an existing proposal, with later discussion of the language and evaluation criteria. This search did not locate a newer decipherment manuscript; it was not an exhaustive bibliographic census.

## How the argument works

Bonta separates inscriptions into recurrent fields before assigning values. In lecture 4, 36:27–39:00, he describes a common right-to-left order of P, M, C and T fields, allowing absent fields. Fish and oval signs cluster in M fields; jar and arrow signs commonly occur in terminal T fields. He proposes that M and T fields are abbreviated notation, while P and C fields and some other inscriptions represent words more directly. The examples are distributional observations; their proposed functions need a separate test.

Lecture 6 supplies the first phonetic hypotheses. Bonta interprets the M fields as quantities or measures. At 7:47–14:06, a staff-like sign becomes Sanskrit *pala*, a weight term also associated with straw, then *pāla*, protector, in other contexts. At 14:10–18:50, a recurrent pair becomes *mātrā*, measure or amount, with component values *ma* and *tra*. At 18:53–23:03, bow and bowman signs lead to *dhanu(s)*, bow, and *dhana*, wealth. These steps combine sign distribution, a pictorial identification, a selected language and lexical associations.

In lecture 7, 5:31–14:24, he searches Sanskrit words that fit several contexts surrounding an unknown sign. The proposed value *bhū* links *bhūdhana*, *bhūma* and *bhūpāla*. This intersection of contexts is more restrictive than finding one word that resembles one string. Its strength depends on how many candidates, sound variants and semantic categories the search permits. At 16:46–18:31, he allows aspirated, unaspirated, voiced and unvoiced alternatives. At 58:40–60:35, the initial reading *vasukara* becomes the Vedic name *Vasukra* after further lexical search.

Lecture 8 extends the values and proposed ligatures. At 31:14–33:55, a sign initially read *su* also permits other sibilants, vowel length and *o*, producing a reading *soma* from *su-ma*. At 64:35–66:29, a proposed *rāṣṭra* ligature contains the hypothesized *ra* and *tra* elements while its intervening consonant is unrepresented. These are concrete matching freedoms that a chance comparison must reproduce.

Lecture 9, 30:04–34:00, interprets terminal signs as abbreviated predicates expressing being, belonging or valuation. Lecture 10, 10:50–13:25 and 28:18–31:00, develops claims about deposits, temple treasuries, rulers and caste from the readings. Lecture 11, 14:49–24:03 and 29:28–34:09, proposes earlier forms and sound changes for words read as *atima* and *sahapati*. Those historical reconstructions depend on the proposed sign values.

## Assessment

The field analysis gives explicit structural claims that can be checked without accepting Sanskrit readings: which signs co-occur, where fields begin and end, whether signs substitute in comparable environments and which patterns vary by site or object type. This review has located the claims and examples. It has not reproduced their corpus counts or resolved every glyph against a catalogue.

One typological exclusion fails a direct source check. In lecture 5, 28:34–30:00, Bonta argues that noun compounding favors Indo-Aryan and excludes Dravidian, including a categorical statement at 29:39–29:48 that Dravidian languages disallow it. Krishnamurti's [*The Dravidian Languages*, chapter 6](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/dravidian-languages/nominals-nouns-pronouns-numerals-and-time-and-place-adverbs/A9F075594201FCBAEC481ED4018731A9) explicitly describes compound nominal stems whose head noun has a noun, adjective or verb modifier. Its reconstructed noun-plus-noun example is *kaṇ-ṇīr*, tear, literally eye-water. The categorical absence claim cannot support excluding Dravidian. Other typological claims, including reduplication and prefixing, require their own comparisons.

Bonta describes a deliberate validation step in lecture 7, 18:32–24:37: he developed readings with Mahadevan's concordance, then tried inscriptions in ICIT, reporting roughly a dozen to fifteen readable examples and a sign-identification correction. This deserves an object-by-object audit. The evidence needed is the pre-validation value table, the full attempted set, the handling of failed readings and a concordance showing which objects were new. Different editions can contain the same excavated objects; some later ICIT material can also be new. This review has not measured the overlap.

The phonetic case needs a baseline that uses the same lexical search and spelling tolerances. A dictionary intersection can look selective while still yielding matches often when many candidate values and repair rules are tried. Freeze the text set, sign inventory, candidate lexicon, admissible sound variants, ligature rules and acceptance criteria, then compare Bonta's mapping with permuted mappings. Count all attempted readings. No such calculation was run in this review, and the examples alone do not establish the proposed language or sounds.

The metrology hypothesis also needs a link between an inscription and an independently observed quantity. The project's [source and identifiability audit](../metrology/source_and_identifiability_audit.json) and [weight study](../../../experiments/2026-10-06-weight-metrology/REPORT.md) address the problems of unit scale, count and source context. A plausible weight term supplies a hypothesis; a text joined to a measured quantity could constrain it. None was established here.

Lecture 12 offers six screening questions: does a proposal cover recurrent material, account for script structure and typology, use distribution as well as shape, justify the language choice, produce plausible readings and acknowledge incomplete coverage? Bonta addresses several of these in the series. The checklist still needs controls for search flexibility, source overlap and predictions made before inspecting new outcomes. Partial coverage is compatible with a developing proposal, but does not by itself validate its values.

## Scope and possible follow-up

The review read the available automatic English captions fully for lectures 5, 6 and 12; read substantial selected passages in lectures 7 and 11; and checked selected passages in lectures 1, 3, 4, 8, 9 and 10. Lecture 2 was inventoried and its captions retrieved, without substantive linguistic review. The second-edition page text was consulted for the proposal and edition changes. Missing glyphs in that extraction prevent a complete sign-value audit. Automatic captions can misrender names and Sanskrit words, so exact glyph identification and disputed wording should be checked against the original figures and audio before a computational study.

A useful next study would first transcribe and freeze the field rules and anchor-sign identities, replicate their distributions, then test the lexical matching procedure against a defined chance model. This keeps each shared assumption visible and lets a result identify which part of the proposal it actually tests. Existing controls in [the cryptography memo](../../../knowledge/directions/cryptography.md) and [the physical-observations manuscript](../../../papers/003-physical-observations/manuscript.md) should guide that design. The tests remain proposed.
