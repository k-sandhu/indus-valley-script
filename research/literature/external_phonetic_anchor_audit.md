# External reading anchors: primary artifact audit

Prepared 2 October 2026. This audit found no inspected object bearing both a transcribed Indus-sign sequence and a legible cuneiform sequence with an established correspondence. It does not prove that no such object exists in an unexamined collection. Indus-style imagery, a borrowed seal shape, and wedge-shaped carving are not interchangeable with a second written text.

## Bibliographic corrections and publication status

**No.1 versus no.6.** Possehl2008 pp.141–142 identifies Gadd no.1 as the stamp with a possible foreign personal name. It reports Marchesi's 2007 personal-communication reading *Ka-lu-lim* or *Ka-lu-si* and says it could be Meluhhan; that origin remains an inference. Frenez2023 p.4 mentions a likely Meluhhan personal name on an Ur stamp, but footnote26 on p.30 cites Gadd pp.7–9, no.6. The original no.6 is a cylinder without that inscription. The most economical explanation is a citation mismatch. This is a source correction, not a decipherment result, and it must not be used to invent a second artifact carrying the same name.

**Hybrid Identities.** Frenez2018 p.190 bibliography records Frenez, Marchesi and Vidale's 2014 Stockholm conference presentation, *Hybrid identities: Seals with Indus traits and cuneiform inscriptions*. Frenez2023 notes the same conference paper. The [author's institution-hosted CV](https://www.unibo.it/it/ateneo/amministrazione-trasparente/consulenti-e-collaboratori/tabella-incarichi-attivi-di-collaborazione-e-di-consulenza-affidati-a-soggetti-esterni/last_years?format=cv&id=42247), dated10 July2024, p.7, lists the expanded *Indus-related seals with Sumero-Akkadian cuneiform inscriptions* under papers in preparation. Searches through2 October2026 did not retrieve a published full paper, DOI, or artifact catalogue under the expanded title. The CV establishes its 2024 status only; it cannot establish its current status by itself. We therefore cite the published illustrated chapters for object content rather than treating the unretrieved paper as published evidence.

**Recent alleged Ketu bilingual.** The [Swaveda article](https://www.swaveda.com/articles/the-seal-of-ketu-persian-gulf-glyptics-and-harappan-dilmun-diplomatic-grammar-1456/), dated18 September2026, claims a circular British Museum seal with *Ku-du/Ke-tu* and three Indus signs. It supplies neither inventory number, readable object image, exact *Puratattva* article/pages, nor a primary epigraphic edition. It cannot be connected to an identified artifact from that report alone. It is an unverified claim and contributes no reading anchor or sign values to this project.

**2026 Dilmun tokens publication.** [Laursen et al.2026, institutional primary abstract](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/1059790), *JNES*85(1), pp.115–145, DOI10.1086/739820, describes three tokens impressed by two seals with cuneiform legends naming Yagli-el and a divine servant title. It raises identity and kingship questions; the retrieved abstract supplies no Indus-sign sequence or aligned bilingual correspondence. The repository marks its full PDF embargoed until1 April2027. We did not retrieve or inspect the full plates and therefore make no stronger artifact-level claim about them. This recent publication does not provide a reading anchor on the currently retrieved evidence.

## What the contact evidence can and cannot constrain

A cuneiform personal name on a seal bearing a common bison motif cannot assign that name to the bison or to an unattested Indus inscription. Names need not match across owners, and the motif may mark a social identity, an institution, an animal, or an artistic convention. Even a demonstrably Meluhhan spoken name would constrain a name's pronunciation in foreign transcription, not the phonetic value of a particular Indus glyph, until an independent bridge links them.

An actual two-script object would first supply two texts. A correspondence then requires independent evidence that they encode matching content or matching entities. Useful bridges would include a readable contract naming its Indus-seal witness, an independently identified seal-owner archive, or replicated parallel labels with variable names and stable titles. A photograph of an image beside a name supplies a weaker relation whose semantics remain to be established.

The defensible result of this audit is a constraint on the research: these familiar Gulf and Mesopotamian objects provide external contact context but no current phonetic mapping. Substituting imagery for missing Indus writing would manufacture the supposed key.
