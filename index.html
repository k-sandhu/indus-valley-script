Indus civilisation and script research

The Indus, or Harappan, civilisation flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BCE across what is now Pakistan and northwest India. It spread one material culture, planned towns, standard weights and an undeciphered writing system across hundreds of thousands of square kilometres. The largest database of its writing lists about 4,700 inscribed objects: nearly half are carved stamp seals, and most of the rest are small tablets, marked pottery and clay tags. The texts are short, usually three or four signs, drawn from roughly 400 to 700 signs depending on how variants are counted. No bilingual text has been found, and the language of the script is unknown.

We test whether AI agents and human researchers working from shared primary sources can make verifiable progress on Bronze Age archaeology and an undeciphered writing system. Findings record measured results, source audits and failed tests; working ideas live as conjectures with numeric credences, testable predictions and kill conditions.

The findings guide groups carded results by subject, and the knowledge index records every dated research programme. Many script results come from held-out tests: we fit a model on some inscriptions, score it on the rest, and compare the score with chance. Each link opens a card or report with exact counts, populations and comparisons. A p-value says how often chance alone would produce a result at least this strong.

Script and inscribed objects

  • The object register covers all 4,695 provisional Lipi objects and 5,680 exported faces in the held sources (225,223 assertions in the base build; 230,697 after review integration), while preserving uncertain identities and source assertions. Register report, Paper 007
  • The digital corpus we work from is incomplete. Of 493 numbers in 19 sections of volume 1 of the Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions (CISI), the main printed catalogue, 166 have no matching entry, and a 503-row census of post-catalogue publications identifies 301 later inscribed finds, leaving 55 to 291 absent from the local corpus under strict matching. Finding, Post-catalogue census
  • Of 114 original museum records inspected in the initial census, 68 could be linked with confidence to objects in the corpus; later museum harvests and CISI basic-data transcriptions raised museum-accession coverage to 1,979–2,450 objects depending on certainty rules. Finding, Paper 007
  • An excavators' caption places H-1657 on the floor of Room 591 in Trench 43 at Harappa and assigns it to late Period 3C; image review confirmed fourteen catalogue-view links in held images, including links for L-163 and L-207 absent from the earlier image index. Source review, Independent review
  • No two of the 445 seals with six or more signs carry the same full text. Of 16 sealings or tags whose text matches a known seal, 13 match only seals found at other sites. Long seal texts, Cross-city sealings
  • Line breaks fall between recurring units. On 69 seals where the catalogue shows how the text was split across lines, whole lines recur elsewhere more often than stretches crossing the break: 23 seals favour the lines, 4 favour crossing stretches and 42 tie (p = 0.0003). Finding
  • Signs follow each other predictably. On 1,596 distinct complete texts, a model that knows the previous sign gives the correct next sign about 1.8 times the probability of a position, site and type baseline (a gain of 0.848 bits per sign). When one sign is hidden, two signs of context restore it exactly in 37.3% of 8,009 trials, against 34.6% for one sign. Prediction, Restoration
  • Grouping sign variants that share a base shape improves held-out prediction by 0.022 bits per sign over random groups of the same sizes (p = 0.005). Finding
  • Groups of short strokes take 3.61 different values on average before a given sign, fewer than counted commodities take in matched samples from Mesopotamian accounts of about 2100–2000 BCE (4.53 to 5.15). Finding
  • Some methods fail. Our estimate of how many signs are lost in a damaged gap was right 46.3% of the time, worse than always guessing one sign (51.1%). Finding

No sign has an established sound or meaning, and the language of the script remains unknown.

Archaeology, cities, continuity and comparative society

  • A register of 34 candidate Harappan towns preserves 1,380 line and system records, 118 dated-part assertions and 620 radiocarbon assertions; published Mohenjo-daro surveys show both nearly cardinal and turned street systems, and a comparative urban study records 287 assertions across twelve core towns showing shared streets, drains and enclosures persisting across local rebuilding. Town register, Urban form study
  • A review of excavation coverage compiles 72 sources and 21 regions of interest where later cities, alluvium, piedmont sediment and changing coasts obscure Indus settlements, documenting why historical counts such as "100 of 1,000 excavated" mix distinct frames and area units. Paper 005
  • The Indus living atlas preserves 115 place entities (112 located), seven source-linked 3D city and building reconstructions, and an 18-case town-history review that finds regional persistence through urban reorganization without establishing an uninterrupted Indus-to-present town sequence. Indus living atlas
  • Placing the Mature Harappan civilisation beside 21 contemporary Bronze Age societies (2600–1900 BCE) shows a material culture spread over 650,000–1,000,000 km² and second-rank cities (200–250 ha at Mohenjo-daro) as the only coded literate society with planned streets, drainage and standard weights that lacks an identified palace or royal tomb. Contemporary civilisations
  • Across 938 Greater Indus entries parsed from Ascalone (2022), Dholavira's 90 intact certain weights of 5–100 g fit a 13.716 g unit (CV 1.591%), while smaller 1.3–5 g weights fit 14.280 g and western assemblages show no Indus-standard excess over chance controls. Weight metrology
  • An archaeobotanical audit of Jognakhera and nine recalculated phase summaries across five sites in Petrie and Bates (2017) show survival of the Indus crop repertoire after 1900 BCE alongside shifting seasonal shares (at Bahola, winter crops rise from 6.87% to 17.29% between Late Harappan and Painted Grey Ware levels). Crop continuity
  • Separate historical and theoretical studies examine selective inheritance and intellectual recombination in early Indian institutions and texts (47 source-linked observations across farming, craft and textual memory) and formalize minimum capacities for long societal persistence across 32 baseline and 16 paired repair-and-learning finite models. Early Indian continuity, Societal persistence

Active working claims

  • Three kinds of inscribed object, three jobs 0.60

    The script was used for three different jobs, and most of its apparent oddness comes from mixing their texts in one corpus.

  • A seal legend is a name with a title 0.45

    A seal legend has two parts.

  • Short recurring legends are titles of offices that existed in many cities 0.55

    The 65 seal texts that recur, on 174 seals, are mostly short, and 41 of them occur at more than one site.

  • Offices worked across cities 0.60

    Seals were used to seal goods and documents that travelled between cities, and a sealing was often made far from where the matching seal was found.

  • The field animal is an affiliation, and the unicorn affiliation was everywhere 0.45

    The animal in a seal's picture field marks the holder's affiliation: a clan, guild or institution.

  • Strokes count tiers on tokens and are frozen parts of names on seals 0.50

    On tablets, tall strokes before sign 700 count a tier or quantity class: two, three or four of a standard unit.

  • Signs are built from bases and added marks, and the bases carry meaning 0.55

    Many signs combine a base shape with added marks such as inner strokes or an enclosure.
