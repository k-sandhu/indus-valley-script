Indus Valley script research knowledge base

The Indus script is the writing of the Indus, or Harappan, civilisation, which flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BCE in what is now Pakistan and northwest India. The largest database lists about 4,700 inscribed objects. Nearly half are small carved stamp seals; most of the rest are small tablets, marked pottery and clay tags. The texts are very short, usually three or four signs, and use roughly 400 to 700 different signs depending on how variants are counted. The script has never been deciphered. No bilingual text has been found, and the language it records is unknown.

This project gathers what can be sourced about every inscribed object and tests ideas about the script with statistics. Most of the work is done by AI agents, and we are using the project to find out whether many agents working from shared evidence can make real progress on a problem this hard. We count as progress only results that someone else can check: new facts about the objects tied to their sources, patterns that hold on inscriptions the analysis did not see, and proposed readings that do better than shuffled versions of themselves. Every attempt is kept on record, including the ones that failed, so the next person or agent starts from what is already known.

Where things stand

The findings guide groups the evidence by subject. Many results below come from held-out tests: we fit a model on some of the inscriptions, score it on the rest, and compare the score with what chance would give. Each link opens a card with the exact numbers, the objects counted and the chance comparison used. A p-value says how often chance alone would produce a result at least this strong.

  • The object register covers all 4,695 provisional Lipi objects and 5,680 exported faces in the held sources, while preserving uncertain identities and source assertions. Register report
  • An excavators' caption places H-1657 on the floor of Room 591 in Trench 43 at Harappa and assigns it to late Period 3C; the field basis for that date remains to be obtained. Source review
  • Image review confirmed fourteen catalogue-view links in held images, including links for L-163 and L-207 absent from the earlier image index. Independent review
  • A register of 34 candidate towns preserves distinct architectural axes and building episodes; published Mohenjo-daro surveys show nearly cardinal and turned street systems. Town register
  • No two of the 445 seals with six or more signs carry the same full text. Finding
  • A sealing is the impression a seal leaves in clay, for example on a closed container. Of 16 sealings or tags whose text matches a known seal, 13 match only seals found at other sites. Finding
  • Line breaks fall between recurring units. On 69 seals where the catalogue shows how the text was split across lines, the text of each line recurs elsewhere more often than a stretch of the same length that crosses the break: 23 seals favour the lines, 4 favour the crossing stretches and 42 tie (p = 0.0003). Finding
  • Signs follow each other predictably. On 1,596 distinct complete texts, a model that knows the previous sign gives the correct next sign, on average, about 1.8 times the probability given by a model that knows only the sign's position and the object's site and type (a gain of 0.848 bits per sign). Finding
  • When we hide one sign in an intact text and ask a model to fill the gap, a model that uses two signs of context gets it exactly right in 37.3% of 8,009 trials, against 34.6% for a model that uses one. Finding
  • Grouping sign variants that share a base shape improves prediction by 0.022 bits per sign, more than random groups of the same sizes do (p = 0.005). Finding
  • Groups of short strokes look like numbers. Before a given sign they take 3.61 different values on average, fewer than real counts take in samples of the same size from Mesopotamian accounts of about 2100–2000 BCE (4.53 to 5.15). The stroke values are tied to the sign that follows them more tightly than counted quantities are. Finding
  • The digital corpus we work from is incomplete. Of 493 numbers in 19 sections of volume 1 of the Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions (CISI), the main printed catalogue, 166 have no matching entry. Some of these are the same objects under other numbers; others are objects missing from the corpus. Finding
  • Of 114 original museum records we inspected, 68 could be linked with confidence to objects in the corpus. Finding
  • Some methods fail. Our estimate of how many signs are lost in a damaged gap was right 46.3% of the time, worse than always guessing one sign (51.1%). Finding

No sign yet has an established sound or meaning, and the language of the script is still unknown.

Working model

1. Three kinds of inscribed object, three jobs

The script was used for three different jobs, and most of its apparent oddness comes from mixing their texts in one corpus. Seals carry identity legends: who, or which office, authorises something. Harappa's moulded and incised tablets are standard tokens made in series: one face, the descriptor, names what the token is for, and another states its tier, a level or quantity class. Marks on pottery and small objects label contents or owners. Credence 0.6.

2. A seal legend is a name with a title

A seal legend has two parts. The opening part, made of many different signs, identifies the individual holder; it is a personal or family name. The closing part names a title or office from a small shared stock, often ending in the jar sign 740 or in the comb sign 400. Credence 0.45.

3. Short recurring legends are titles of offices that existed in many cities

The 65 seal texts that recur, on 174 seals, are mostly short, and 41 of them occur at more than one site. Copies of a recurring text at Mohenjo-daro are found together in that city no more often than chance predicts. These texts are titles or role formulae held by several people, not names shared by relatives. Credence 0.55.

4. Offices worked across cities

Seals were used to seal goods and documents that travelled between cities, and a sealing was often made far from where the matching seal was found. Most sealings that carry a seal text match seals from other cities: 13 of the 16 matching sealings, carrying 4 of the 6 texts involved. Ten Lothal sealings carry the four-sign text of the Mohenjo-daro seal fragment M-895. Either the same office had holders in both cities, or one holder's goods moved between them. Credence 0.6 for the first reading over the second.

5. The field animal is an affiliation, and the unicorn affiliation was everywhere

The animal in a seal's picture field marks the holder's affiliation: a clan, guild or institution. The unicorn affiliation was present in every city and accounts for most seals, 77% of square seals with a recorded animal in our corpus. The rarer animals mark smaller or more local affiliations. Credence 0.45.

6. Strokes count tiers on tokens and are frozen parts of names on seals

On tablets, tall strokes before sign 700 count a tier or quantity class: two, three or four of a standard unit. On seals, most stroke groups are fixed parts of names or titles and do not count goods; Parpola read some groups of strokes and a fish sign in this way, as names of asterisms (star groups). Credence 0.5.

7. Signs are built from bases and added marks, and the bases carry meaning

Many signs combine a base shape with added marks such as inner strokes or an enclosure. Signs that share a base behave alike in context, so bases are meaningful units and the added marks modify them. Credence 0.55.

Fish labels and four-unit tablets

Fish combinations form an ordered designation field within seal legends. Credence 0.45. The frozen order predicts 43 independent text groups, with eight opposing it and two ties. The numeral result depends heavily on two-stroke constructions and does not decide between fixed expressions and standardized quantities. The narrow asterism-coverage model failed its registered rules, leaving credence 0.05. Fish study, C-0007, C-0008.
