Prepared with AI research and writing assistance; author names, institutional affiliations and a submission venue have not been assigned. This draft is not ready for journal submission until an independent Harappan archaeologist verifies the export's mapping of M. S. The manuscript assigns no sound value, meaning or spoken language to Indus signs.

Abstract

Whether Indus seal inscriptions stayed uniform through the Mature Harappan period or changed alongside seal manufacture has rested largely on qualitative impressions and stylistic dating. We test stratigraphic change in seal legends using two pre-registered, two-stage frozen protocols on the Lipi corpus export: first across two excavations at Mohenjo-daro (625 discovery seals from Mackay's DK-G area; 434 confirmation seals across 15 sections from Marshall's 1931 excavations), and then across Vats's Mound F sequence at Harappa (99 discovery seals outside Mound F; 173 confirmation seals across six trench frames spanning Strata VII to I). At Mohenjo-daro, the twelve-sign closing stock ends 73.1% of bottom-third legends and 72.5% of top-third legends (difference −0.006, 90% bootstrap interval −0.105 to +0.096), and no tested property of the legend front moves. At Harappa Mound F, rectangular seals increase from 6.5% to 22.6% by stratum third (positive control r = 0.230, within-trench permutation p = 0.0017), confirming that the sequence spans a documented shift in seal form. Within those same trenches, the fifteen-sign closing stock remains bounded between 84.8% in the early third and 80.5% in the late third (difference −0.044, 90% interval −0.190 to +0.102, permutation p = 0.591), and neither opening-sign identity (p = 0.395) nor closing-sign identity (p = 0.700) shows omnibus drift, though pre-freeze power simulations show that the ±0.25 equivalence margin still admits moderate shifts and establishes bounded stability rather than an invariant grammar. By contrast, three frozen drift tests reject fixity after Holm multiplicity correction and single-unit influence checks: complete legends grow from 4.04 to 4.77 to 5.31 signs (r = 0.273, two-sided p = 0.0030, Holm p = 0.012), the sign inventory shifts (p = 0.0016, Holm p = 0.008; signs 806, 817 and 233 rise while 390 and 741 fall), and graphic variant choice within Mayig classes shifts (p = 0.0001, Holm p = 0.0006). Exploratory controls show that all three drifts persist when permuting within trench and seal format together and on the 147 square seals alone, with the added length sitting between the first and last signs. Leave-one-trench checks reveal an important asymmetry: length drift holds across every trench (all p ≤ 0.014) and inventory drift holds in five of six trenches (p = 0.055 without the Granary area), whereas variant drift depends on Trench I (p = 0.160 without Trench I; p ≤ 0.0013 without any other trench). Through the Mound F sequence, interior signs diverge from Mohenjo-daro's pooled sign profile (r = −0.253, p = 0.0044), contrasting with a separate cross-city study in which adding city failed to improve graphic-variant prediction (−0.01584 bits per target across 306 components). A three-pass archaeological context audit of nine HARP seal records separates deposit-based dates from style-reassigned dates, establishing traceable field keys for H-1657 (Mound F Room 591 floor, late Period 3C) and H-1688 (Mound E Area C lower street levels, Period 3A) while confirming that independent lot-level verification of Vats's stratum-to-period mapping remains required before converting relative within-trench drift into absolute calendar chronology.

1. Introduction

Across M. S. Vats's Mound F trenches at Harappa, Indus seal legends kept a bounded closing stock while their middle sections grew longer and shifted sign repertoire. Complete legends lengthen by 1.27 signs from the deepest third of Mound F strata to the uppermost third, specific signs enter and leave the corpus within individual trenches, and those changes hold on standard square animal seals as well as on the long rectangular script-only seals that proliferate in upper strata. At Mohenjo-daro, the same statistical machinery finds a flat stratigraphic sequence across both Mackay's and Marshall's excavations, with the closing stock in place from the lowest recovered deposits and no reproducible movement in length, opening signs, or graphic variants.

This contrast addresses a longstanding disagreement in Indus epigraphy. Excavators and epigraphists working primarily from Mohenjo-daro argued that the script changed little over the Mature Harappan occupation (Marshall 1931; Rao and Mahadevan 1985; Parpola 1994). Archaeologists working from stratigraphically controlled excavations at Harappa argued instead that seal forms, carving techniques, and inscribed object types changed markedly across Harappa Periods 3A, 3B and 3C (roughly 2600–1900 BCE), and expected sign usage to have changed alongside them (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010; Kenoyer 2020). Recent stylistic seriation of over a thousand seals pointed toward longer legends and an expanded sign inventory in late phases (Suga 2024), yet stylistic dating cannot separate chronological drift from workshop convention without an independent stratigraphic clock.

Our investigation proceeded in two frozen stages designed to test these opposing views directly. First, we tested whether Mohenjo-daro seal legends combined a fixed closing stock with a drifting front (C-0006). That conjecture died when Marshall's confirmation sections showed no movement at the front of the legend, though Marshall's sections also failed to reproduce the upward rise of rectangular seals seen in Mackay's DK-G area and spanned a median vertical gap of less than four feet. Because a short stratigraphic span can mask gradual change, we formulated the successor conjecture (C-0010) that the entire seal legend stayed fixed even where seal form demonstrably changed, and tested it once on Vats's Mound F sequence at Harappa using the documented rise of rectangular seals as a built-in positive control. The positive control passed, the closing stock stayed within its equivalence margin, and three tests of the legend body rejected fixity after multiplicity and single-object influence controls.

Throughout this manuscript we separate two distinct chronological claims. Every statistical test in the Harappa study uses relative stratigraphic order: Vats's excavated strata (Stratum VII oldest through Stratum I latest) and, in a pre-declared sensitivity check, Vats's printed find depths ranked within each trench. The Lipi export also maps Vats's Mound F strata onto HARP calendar period labels (Period 3B-1 through Period 3C-4). That conversion is not independently documented in our held sources. We therefore treat the within-trench stratigraphic measurements as our primary empirical result, report the exact limits of our statistical power and trench-level sensitivity, and document a three-pass archaeological context audit that defines the exact verification required before attaching absolute period dates to the drift.

2. Related work

Two traditions shape the study of change over time in Indus inscriptions. The stability view begins with John Marshall (1931, vol. I, pp. 40–41), who reported that Indus sign forms underwent no perceptible alteration through the occupation of Mohenjo-daro and noted that the jar sign usually stood last. Ernest Mackay (1938, vol. I, p. 325) added an important taphonomic caution: seals dropped into holes dug by brick-robbers cannot readily be dated by find level. Mythili R. Rao and Iravatham Mahadevan (1985, pp. 25–38) provided the first quantitative stratigraphic test at Mohenjo-daro, grouping Mackay's DK-G seals into Early, Middle and Late levels and finding no statistically significant shift in the relative frequencies of frequent sign pairs. Asko Parpola (1994, p. 54) summarized the consensus by observing that, apart from stylistic variation tied to material, the script changed little during the Mature Harappan period. Iravatham Mahadevan (1982) and Nisha Yadav and colleagues (2010) established that terminal signs form a tightly concentrated class compared with initial signs, though without stratigraphic controls.

The case for chronological change rests on Harappa's stratigraphy and seal typology. M. S. Vats (1940, vol. I, pp. 9–10) divided Mound F into Late (Strata I–III) and Intermediate (Strata IV–VII) occupations and recorded an increasing proportion of tiny inscribed objects in the lower strata. J. Mark Kenoyer and Richard H. Meadow (2010, pp. xliv–lviii) synthesized the 1986–2007 HARP excavations, dating Harappa Periods 3A (ca. 2600–2450 BCE), 3B (ca. 2450–2200 BCE) and 3C (ca. 2200–1900 BCE), tracing changes in seal forms and boss shapes across those periods, confining long rectangular script-only seals to Period 3C (p. xlix), and predicting that stratigraphic study would overturn the assumption of a uniform writing system (p. lvi). Kenoyer (2020) further traced early occurrences of the jar sign on Ravi and Kot Diji phase pottery at Harappa. Andreas Fuls (Corpus of Indus Inscriptions I, 2022, p. 13, as quoted in the harappa.com review; the book itself was not consulted directly) reported that rectangular seals increase in the later phases of Harappa Mound F and of the northern and southern sectors of DK-G at Mohenjo-daro. Ayuha Suga (2024) assigned 1,137 seals to Periods 3A, 3B and 3C from carving style and reported longer legends and a larger sign inventory in the late period, without stratigraphic controls or formal within-trench permutation tests.

Regional variation has been studied alongside chronology. Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat and Michael Witzel (2004, p. 44) noted palaeographic sign variants as a potential chronological marker. Gabriel Recchia and Max Louwerse (2016) showed that sign co-occurrence carries enough geographic signal to predict find sites, whereas Yadav and Vahia (2011) emphasized a shared sign list across settlements. Neither study tested whether fine graphic variants within a sign class differ systematically by city after controlling for carrier, motif, and context, which we address by comparing our Mound F temporal drift with a frozen cross-city variant-prediction test (2026-10-20-script-variation).

One public online research record also precedes our October 2026 studies. Daniel Bourdeau's Cyphersolver project (Cyphersolver commit 1c736459, committed 1 October 2026) publicly discussed stratigraphic differences alongside line breaks and seal individuality before our 20 October experiment packages were frozen. That page is an unreviewed project record rather than a peer-reviewed publication, and its alternative sign transcriptions cannot be counted as independent physical observations; we cite it to record public priority for examining stratigraphic differences on digital Indus corpora. Two additional published studies could not be read directly during our literature audit and may refine priority for specific details: Bryan Wells (2011, chapter 1), cited in secondary literature for allographic variation across stratigraphic layers, and Ute Franke-Vogt (1991) on the vertical distribution of Mohenjo-daro seals. Kenoyer and Meadow (2010), Fuls (2022) and Suga (2024) already advanced the general case that Indus seal inscriptions and seal formats changed over time. What we add here are pre-registered within-trench measurements that separate a bounded closing formula from a lengthening and drifting legend body, quantify detection power under stratigraphic disturbance, and audit trench-level and archaeological sensitivities.

3. Data and methods

3.1 Corpora, sign coding, and chronological clocks

All inscription texts and Harappa chronology fields come from the normalized Lipi export (data/normalized/lipi_observed_faces.jsonl, SHA-256 1220f559...), derived from yajnadevam/lipi (commit fc8813a5). Signs are coded in the Wells and ICIT numerical sign list (three-digit codes such as 740 for the jar sign and 000 for an unreadable slot). Graphic variants are grouped using the Mayig sign classification (data/normalized/mayig_wells_class_crosswalk.json, SHA-256 9fea45e1...), which maps fine Wells codes to broader shape classes (for example, class P086 groups 390, 405 and 407; class P364 groups 803 and 806; class P385 groups 817 and 861). Only Wells codes that map to exactly one Mayig class enter the variant tests. We treat Mayig classes as an input graphic taxonomy rather than a proved phonetic or semantic equivalence.

For Mohenjo-daro, excavation levels and depths derive from Nitin Stephen Koshy's digital transcriptions of the Mackay (1938) and Marshall (1931) seal tables (epachayan/indus-script, commit 5c14a01, CC BY 4.0), joined to Lipi object families. Mackay recorded seal heights in feet relative to the fixed DK datum in DK-G (range −30.5 to +2.0 ft across 625 joined DK-area seals). Marshall recorded depths in feet below the modern surface across 15 excavation sections (434 joined seals with numeric depth in sections holding at least five seals).

For Harappa, the export contains 428 seal families. Each object family contributes one legend face, chosen deterministically as the longest complete text without unknown signs or blanks, then longest overall, then lowest record identifier. Chronology appears in two forms: seals from Vats's excavations carry a stratum number (phase, with Stratum I at the top of the mound and Stratum VII at the bottom) alongside an export-assigned HARP period label (time), whereas seals from the 1986–2007 HARP excavations (CISI numbers H-1521 to H-2583) carry HARP's own period labels (3A, 3B, 3B/C, 3C). Because HARP's own seals in the export number only 36 families (18 on Mound E), they are too sparse for a within-mound test. The longest single-mound sequence is Vats's Mound F, comprising 175 seal families with a stratum. The export maps Vats's Mound F strata onto HARP periods as Stratum VII to 3B-1, VI and V to 3B/C, IV to 3C-1, III to 3C-2, II to 3C-3, and I to 3C-4. Because the provenance of that stratum-to-period table is undocumented in our held files (Q-1021), every primary statistical test uses only Vats's relative stratum order (with Stratum VII oldest and Stratum I latest) and checks it against Vats's printed find depths in feet.

In the Lipi export, every single-line inscription is normalized to read right to left, so the leftmost visual sign closes the legend and the rightmost visual sign opens it. Edge markers indicate whether each end is intact (+) or broken ([, ]). Because many Harappa seals are damaged, the Harappa study reads a legend's closing sign whenever the text occupies a single line, its left edge is intact, and the leftmost sign is readable; opening signs follow the symmetric rule on the right edge. Complete one-sign legends are excluded from both opening and closing tests because their opening and closing signs coincide. Legend length requires a complete text with both edges intact and no blanks or unreadable signs. Inventory and variant tests use all legends with readable signs. To prevent literal copies from inflating sample sizes, identical complete legends within the same excavation frame are merged into a single analytical unit placed at their median chronological rank.

3.2 Two-stage frozen protocols

Both stratigraphic studies followed the project's two-stage conjecture-shot protocol: unrestricted exploration on a discovery population to set parameters and estimate power, followed by a single frozen read on a disjoint confirmation population enforced by the repository's bench shots loader.

At Mohenjo-daro (C-0006), Mackay's 625 DK-G seals served as discovery material, defining a twelve-sign closing stock (signs 740, 520, 090, 400, 151, 156, 527, 617, 621, 226, 892 and 407, each closing at least five legends of two or more signs and standing last in at least half of its occurrences) and 25 two-sign closing formulae (each closing at least four legends). Predictions P1 through P9 were hashed and frozen at 00:44:49 UTC on 21 October 2026 (e4b6f43c...) and evaluated once on Marshall's 434 confirmation seals across 15 area-section strata.

At Harappa (C-0010), the confirmation population comprised all 175 Vats Mound F seal families with a recorded stratum. Every other dated Harappa seal family (109 families across Vats's other mounds and areas plus HARP's seals) formed the discovery population, of which 99 sat in seven excavation frames with at least five seals (Mound AB Vats 46, Mound E HARP 18, Mound F HARP 10, Mound D Vats 8, Area J Vats 6, Area H export-period 6, Area H Vats 5). Thirty Mound F seals with an export period of 3B/C but no stratum were held out of both dated populations, and 114 undated Harappa seals joined the 109 discovery and 30 held-out families to form a 253-family non-confirmation pool for estimating the closing stock and generating synthetic power populations.

The Harappa closing stock was frozen as the union of Mackay's twelve DK-G closing signs and the eight signs meeting the same closing rule in the 253-family Harappa pool (740, 520, 405, 154, 090, 151, 407 and 595), yielding fifteen signs (090, 151, 154, 156, 226, 400, 405, 407, 520, 527, 595, 617, 621, 740, 892). Out of sample across 200 random halvings of the Harappa pool, this union closed 69.6% of legends (5th to 95th percentile 60.8% to 77.3%), compared with 61.0% for the DK-G stock alone. Before freezing, we inspected only the Mound F census needed to define frames and check sample sizes (counts by trench, stratum, depth, and object type); no Mound F legend, sign, or length was tabulated against stratum. Predictions (PREDICTIONS.json, SHA-256 d3c37d29...) and nine code and parameter artifacts were frozen at 03:34:45 UTC on 21 October 2026. The confirmation script read both halves of the loader split (80 and 95 rows), pooled them, and scored them once.

3.3 Statistics, permutation nulls, multiplicity, and influence

In Harappa Mound F, excavation frames are Vats's trench cells containing at least 15 seals: Trench I (37), the Granary area (37), Trench VI (33), Trenches IV and V combined (24), Mound F seals without a printed trench name (F-, 22), and Trench III (20). The remaining two seals (one each from Trench A and Trench V) fell below the five-seal pooled minimum and were dropped, leaving 173 seals in six frames. Because Vats defined strata as mound-wide building levels, each seal's chronological clock value $u$ is the mid-rank of its stratum over all 173 seals scaled to $(0, 1)$, with 0 oldest (Stratum VII) and 1 latest (Stratum I). Under the population's stratum counts (VII: 2, VI: 19, V: 25, IV: 39, III: 35, II: 40, I: 13), the early third ($u < 1/3$) comprises Strata VII–V (46 seals), the middle third ($1/3 \le u < 2/3$) comprises Strata IV–III (74 seals), and the late third ($u \ge 2/3$) comprises Strata II–I (53 seals).

Because trenches sample strata unevenly (for example, Trench III concentrates in Strata VI–V, the Granary in Stratum IV, and Trench VI in Strata III–II), every null distribution permutes $u$ only among units within the same trench frame (9,999 draws, with the observed value counted as one draw). Every test therefore compares deeper and shallower legends excavated inside the same trench. Exact mathematical definitions appear in methods.md; in brief:

  • Closing-stock equivalence (P1) and early level (P2): P1 measures the late-third minus early-third share of legends ending in the fifteen-sign closing stock, with a 90% percentile bootstrap confidence interval resampled within frames (9,999 draws) and a two-sided within-frame permutation test. P1 passes if the 90% interval lies strictly inside $(-0.25, +0.25)$ and fails if permutation $p \le 0.05$ with absolute difference at least 0.15. P2 checks that at least 55% of early-third legends close in the stock (not counted as fresh confirmation because corpus-wide closing structure was previously examined).
  • Opening-sign (P3) and closing-sign (P7) identity: Rank analysis of variance of $u$ across signs appearing at least five times in that slot (pooling rarer signs into an OTHER category), computed as $n$ times the between-category share of variance in $u$ ($\eta^2$).
  • Graphic variant choice (P4): For each Mayig class having at least two Wells codes with three or more tokens and at least ten total legend-class rows, compute the between-code rank statistic minus its degrees of freedom, and sum across qualifying classes.
  • Legend length (P5): Two-sided correlation between $u$ and legend length ranked within each trench frame, computed on complete legends.
  • Sign inventory (P6): For each sign appearing in at least eight legends, compute its legend count times the squared deviation of its legends' mean $u$ from the overall mean, sum across qualifying signs, and divide by the variance of $u$.
  • Positive control (PC): One-sided Pearson correlation between $u$ and a binary indicator for rectangular seals (SEAL:R) across all 173 seals, permuted within frames. Its role is to gate positive credence gains for fixity: a null result on legend drift is informative only when the sequence spans an established typological transition.

To guard against multiple testing and misplaced objects, three safeguards were frozen before the read. First, Holm step-down multiplicity correction was applied across the six primary p-values (P1's permutation p and P3 through P7); C-0010 died if P1 failed or any drift test passed with Holm-adjusted $p \le 0.05$. Second, a ten-deletion influence rule required every passing P1 equivalence check to keep its 90% interval inside $\pm 0.25$ after deleting, one at a time, each of the ten units whose removal shifted the estimate most (1,999 bootstrap resamples each), and required every drift outcome ($p \le 0.05$) to retain $p \le 0.10$ after deleting, one at a time, each of the ten units whose removal pushed the statistic most toward its null (2,999 permutations each). Third, leave-one-trench-out results were declared for reporting alongside four pre-declared sensitivity analyses (mound-wide permutation, Vats's printed depth ranked within trenches, the twelve-sign DK-G stock alone, and complete legends alone) plus split-half scoring and an independent verification script written from scratch.

Before reading Mound F, we ran a synthetic power analysis (200 replicates per alternative, 400 under the null, 999 permutations per replicate) on Mound F-shaped populations populated from the 253-family Harappa pool. To model residual, intrusive, and misassigned seals, we evaluated each planted effect both without disturbance and after randomly exchanging strata for 25% or 50% of seals within each trench. Under true fixity, false-positive drift rates across P3–P7 stayed calibrated at 3.5% to 5.8%, while P1 passed 55.8% of the time (40.0% inconclusive, 4.25% fail).

4. Results

4.1 Mohenjo-daro shows a flat sequence across both excavations

At Mohenjo-daro, the closing stock stays constant from the deepest excavated levels to the top in both Mackay's and Marshall's excavations, and no tested property of the legend front replicates across them (Table 1). In Mackay's DK-G discovery sequence (625 seals, −30.5 to +2.0 ft), the twelve-sign closing stock ends 73.2% of legends in the bottom third (below −13.6 ft), 75.5% in the middle third, and 71.9% in the top third (above −8.7 ft), and still closes 73.8% of the 61 legends recovered below −18 ft. Opening signs show no height association ($z = 0.42$, $p = 0.311$), while variant choice ($p = 0.129$), legend length (mean 4.77, 4.76 and 5.06 signs; $p = 0.109$), and sign inventory ($p = 0.066$) move weakly without reaching 0.05. Only seal format moves clearly: rectangular seals double from 10.1% in the bottom third to 20.4% in the top third ($r = 0.118$, $p = 0.0018$).

In Marshall's 1931 confirmation areas (434 seals across 15 area-section strata, 307 complete legends of two or more signs), the closing stock reproduces Mackay's bottom level almost exactly: 73.1% of bottom-third legends (79 of 108) and 72.5% of top-third legends (74 of 102) close in the twelve-sign stock, a difference of −0.006 (90% interval −0.105 to +0.096, passing P1 under all ten single-unit deletions). The 25 two-sign closing formulae likewise hold steady (37.0% bottom vs. 31.4% top; difference −0.057, 90% interval −0.163 to +0.047). Every drift prediction fails: opening signs ($z = 0.91$, $p = 0.174$), opening-minus-closing contrast ($z_{\text{open}} - z_{\text{close}} = -0.35$, $p = 0.611$), Mayig variants across eight classes ($z = 1.39$, $p = 0.097$), complete legend length ($r = 0.018$, $p = 0.381$; mean 4.97, 4.63 and 5.23 signs), and out-of-sample transfer of non-final sign heights learned in DK-G ($r = 0.055$, $p = 0.156$). Under C-0006's frozen kill rule, failure of all front-drift tests reduced its credence from 0.20 to 0.02.

The auxiliary check on rectangular seals also failed in Marshall's sections ($r = -0.003$, $p = 0.527$; shares 18.4%, 10.3% and 16.3% by third). Measuring the vertical separation explains why Marshall's sections may have spanned too little time to capture gradual change: the median bottom-third seal lies 6.0 ft below the surface and the median top-third seal 2.3 ft below, a gap of under 4 ft, whereas Mackay's DK-G bottom and top thirds lie 10.9 ft apart (medians −17.35 ft and −6.5 ft). When Mackay and Marshall are combined by Stouffer's method, opening signs ($p = 0.173$) and length ($p = 0.141$) remain null, and the combined variant value ($p = 0.036$) reflects opposite directions in the two excavations (for example, 405 and 407 sit above 390 in DK-G but below 390 in Marshall). Mohenjo-daro therefore establishes early fixity of the closing stock and rules out large front drifts in Marshall's sections (pre-freeze power 0.98 for a 25% opening shift unmixed, 0.80 with 25% mixing), while leaving gradual chronological change over longer spans to be tested at Harappa.

4.2 Harappa Mound F keeps its closing stock within bounded margins

Before reading Mound F, the 99 Harappa discovery seals outside Mound F showed no legend drift (opening $p = 0.560$, closing $p = 0.588$, length $p = 0.511$, inventory $p = 0.820$, variants $p = 0.084$) alongside a steady fifteen-sign closing stock (71.4% early, 72.7% late) and rising rectangular seals ($r = 0.211$, $p = 0.015$), raising C-0010's pre-freeze credence from 0.35 to 0.40.

On the 173 Mound F confirmation seals across six trenches, the positive control passes clearly: rectangular seals rise from 6.5% in the early third (Strata VII–V, 46 seals) to 14.9% in the middle third (Strata IV–III, 74 seals) and 22.6% in the late third (Strata II–I, 53 seals), giving a within-trench correlation of $r = 0.230$ ($z = 2.99$, one-sided $p = 0.0017$). Unlike Marshall's Mohenjo-daro sections, Vats's Mound F sequence therefore captures a substantial stratigraphic shift in seal form within individual trenches.

Across this same span, the closing edge of the legend shows no detectable shift (Table 2). Of the 130 legends with a readable closing sign, the fifteen-sign closing stock accounts for 84.8% in the early third (28 of 33), 82.1% in the middle third (46 of 56), and 80.5% in the late third (33 of 41). The late-minus-early difference is −0.044, with a 90% within-frame bootstrap interval of −0.190 to +0.102 (95% interval −0.213 to +0.130) and a two-sided permutation $p = 0.591$ (Holm $p = 1.0$). All ten most influential single-unit deletions keep the 90% interval inside $\pm 0.25$ (worst lower bound −0.218, worst upper bound +0.132), so P1 passes. Early-third closing coverage passes P2 at 0.848 (0.844 after the worst deletion, well above the 0.55 threshold). The dominant jar sign 740 alone closes 42.4%, 46.4% and 43.9% of readable legends across the three thirds. Neither the identity of the closing sign (P7: observed 2.13 against null mean 3.80, $z = -0.65$, $p = 0.700$, five categories) nor the omnibus identity of the opening sign (P3: observed 5.55 against null mean 5.28, $z = 0.09$, $p = 0.395$, six categories) moves with stratum.

Our pre-freeze power simulations require a strictly bounded reading of these stability results. Because Mound F has only 33 early-third and 41 late-third closing units, the 90% confidence interval is nearly 0.30 wide. In synthetic Mound F populations, the $\pm 0.25$ equivalence rule passes 55.8% of truly fixed populations, 35.0% of populations with a true 0.10 closing-stock shift, 10.5% of populations with a true 0.20 shift, and 2.5% with a 0.30 shift. P1 therefore rules out a wholesale collapse or replacement of the closing stock (shifts of 25 percentage points or more) while remaining compatible with moderate shifts of 10 to 15 percentage points: it is a bounded equivalence result, not proof of an invariant grammar. Similarly, P7 detects a 50% shift of stock-closing legends to period-specific closers in 99.0% of unmixed populations (83.5% with 25% stratum mixing) but detects a 25% shift in only 36.5% (27.5% mixed); P3 detects a 35% opening shift in 89.0% (68.0% mixed) and a 25% shift in 63.5% (42.0% mixed).

4.3 Length, sign inventory, and variant choice drift within trenches

4.4 Leave-one-trench sensitivity separates mound-wide drift from Trench I

Checking each drift test after removing one trench frame at a time separates effects shared across Mound F from effects concentrated in one excavation trench (Table 3). Legend lengthening (P5) is the most geographically uniform signal on the mound: dropping any single trench frame keeps $p \le 0.0143$, and dropping Trench I actually strengthens the length correlation ($z = 3.23$, $p = 0.0007$). Sign-inventory drift (P6) holds below $p = 0.02$ in five of six leave-one-out subsets, weakening to borderline significance when the Granary area is omitted ($z = 1.71$, $p = 0.0553$).

By contrast, Mayig variant drift (P4) rests heavily on Trench I. Removing any of the other five frames leaves P4 strongly significant ($p \le 0.0013$, $z$ between 4.15 and 6.27), but removing Trench I drops $z$ from 5.94 to 0.97 and moves $p$ to 0.160. Variant choice therefore cannot be claimed as a mound-wide trend on present evidence: whatever happened in Trench I (including Vats's stratigraphic assignments there, where Kenoyer and Meadow later re-evaluated the dating of tiny inscribed tablets) accounts for most of the pooled P4 statistic. Separating Trench I's variant concentration from the mound-wide length and five-trench inventory shifts is essential for any archaeological interpretation.

4.5 Mound F interior signs diverge from Mohenjo-daro despite a shared city-level graphic standard

An exploratory post-outcome check tested whether the rising and falling signs of Mound F follow the same chronological trajectory elsewhere (post-outcome-v1/sign-check.json). They do not. Across the 98 discovery legends from Harappa's other mounds, only sign 390 leans downward (5, 4 and 1 legends by third, two-sided $p = 0.0573$), while 806 (0, 3, 1; $p = 0.362$), 817 (1, 3, 1; $p = 0.426$), 233 (1, 0, 1; $p = 1.00$), 741 (3, 4, 1; $p = 0.623$), and length ($r = 0.084$, $p = 0.526$) show no trend. Across Mackay's 610 DK-G legends at Mohenjo-daro, the direction reverses for 817, which is commonest at the bottom (20, 14 and 10 legends by third, $p = 0.099$), while 806 leans slightly upward (5, 5 and 11, $p = 0.186$), 233 is flat (12, 13 and 13, $p = 0.834$), 390 is flat (16, 20 and 14, $p = 0.503$), and length is flat ($r = 0.056$, $p = 0.219$).

Early Mound F legends are shorter than Mohenjo-daro's (4.04 vs. 4.77 signs in DK-G's bottom third), while late Mound F legends are slightly longer (5.31 vs. 5.06 signs). To ask whether Mound F's changing repertoire converged toward Mohenjo-daro or moved away from it, we scored every readable sign by the smoothed log ratio of its frequency across 1,352 Mohenjo-daro seal faces to its frequency across the 253 Harappa seal families outside Mound F (post-outcome-v1/convergence.json). Positive scores mark signs proportionally commoner at Mohenjo-daro. Across all signs (171 merged legends), Mound F shows no trend ($r = -0.019$, $z = -0.40$, two-sided $p = 0.694$; mean scores 0.130, 0.038 and 0.105 by third). Across interior signs only (excluding the first and last sign; 130 merged legends), Mound F diverges clearly away from Mohenjo-daro's profile: the mean Mohenjo-daro log-ratio score falls from 0.349 in the early third to 0.081 in the middle third and 0.038 in the late third ($r = -0.253$, $z = -2.72$, two-sided $p = 0.0044$).

This temporal divergence of Mound F's interior sign repertoire sits alongside a distinct spatial finding from our companion graphic-variation study (2026-10-20-script-variation). In that study, we asked whether knowing the find city improves prediction of which fine Wells variant is cut inside a Mayig graphic family once carrier, material, motif, preservation, CISI volume, position, text length, and neighbouring graphic classes are controlled. Across 306 confirmation components in nine graphic families (181 Mohenjo-daro, 76 Harappa, 18 Dholavira, 31 other sites), adding city reduced mean log-probability by 0.01584 bits per target (95% component-bootstrap interval −0.04107 to +0.00610), well below the declared +0.03-bit usefulness threshold. Every leave-one-family-out score stayed negative. Restricting to seals alone (222 components) gave −0.01595 bits (95% CI −0.04554 to +0.01160), and restricting to excavation-qualified CISI records (171 components) gave −0.02881 bits (95% CI −0.05974 to −0.00083). Synthetic controls with a planted city spelling rule gained +0.40540 bits, confirming that the model could detect genuine city-level variant conventions.

Taken together with Recchia and Louwerse (2016), who found site signal in overall sign co-occurrence, these results distinguish two levels of variation. Pooled across time, Indus cities share one graphic-variant standard inside the tested Mayig families, and Harappa and Mohenjo-daro preserve identical text frames differing only in a single variant slot (for example, seals H-407 and M-712 substituting 817 and 861). Over time inside Harappa Mound F, however, the interior sign mix shifted away from Mohenjo-daro's pooled lexical or sign profile, while variant-level turnover concentrated locally in Trench I.

4.6 Archaeological context audit and the chronology verification gap

Because the Mound F statistical results rely on Vats's 1940 excavation strata while the Lipi export attaches HARP period labels (3B-1 through 3C-4) without documenting the crosswalk, converting our relative within-trench stratigraphy into absolute Harappa periods requires independent archaeological verification. To test how far published HARP records themselves can supply an independent clock for a future prospective cohort, we conducted a three-pass source audit of Harappa seal contexts (2026-10-24-harappa-context-extension, pass2-review, pass3), recovering nine source-qualified records from Kenoyer and Meadow (2010) and Dales and Kenoyer (1991).

The audit demonstrates why published period labels cannot be ingested mechanically as an independent stratigraphic clock. Among the nine audited HARP seals, two (H-2588, field key H98-3493 8314-0001, and H-1537) came from mixed contexts and were explicitly reassigned from Period 2 to Period 3C on the basis of carving technique and glaze (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, pp. 4, 12); a third (H-1689, western Mound E) received a late 3A or early 3B boundary attribution partly from carving style (p. 10); a fourth (H-1683) is an unfinished seal without an independent period; and a fifth (H-2587, circular button seal) carries an internal source conflict between H96/7158-01 in the 2010 prose (p. 9) and H96/7458-0001 in the 2010 Figure 2 caption (p. 10), where the earlier 1999 Figure 4 item 2 pixels (H96/7458-01) corroborate 7458 while leaving the prose conflict unresolved pending the field item register. Using style-reassigned seals to test whether seal inscriptions changed across periods would reintroduce the circularity that stratigraphic analysis aims to avoid.

Two audited seals did yield verified deposit-level field routes, recorded as findings F-0040 and F-0041:

  • H-1657 (F-0040): Kenoyer and Meadow (2010, p. 3, footnote 4) identify H-1657 as H99-4064/8796-1, and HARP's primary caption (Large unicorn seal, slide 56) places H99-4064/8796-01 on the floor of Room 591 in Trench 43 on Mound F, assigning it to late Period 3C. An independent second-pass review verified the caption chain and separated H-1657 from an adjacent Period 4 crushed vessel (H99/8763-503, slides 59–61), which belongs to a separate object record without a published stratigraphic tie to Room 591 or lot 8796.
  • H-1688 (F-0041): Dales and Kenoyer (1991, Figure 13.44e, p. 249, cited by Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, p. 10) join H-1688 to field key H90-1600/3166-1 and to lower street levels in Area C on the southern slope of Mound E, assigned to Period 3A (ca. 2600 BCE).

Together with two additional early candidates from Kenoyer and Meadow (2010) lacking retrieved field lots (H-1682, eastern Mound E Period 3B trench, p. 10; H-1692, dustbin outside a northeastern Mound E Period 3B house, p. 11), H-1657 and H-1688 form a traceable audit queue rather than a ready confirmation cohort. None of the four yet has a published lot sheet linking deposit walls and floors to diagnostic pottery or radiocarbon samples, none carries a manufacture date separate from its deposit phase, and source inspection incidentally exposed graphic descriptions or sign sequences for several cases.

This context audit defines the decisive pre-submission requirement for the present manuscript. Our Mound F findings show that seal legends lengthen and shift interior sign inventory upward through Vats's excavated strata and find depths within individual trenches. Before those strata are equated with HARP Periods 3B-1 through 3C-4 in a journal submission, an independent Harappan archaeologist must verify the Lipi export's stratum-to-period conversion against Vats (1940) and HARP's trench cutbacks, evaluate context integrity in Trench I and the Granary area (particularly where Trench I drives the P4 variant signal), and confirm that no circular typological relabelling entered the stratum assignments.

5. Discussion

The measurements across Mohenjo-daro and Harappa Mound F clarify how an apparently static Indus writing standard could coexist with measurable chronological drift. Earlier debates often posed stability and change as mutually exclusive descriptions of the whole inscription. Our two frozen shots show instead that different parts of the seal legend behave differently along a stratigraphic sequence.

At both cities, the closing stock of signs was established early and stayed in place. At Mohenjo-daro, the twelve-sign closing stock accounts for roughly 73% of legends at both the bottom and the top of Mackay's 30-foot DK-G deposit and Marshall's 15 sections. At Harappa Mound F, the fifteen-sign closing stock accounts for 85% of early-third legends and 80% of late-third legends, with sign 740 holding steady near 44% throughout. Neither opening-sign identity nor closing-sign identity shifts across strata in an omnibus test. At the same time, we must state the boundary of that evidence plainly: at Mound F's sample size, the $\pm 0.25$ equivalence margin excludes major restructuring of the closing stock while still allowing unobserved shifts of 10 to 15 percentage points.

Inside that frame, Harappa Mound F legends changed. Complete legends gained more than a full sign from the deepest third to the uppermost third, and the gain sits between the first and last signs on square animal seals just as much as on rectangular script-only seals. This provides direct within-trench stratigraphic support for the late-period lengthening that Suga (2024) inferred from carving style, while showing that lengthening occurs independently of the late arrival of rectangular seals documented by Kenoyer and Meadow (2010) and Fuls (2022). Sign inventory shifts across five of six Mound F trenches, led by the entry of signs 806, 817 and 233 and the decline of 390 and 741. By contrast, the strong pooled variant-choice signal (P4) loses significance once Trench I is removed ($p = 0.160$) and largely reflects the same sign replacements seen through Mayig classes (P086, P364, P385), so variant drift should be treated as a localized Trench I finding pending excavation-level review rather than a mound-wide law.

The contrast between Mound F's interior divergence from Mohenjo-daro ($r = -0.253$, $p = 0.0044$) and the cross-city graphic-variation null (−0.01584 bits) refines the picture of Indus standardization. Across cities, scribes shared a common pool of closing signs and common graphic-variant conventions inside sign classes, yet during Mound F's later strata Harappa's interior sign repertoire moved along a local path rather than converging toward Mohenjo-daro's profile. Whether that local shift reflects administrative expansion in late Harappa, neighbourhood-specific seal workshops on Mound F, or chronological offset between Mohenjo-daro's sampled levels and Mound F's uppermost strata cannot be resolved from Vats's 1940 stratigraphy alone.

Two limitations govern any interpretation until further fieldwork and catalogue access close them. First, every count rests on the Lipi corpus export, whose third-party rights remain unresolved and whose mapping of Vats's Mound F strata to HARP periods awaits specialist verification. Second, no study in this project has yet reached independent specialist verification above the submitted tier; our independent check verified the code and numbers from the export, not the physical seal impressions or Vats's original field registers.

Boldest supported reading and the test that would decide it: The Harappan seal legend combined a durable closing formula with a mutable interior body. Across Vats's Mound F strata, the closing stock held its share while the body between the first and last signs grew by more than a sign, adopted signs 806, 817 and 233, dropped 390 and 741, and diverged from Mohenjo-daro's interior sign profile on square animal seals as well as on late rectangular seals. The test that would decide whether this pattern reflects Period 3A–3C urban history rather than a single mound's excavation history is a frozen run on HARP's own stratigraphically controlled seals from Periods 3A, 3B and 3C in CISI 3.1 (Q-1008), analyzed within Mound E and Mound AB after lot-level context verification separates deposit dates from style-reassigned dates. Three prospective predictions are already frozen in the Mound F report before any CISI 3.1 confirmation data are read: (1) the closing-stock share of Period 3C legends lies within 0.25 of Period 3A–3B legends; (2) complete Period 3C legends carry more interior signs between their first and last signs than Period 3A–3B legends (one-sided within-mound $p \le 0.05$); and (3) signs 806 and 817 together appear in at most 3% of Period 3A–3B legends and in at least 8% of Period 3C legends. If HARP's Mound E and Mound AB seals reproduce interior lengthening and the arrival of 806 and 817 while keeping the closing stock bounded, the two-part diachronic legend stands; if HARP's 3C legends are no longer than its 3B legends, the Mound F drift belonged to one mound and one excavation.
