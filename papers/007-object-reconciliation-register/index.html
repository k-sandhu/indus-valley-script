Abstract

Quantitative analyses of the undeciphered Indus script routinely treat a single digital export as a complete, reconciled catalogue of inscribed artifacts. In practice, public exports inherit inconsistent catalogue keys, silently mix measurement units and placeholders, omit substantial shares of published finds, and overwrite disagreements between primary reports. We present a deterministic, source-linked object reconciliation register built over the 4,695 provisional object families and 5,680 inscribed faces of the pinned Lipi export (commit fc8813a), together with quantitative audits of metadata missingness, catalogue coverage and post-catalogue discoveries from 1987 to 2026. The Interactive Corpus of Indus Texts (ICIT) already provides an integrated archaeological and epigraphical database; our resource adds an open reconciliation schema, a versioned uncertainty taxonomy (observed, catalogued, inferred, unknown and conflicting), explicit preservation of unresolved alternatives, and verifiable coverage bounds across specified corpus versions. The base register (run-v2) records 225,223 source-attributed assertions, 16,434 identifiers, 11,993 measurements and 6,826 object-to-image links; integrating held-source reviews of 29 divided faces and 358 candidate inventory rows brings the total to 230,697 assertions while keeping every physical family provisional and every exposure record unreviewed for fresh testing. Joining full double-read transcriptions of the basic-data tables of Volumes 1 and 2 of the Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions (CISI: 3,682 printed rows, 3,680 join-eligible, 2,602 unique joins), museum open-access harvests (87 joined records across seven queried institutions) and machine-readable Mackay 1938 and Marshall 1931 seal tables (1,146 joined rows covering 1,134 Mohenjo-daro objects) raises museum-accession coverage from 5.8% in the earlier website export to 52.2% (2,450 objects) when uncertain bracketed digits count, 44.3% (2,079) when at least one accession number in the cell is definite, 42.5% (1,994) when the whole cell is unbracketed, and 42.2% (1,979) under the register's conservative accepted-field rule. Against CISI 3.1 and 3.2 ranges inferred from the export (2,590 slots across six site prefixes), 1,463 numbers are present and 1,127 are absent, of which 634 lie between marked pottery and 317 between seals, tablets or sealings. A census of post-catalogue excavation reports and Indian archaeological journals lists 503 physical objects, including 301 definite post-catalogue inscribed objects from 21 sites; strict registration or plate matching leaves 55 to 291 absent from the local corpus, and a post-outcome context-join refinement narrows that range to 55 to 224. Across the register, 2,870 unadjudicated CISI 2 cell disagreements and 2,596 objects with unreconciled cross-source field variants remain visible instead of being silently collapsed, and two archival case studies (Lothal Plate CLXI F and Harappa H-2587) illustrate the exact boundary between safe source-qualified display and withheld identity joins. All project schemas, code, protocols, join keys and aggregates are released under CC0 1.0 and the Unlicense, while unresolved-rights corpora and copyrighted scans stay local behind a SHA-256 manifest. We close with a six-stratum human accuracy-audit protocol required before journal submission.

1. Introduction

Empirical claims about the Indus script depend directly on the object record underneath the sign strings. Whether a study tests chronological drift across excavation strata, measures geographic recurrence of seal legends, or estimates the predictive gain of a sign-restoration model, its denominator is a set of physical artifacts and their recorded contexts. When an analysis relies on an unverified digital export, three distinct errors enter silently. First, an artifact inscribed on several sides appears as separate face rows that can leak across cross-validation folds or inflate sample sizes if object identity is not separated from face identity. Second, missing values written as dashes, zeros or question marks, combined with undocumented conversions between inches, centimetres and millimetres, distort size and provenance analyses. Third, the relation between a digital export and the wider archaeological record remains unmeasured, so readers cannot tell whether an apparent gap reflects ancient practice, catalogue exclusion or a missing file.

Integrated Indus databases already exist, and we do not claim priority for linking inscriptions to archaeological metadata. Mahadevan (1977) compiled The Indus Script: Texts, Concordance and Tables, selecting 2,906 inscribed objects from a 3,455-object photographic card catalogue (549 exclusions) and establishing a foundational site and sign concordance, though without a machine-readable cross-concordance to excavations published over the past five decades. The Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions (CISI; Joshi and Parpola 1987; Shah and Parpola 1991; Parpola, Pande and Koskikallio 2010, 2019; Parpola et al. 2022) provides the standard photographic reference, but Volumes 1 and 2 omit dimensions, material, depth and findspot from their basic-data tables, and Volumes 3.1 to 3.3 have no open digital edition. The Interactive Corpus of Indus Texts (ICIT; Fuls 2010, 2022, 2023; Wells 2011, 2015) already maintains a relational archaeological and epigraphical database containing 4,665 inscribed objects, 5,650 texts, 17,966 legible sign occurrences and 16,015 photographs or drawings across 29 tables (Fuls 2023, CAA abstract 76, p. 281). Access to ICIT is restricted by administrator approval, and its current full object inventory is not publicly downloadable for programmatic auditing.

Because ICIT is not openly downloadable, recent computational work relies on derivative web exports, primarily the Lipi repository (yajnadevam/lipi), an earlier SQL export (yajnadevam/indus-website), the 179-seal Mohenjo-daro unicorn subset curated by Carlson (mayig/indus-valley-script-corpus), or subsets without a validated cross-concordance (for example Nair 2026, who notes that his 1,916 inscriptions lack one). None of those exports audits its own completeness against printed catalogues number by number, separates transcription errors from genuine conflicts between excavation reports, or tracks the licensing boundaries of the underlying scans and tables.

We address that gap with a source-linked reconciliation layer built over held Indus sources. Specifically, we provide: (1) a deterministic versioned data model (Schema v2.0) separating provisional physical objects from inscribed faces, carrying a five-class uncertainty taxonomy and preserving every raw assertion with its source hash and page locator; (2) exact missingness and coverage measurements across the Lipi export, CISI 1 and 2 basic-data tables, inferred CISI 3.1 and 3.2 number ranges, open museum collections, the Marshall 1931 and Mackay 1938 Mohenjo-daro seal tabulations, the 8,722 local photograph crops, and a new 503-row census of post-catalogue finds published between 1987 and 2026; (3) an explicit conflict-preservation architecture that retains 2,870 unadjudicated CISI 2 cell disagreements, 2,596 objects with cross-source field variants and two deeply audited archival identity disputes without forcing premature merges; (4) a clean separation between redistributable CC0 and Unlicense project outputs and local-only third-party materials; and (5) a concrete stratified human accuracy-audit protocol required to elevate this resource from an AI-assisted working build to a journal-grade reference release.

This paper makes data-engineering and corpus-measurement claims about specified source versions. It does not claim decipherment, assign phonetic or semantic values to signs, or treat alternative digital transcriptions as independent physical excavations.

2. Data model and uncertainty taxonomy

To prevent conflation of physical objects, inscribed sides and catalogue aliases, we define five analytical units used throughout this paper. A face is one row of the pinned Lipi observational export (data/normalized/lipi_observed_faces.jsonl, derived from yajnadevam/lipi commit fc8813a5, SHA-256 1220f559...), representing one source-defined inscribed side or view. A provisional object family groups face rows sharing the export's object_family_id; because physical identity across catalogues has not been adjudicated object by object, object counts remain provisional and no external alias merges two families automatically. A catalogue entry is a numbered item in a printed catalogue such as CISI 1 or CISI 2. An absent key is a catalogue base identifier with no exact or source-attested alias match among local identifiers; an absent key can represent a missing object, an alias, a non-script mark or an extra photograph. A metadata value is usable when it is neither empty nor a placeholder token (-, --, - -, ?, ??, ???, Unknown, or a zero or non-positive number in a dimension field); None in the motif fields (symbol, cult) is an asserted absence and counts as usable.

2.1 Relational core and compact sidecars

The register reuses the unchanged JSON Schema Draft 2020-12 (schema-v2.json) developed in our 7 October object-ledger pilot (experiments/2026-10-07-object-ledger/). Every register build (register-v2.json) enforces a strict sequence policy (No sign sequences; transcription payloads are local-only) so that archaeological metadata, identity links and provenance can be audited independently of sign strings. The schema organizes all records into twelve top-level tables: objects, contexts, surfaces, transcriptions (empty under the sequence policy), images, sources, identifiers, measurements, assertions, rights, relationships and correction_overlays.

Stable identifiers separate the physical-carrier level from the inscribed-surface level. Every provisional object takes the key LIPI:<object_family_id> (4,695 records) and links one-to-one with a context row CTX:<object_family_id>. Every exported inscribed face takes the key LIPI:<record_id> (5,680 records) and points to its parent object. Each field in objects and contexts satisfies a strict oneOf contract: either it carries a non-null value, a non-empty list of supporting assertion_ids and missing_reason: null, or it carries value: null, assertion_ids: [] and an explicit non-empty missing_reason.

Beside register-v2.json, each deterministic build emits six JSONL sidecars for downstream auditing: objects.jsonl (compact object records with displayed values, canonical statuses, aliases and conflict lists), faces.jsonl (compact face records with image links and alignment flags), assertion-values.jsonl (every raw and normalized assertion value with source hash, locator and join status), missing-values.jsonl (every absent object or context field with its reason), join-queue.jsonl (external rows that failed unique matching or lay outside the Lipi base population), and cisi-reader-decisions.jsonl (all imported CISI double-reading disagreement records).

2.2 Canonical uncertainty taxonomy and acceptance rules

Each field in the target object model and compact sidecars carries a canonical value_status drawn from six controlled states (five operational states plus not-applicable):

value_status Meaning in the register Example
observed Recorded directly from visual inspection of a physical object, plate or photograph Line-layout observation from sealed visual inspection of a CISI crop
catalogued Transcribed or imported directly from a printed catalogue, excavation table, museum database or digital export CISI 1 basic-data museum accession; Mackay 1938 printed seal material
inferred Computed or decomposed from a source string by an explicit rule Registration, citation and lot split from Lipi excavation-idno
unknown No usable value recorded in integrated sources; always paired with a missing_reason Absent calendar date or placeholder - in Lipi depth
conflicting Two or more accepted non-repeated source assertions disagree on the value and remain unreconciled Material listed as silver in Mackay 1938 and steatite in Lipi
not-applicable Field does not apply to the object class or frame CISI 1 India counts for finds excavated outside South Asia

Crucially, the register keeps four epistemic dimensions orthogonal so that consumers never confuse them:

  1. Value status (observed, catalogued, inferred, unknown, conflicting, not-applicable) states how a field value entered the record.
  2. Uncertainty flags (bracketed, unresolved-reader-disagreement, candidate_identity, implausible) record reading or plausibility caveats on individual assertions.
  3. Review status (not-expert-reviewed on all core assertions in Schema v2.0) records that no human epigraphist or archaeologist has yet verified the cell against the original publication or object.
  4. Exposure status (unreviewed across all 4,695 objects in the base register; eligible_fresh = 0) tracks whether an object or its source pages have been viewed by contributors or models, preventing accidental reuse of examined data as a fresh confirmation cohort.

Accepted fields in register-v2.json enforce a conservative filter. Assertions derived from automatic CISI reader merges, undecidable adjudications, bracketed cells, disputed CISI column-1 identities, candidate-only joins, or measurements above the 1,000 mm pilot plausibility bound are never promoted into accepted object fields. They remain intact in assertions, assertion-values.jsonl, identifiers (with status: "candidate"), and join-queue.jsonl. Likewise, printed dimension ranges such as 5 - 9 mm are preserved as raw observations rather than replaced by midpoints or endpoints.

3. Corpus coverage and missingness measurements

Missingness is heavily structured by site and artifact class. Seals and Mohenjo-daro are the best documented; pottery, tags and sealings, minor sites and Dholavira are the sparsest. Dholavira stands out as the least linked major settlement: only 0.9% of its 230 local objects carry a CISI number or local image, 18.7% carry an excavation identifier, and none has a museum number in the older export.

Inspecting raw strings also exposed systematic encoding traps. Missing values appear as - (3,362 faces in period, 3,672 in color, 4,168 in boss), -- (3,489 in room-grid, 4,167 in block-house), - - (2,870 in depth), ? (667 in complete) and 0 (1,810 horizontal, 1,681 vertical and 3,586 thickness values). The excavation-idno column mixes true field registrations with 146 hyphen-led citation strings, and depth strings mix feet (2,557 usable faces), metres (72) and other expressions (181, including 50 surface finds) referenced to distinct excavation datums.

A dedicated dimension-unit audit (experiments/2026-10-13-image-index-and-units/run-2026-10-13/dimensions/summary.json) showed that Lipi's h, v and th columns store each measurement as originally recorded in whichever unit the source used, while the millimetre columns hold conversions. Across all 17,040 face-axis values (5,680 faces × 3 axes), 6,656 are empty or zero, 5,643 were recorded in inches (2,295 to 0.1 in, 3,298 to 0.01 in, 37 to 1 in and 13 to 0.001 in), 4,250 exist only in millimetres (2,501 whole mm, 1,749 to 0.1 mm), 389 were recorded in millimetres and 102 in centimetres. The millimetre field reproduces the recorded value within 0.05 mm for 5,285 inch, 292 millimetre and 97 centimetre entries, with 23 rounded and only 15 genuine conversion mismatches. Of the 228 apparent inch-to-millimetre discrepancies flagged in the 12 October inventory, 180 were values recorded in millimetres, 42 were recorded in centimetres and only 6 were true conversion errors. Similarly, 199 of the 206 apparent disagreements with the older website export arose because Lipi prints thickness as a range (such as 5 - 9, occurring on 530 faces); once ranges are parsed, 2,340 objects agree on every comparable axis, 20 website rows give the lower bound of a Lipi range, and only 9 objects disagree on an axis. Within the register's scalar plausibility rule (values ≤ 1,000 mm), 3,559 of the 3,598 Lipi dimension-bearing families have a valid scalar measurement, 39 carry only a range or outlier (including one 3,000 mm value quarantined as implausible), and external adapters add scalar dimensions for 4 more, yielding 3,563 accepted measurement-bearing objects.

3.2 Evidence census and CISI 1 and 2 identifier gaps

The 6–7 October evidence census (experiments/2026-10-06-evidence-census/synthesis/REPORT.md and coverage.json) audited site coverage and printed catalogue ranges against the Lipi export. Across four regional batches (123 raw rows), merging source-attested aliases (such as Nuhato and Gharo Bhiro) yields 115 site or provenance entities representing 5,652 of the 5,680 local faces and 4,668 of the 4,695 provisional families; the remaining 28 faces (27 families) carry the label Unknown (Finding F-0009).

Matching printed CISI identifiers number by number demonstrated substantial local omissions. Across 19 checked CISI 1 site ranges in Gujarat and north India, 327 of 493 identifiers matched locally (66.3%) and 166 were absent: 75 in pottery and graffiti sections and 91 in sections for seals, impressions, tablets and other objects (Finding F-0007). Within that frame, Lothal accounts for 111 absent keys out of 290 (34 seals, 25 impressions, 52 pottery or graffiti), Kalibangan 26 of 122 (12 seals), and Banawali 14 of 37 (8 seals). None of the 166 absent keys appears under another site label anywhere in the Lipi export. Across all 3,620 CISI 1 and CISI 2 entries measured in the census's catalogue-coverage.csv, 1,032 had no local identifier match, led by Rehman Dheri (268 absent out of 270 mixed entries) and Sarai Khola (10 absent out of 10). Eight printed catalogue sections totalling 16 entries have neither a contributing census row nor a local site label: Maski, Mehi and Shahi-Tump in CISI 1 (one entry each) and Kalako-deray, Lewan-dheri (Dar Dariz), Loebanr, Periano-ghundai and Tarakai Qila in CISI 2 (13 entries combined). Because no common physical-object inventory links Mahadevan 1977, CISI 1 to 3, ICIT and post-catalogue excavation reports, worldwide omissions cannot be estimated by subtracting corpus totals.

3.3 Full CISI 1 and CISI 2 basic-data transcription and FC concordance laws

Both CISI 1 (Joshi and Parpola 1987, printed pp. 366–375) and CISI 2 (Shah and Parpola 1991, printed pp. 435–447) print five basic-data columns: CISI number, Finnish concordance (FC) number, excavation number, holding museum with registration numbers, and photograph sources. Neither prints findspot, depth, material or dimensions. We transcribed all 3,682 printed rows across both volumes using two independent blind AI reading passes per half-column block (experiments/2026-10-13-cisi2-basic-data-pilot/ and experiments/2026-10-13-cisi-basic-data-full/): 1,543 rows in CISI 1 and 2,139 rows in CISI 2 (327 in the M-621 to M-947 pilot plus 1,812 across 23 remaining blocks). Excluding two CISI 1 cross-references to CISI 2 ({H-356} and deleted M-595) leaves 3,680 join-eligible rows, of which 2,602 join uniquely to one Lipi family each (1,239 from CISI 1, 1,363 from CISI 2) and 1,078 match no Lipi family.

Scan sharpness produced a sharp accuracy split between the two volumes (Table 3). CISI 1 read cleanly: the two blind passes agreed on 98.6% of 7,715 cells (106 cell disagreements plus 6 corrigenda lines), every disagreement was adjudicated against the page, definite error rates stayed below 0.1% in the CISI, FC and excavation columns (7 definite errors per pass across all columns), and only 5 cells stayed bracketed. In the CISI 2 pilot block (M-621 to M-947, 1,635 cells), blind agreement was 90.5% (156 disagreements: 48 decided from the page, 108 undecidable; 119 cells bracketed after non-blind cross-check) because blurred italic digits (3/5, 6/8, 0/9, 1/7) cannot be separated at scan resolution. Across the remaining 23 CISI 2 blocks (9,075 cells), scan degradation lowered blind agreement to 65.3% (3,146 disagreements). Three blocks (pp. 437L–439L, 256 disagreements) were adjudicated before adjudication was paused for cost; under Amendment 2, the remaining 2,870 disagreements were automatically merged to keep every candidate digit either reader allowed, leaving 2,946 bracketed cells in the new CISI 2 blocks.

Joining the CISI basic data also tested and confirmed three frozen structural predictions (predictions.json). Third, CISI 1 blind agreement (98.6%) exceeded the CISI 2 pilot (90.5%).

3.4 Museum-accession coverage under explicit certainty definitions

Museum-accession coverage illustrates why headline completeness figures must always state their exact certainty definition. We combined four distinct custody sources: the older website export (MUSEUM field), the 6 October evidence census of 114 online museum originals (68 confirmed joins, 46 open; Finding F-0008), the 13 October museum open-access harvest and Q-1019 review (experiments/2026-10-13-museum-open-access/), and the full CISI 1 and CISI 2 basic-data tables.

The 13 October harvest queried seven institutions (Metropolitan Museum of Art, Cleveland Museum of Art, Art Institute of Chicago, Smithsonian Open Access across 539,615 bulk records, Penn Museum's 430,871-row CSV, Victoria and Albert Museum API, and saved British Museum search listings behind a Cloudflare challenge) and revisited all 46 open census records against CISI 1 basic data, Marshall and Mackay tables, and numbers painted on objects in CISI plates. It decided 86 museum records and produced 27 exact or confirmed joins (19 new against the census, raising the museum-join lower bound from 68 to 87 records: 16 of the 46 open census records confirmed, 21 candidate sets, 8 unmatched, 1 uninscribed, plus 3 newly harvested joins at the V&A and British Museum). An independent review verified all 26 joins made before review (100% precision; one-sided 95% exact lower bound 89%) and recovered one additional Indian Museum join (10377/A7252/C.2803 = M-1809 via Marshall 1931 vol. II p. 403 row 199). None of the eight CC0 seals at the Metropolitan and Cleveland museums could be joined because their records omit findspot and excavation numbers and each matches 10 to 102 local objects on motif, size and sign count; consequently zero local objects gained an openly licensed photograph. Five museum originals were documented as absent from the local corpus: CISI H-224 (National Museum New Delhi 12906-1296), Banawali seal National Museum 3714, and three Dholavira pieces (DHR-22198, DHR-6032, DHR-7860).

3.6 Excavation-table joins (Marshall 1931 and Mackay 1938)

To trace Mohenjo-daro metadata back to primary excavation reports and attach explicit vertical datums, we joined Nitin Stephen Koshy's CC BY 4.0 transcriptions of Mackay's 1938 and Marshall's 1931 Mohenjo-daro seal tabulations (epachayan/indus-script, commit 5c14a01; 709 Mackay rows and 560 Marshall rows, 1,269 total) to the Lipi export (experiments/2026-10-13-mackay-marshall-tables/). Upstream transcription quality was audited on random samples of 30 rows per table against printed page scans: 0 of 30 Mackay rows differed from print (Wilson 95% CI 0.0–11.4%) and 3 of 30 Marshall rows contained an error in size, serial number or type (10.0%, 95% CI 3.5–25.6%), with 11 further print corrections documented in print-corrections.csv and kept separate from the unchanged upstream values.

Of the 1,269 table rows, 1,146 joined a Lipi family (1,108 exact, 38 confirmed through variant keys or print-checked adjudication), 2 stayed ambiguous (Mackay No. 434 and Marshall No. 483), and 121 were unmatched with explicit reasons (106 field numbers absent from the export, 10 duplicate or misprinted listings sharing a joined field number, 3 Marshall rows printing no field number, and 2 rejected candidates). The 1,146 joined rows cover 1,134 Mohenjo-daro objects (11 families carry two excavator listings), including 1,122 of the 1,318 Mohenjo-daro seals in the export (85.1%). A stratified random check of 30 joins (20 exact, 10 confirmed) verified 29 against the printed tables and, where held, the CISI 1 or 2 basic-data excavation number, with 0 wrong and 1 unresolved (overall precision 96.7%, 95% CI 83.3–99.4%; exact joins 20 of 20).

Because the Lipi export descends largely from the same excavation tables via CISI and ICIT, agreement between comparable fields is very high (98.6% on size within 0.06 in, 99.5% on material, 99.0% on block, house and room, and 99.7% on level within 0.2 ft) and raw fill counts rise only slightly (+12 objects with both face dimensions, +5 with material, +10 with any spatial unit, +2 with depth). The genuine gain is provenance structure: 1,134 objects gain a field number separated from the concatenated excavator catalogue number (decoding strings like DK343942 into field number DK 3439 and Mackay No. 42) plus an explicit publication reference, 1,091 gain a size traced to a printed page in inches, and 1,101 gain a level tied to its stated datum (Mackay's DK datum or SD bench mark, or Marshall's depth below modern surface).

3.7 Face-level image index

The 8,722 photograph crops in the Lipi directory reproduce plates from CISI 1 and CISI 2 with their printed captions (experiments/2026-10-13-image-index-and-units/). Lipi's own application index maps only 6,570 files under 3,318 keys, skewing seal links toward impressions (1,777 impression links against 1,266 object photographs) while leaving 2,152 files unmapped, including 1,780 object-side photographs named in the form M-12_A.jpg. Parsing all 8,722 filenames, checking printed captions by OCR and visual inspection, and placing 3,664 catalogue keys on their exact CISI 1 (PDF = printed + 36) and CISI 2 (PDF = printed + 35) plate pages produced 8,444 face-to-image links across 6,826 object-to-image pairs.

Indexing all files raises objects with any image modestly, from 2,509 (53.4%) to 2,562 (54.6%; faces 51.9% to 52.9%, 3,003 faces), but more than doubles verified object-side photograph coverage: objects with a photograph of the inscribed face's own side rise from 825 (17.6%; seals 10.5%) under Lipi's index to 2,159 (46.0%; seals 68.3%), and faces rise from 846 (14.9%) to 2,181 (38.4%). Caption verification caught 23 crops whose filenames named the wrong object (such as H-502_B.jpg, which shows H-902 B, and K-823_B.jpg, which shows H-823 B), yielding 26 visual name overrides, corrected 8 I/l prefix errors, and read legible captions on 231 of 299 files named only by volume and page (linking 182 to Lipi faces). In a random sample of 60 links, 60 of 60 were correct on identity and view kind (Wilson 95% CI 94.0–100%). However, an initial ordinal rule assigning sides to multi-face objects scored only 15 of 18 in its targeted sample and was withdrawn under Amendment 2: side assignments remain accepted for single-face objects and 21 visually verified multi-face objects, while 881 multi-face objects retain object-level links with open side assignments. Four keys flagged in a later line-division plate audit (H-603, M-663, M-1187, M-1202) are quarantined from side_match=true in the register pending image-by-image reconciliation. Of the 2,677 faces still lacking an image, 661 have no CISI key and 1,926 carry CISI 3 numbers; conversely, 1,838 local crops depict 844 CISI keys absent from Lipi.

3.8 Post-catalogue finds census (1987–2026)

Objects excavated or published after the CISI volumes for their sites closed are absent from CISI 1 and 2 and scattered across excavation monographs and journals. Our 20–21 October census (experiments/2026-10-21-post-catalogue-finds/) swept post-1987 volumes of Indian Archaeology – A Review (1987–88 through 2013–14), Puratattva (18–37), 15 volumes of Man and Environment, seven Internet Archive excavation reports, held census reports and 367 web queries across 61 site labels. It compiled 503 physical object rows (objects.csv): 301 definite post-catalogue inscribed objects from 21 sites (18 in India and 3 in Pakistan: Ganweriwala, Lakhanjo-daro and Nuhato), 56 post-catalogue objects whose inscription status the publication leaves open, 145 objects already present in a CISI baseline (138 of them inscribed rods from Harappa and Mohenjo-daro whose CISI numbers Parpola 2019 prints), and 1 rod possibly in CISI 3.1.

Under the frozen protocol's strict identity rule, which accepts only a matching excavation registration or cited plate, 10 of the 301 definite post-catalogue objects match a local Lipi family, 271 remain candidates for same-type objects at their site, and 20 have no possible local counterpart. The local corpus therefore lacks between 55 and 291 of these 301 objects (the lower bound assumes every candidate matches an available local family; the upper bound assumes none does). After inspecting run proposals, we formulated a post-outcome context-join rule admitting a link when area, trench, phase, type and material (or Dholavira grid square) pick out exactly one object on both sides without depth, layer or period conflict, alongside one Khirsara plate join. That labelled refinement adds 67 matches to the main set (58 definite at Dholavira plus 1 uncertain-status row, 5 at Farmana, 2 at Kanmer, 1 at Karanpura and 1 plate join at Khirsara), raising matched main rows to 77 and narrowing the corpus absence range to 55 to 224 (Table 6). Neither bound implies that all 301 objects are missing from every prior corpus.

4. Conflict preservation and unresolved archival case studies

Reconciling independent archaeological publications exposes disagreements that cannot be resolved by automated precedence rules. Silently overwriting one source with another destroys provenance and can attach a wrong findspot or period to an inscription. Our register therefore retains every competing assertion and exposes disagreements at three levels.

4.1 Systematic disagreements preserved across the corpus

At the transcription level, cisi-reader-decisions.jsonl imports all 3,238 CISI reader-decision rows, including all 2,870 unadjudicated Amendment 2 automatic merges in CISI 2 and 2,943 outcomes labelled undecidable (overlapping sets). Every affected CISI cell stays excluded from accepted core fields (6,226 uncertain CISI assertions withheld in run-v2) while both blind readers' readings and merged bracketed strings remain inspectable.

At the cross-source object level, objects.jsonl flags 2,596 provisional families with unreconciled field variants across non-repeated fields (coverage.json, objects_with_unreconciled_field_variants). These variants mix benign formatting or abbreviation differences (such as museum codes and material spellings across Lipi, CISI, the older website export and excavation tables) with genuine substantive contradictions. Among the 1,134 Mackay and Marshall joins alone, the excavation tables and Lipi disagree for 27 objects on field number (mostly transcription errors in the table), 15 on size, 14 on object type, 5 on material (for instance Mackay prints silver for Nos. 16 and 533 where Lipi lists steatite, and Marshall prints red pottery for Nos. 431 and 450), 5 on findspot, 3 on level and 3 on thickness.

Cross-checking identifiers also exposed inverted primary records in both CISI and Lipi. In the CISI 2 pilot block, four Mohenjo-daro excavation records were crossed in two pairs between Lipi and CISI 2 (M-653 with M-655, and M-656 with M-707); direct measurement of the four seals on the 2:1 CISI 2 plates demonstrated that CISI 2's printed basic-data table, not Lipi, swapped the pairs (experiments/2026-10-13-data-round-1-verification/swap-check.md). Conversely, the 13 October museum review uncovered two Lipi faults at Lothal: family 2067 (L-122) carries the excavation identifier that CISI 1 assigns to L-100, and family 2084 (L-142) carries the identifier of L-166. Among the 61 census museum joins given paired-field comparisons, museum records and Lipi disagreed on 1 site, 4 materials, 5 dimensions and 9 sign counts.

4.2 Twenty-target pilot and two deep identity-conflict case studies

To test how source-qualified metadata, crop pointers and unresolved conflicts work together at specimen resolution, we built a local browsable catalogue over twenty priority targets: ten Dholavira accession targets, five Harappa catalogue targets and five Lothal catalogue targets (experiments/2026-10-26-object-pilot/ and experiments/2026-10-26-source-conflicts/). The pilot retains 539 inherited source assertions plus 2 conflict summaries (541 displayed assertions), keeps all 62 candidate corpus families and 274 comparison relationships open for the ten Dholavira targets with zero physical merges, adds 14 independently verified asset-to-catalogue crop links (bringing L-163 and L-207 into the crop index), and leaves 34 unresolved anonymous crops queued for future visual checks.

Two pilot objects underwent dedicated 65-page archival audits (experiments/2026-10-26-source-conflicts/REPORT.md) that define the exact operational boundary between displayable metadata and blocked joins:

  1. Lothal Plate CLXI F and L-164. CISI 1 (p. 373, PDF 409) maps catalogue entry L-164 to excavation number 1864 and site-museum accession LTH SRG1232. In S. R. Rao's excavation report on Lothal, printed p. 319 (PDF 70) associates Plate CLXI F with 1864, whereas printed p. 325 (PDF 76) associates the same panel CLXI F with 1830, which CISI catalogues as a separate object. Inspecting fifty Rao pages including front matter, Appendix III (PDF 445–450) and the thematic index (printed pp. 705–712, PDF 451–458) recovered no erratum or specimen concordance. The register therefore displays L-164's CISI 1 identifiers and retains both Rao passages as source-qualified assertions, while retaining CLXI F only as a candidate page and blocking any transfer of the p. 325 find context to L-164. Resolving the dispute requires the Archaeological Survey of India original negative register for Plate CLXI F alongside the labelled specimen cards for 1864 and 1830.
  2. Harappa H-2587. In Kenoyer and Meadow's 2010 Harappa overview (Inscribed Objects from Harappa Excavations 1986–2007), p. 9 identifies H-2587 as a steatite object of 15.29 mm maximum diameter with registration H96/7158-01, whereas p. 10 Figure 2.1 captions the drawing as H96/7458-0001. An earlier 1999 caption (Figure 4, item 2) prints H96/7458-01, and the 2010 drawing explicitly descends from Kenoyer and Meadow 2008 (p. 127, fig. 4.3). Because these publications share one excavation lineage, majority voting across published captions cannot settle which 1996 lot owns the object. The register displays H-2587's steatite material and 15.29 mm diameter as source-qualified assertions, preserves both 7158 and 7458 registration alternatives, and blocks automatic merger or canonical registration assignment until the HARP object database rows and original 1996 object cards for lot 7158 item 01 and lot 7458 item 0001 are inspected.

6. Limits and pre-submission stratified accuracy-audit protocol

Four limitations separate this working manuscript from a finished journal data paper, matching the "B, close" assessment in our 22 October field novelty review (experiments/2026-10-22-field-novelty-review/REPORT.md Section 4):

  1. No human specialist verification. Every package synthesized here currently stands at the submitted verification tier. All visual readings of CISI basic-data pages, photograph captions and divided-face layouts were made by AI models (primarily instances of Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1-Sol). Same-model blind passes can share systematic misreadings, as shown in the CISI 2 pilot where 5 of 299 agreed excavation cells (1.7%) proved ambiguous upon cross-checking against Lipi.
  2. Unresolved CISI 2 disagreements. While CISI 1 is fully adjudicated (5 bracketed cells), CISI 2 carries 2,870 unadjudicated disagreements merged under Amendment 2 across blurred scans. Row completeness does not establish field accuracy.
  3. Unheld denominators and unadjudicated physical identity. CISI 3.1 to 3.3, the full ICIT database, Mackay's 1938 plate volume, Vats's 1940 Harappa seal table and several post-catalogue excavation reports remain unheld. All 4,695 Lipi families remain provisional and all exposure statuses remain unreviewed for confirmation testing.
  4. Pending third-party redistribution clearance. Full record-level publication of register-v2.json awaits a documented reuse basis for Lipi metadata (Q-1028) and permission or a clean derived-only packaging decision for CISI 1 and 2 basic-data values.

To close the accuracy gap before submitting to a data or digital-archaeology journal (such as Journal of Open Archaeology Data, Data in Brief or Journal of Computer Applications in Archaeology), we specify a six-stratum human accuracy-audit protocol totalling 350 audited units, executed by at least one human archaeologist or epigraphist blinded to automated labels wherever feasible and working from physical printed volumes, high-resolution scans or museum photographs (Table 8).

7. Conclusion: boldest supported claim and deciding tests

The boldest claim this work supports is that the scattered Indus epigraphic and archaeological literature can be maintained as a single deterministic, source-linked reconciliation register that preserves every unresolved disagreement while quantifying what digital corpora omit, and that two concrete structural regularities already govern the largest remaining gaps. First, the Helsinki FC concordance numbers printed in CISI 1 and 2 encode excavators' primary catalogue numbers by fixed arithmetic at Mohenjo-daro (97.7%) and in Lipi's Harappa registrations (97.8% of scorable extensions), providing an independent deterministic bridge back to primary excavation tables. Second, most missing entries in the CISI 3.1 and 3.2 ranges (634 of 1,127 slots) lie inside marked-pottery blocks rather than unrecorded seals, and most uncertainty in our post-catalogue absence range (147 of 224 candidate rows) comes from Dholavira objects likely already present among the 187 unregistered local Dholavira families, placing the true count of post-catalogue finds missing from the local corpus near the lower bound of 55 to 90 objects.

Three concrete tests will decide these claims and complete the resource for journal publication:

  1. The six-stratum human accuracy audit (Table 8) against printed CISI 1 and 2 volumes, excavation plates and museum photographs, testing whether the register's deterministic joins and adjudicated tables meet the ≥ 95% to 98% precision gates.
  2. Acquiring CISI 3.1 and transcribing Vats's 1940 Harappa catalogue table, which directly tests two falsifiable predictions: that at least 80% of the 340 pottery-context CISI 3.1 absent slots are potsherds and at least 80% of the 244 seal-and-tablet-context slots are seals, tablets or sealings (failing if either falls below 60%), and that Harappa FC numbers map to Vats's 1940 catalogue numbers by a single fixed offset.
  3. Archival bridge recovery for Dholavira, Lothal and Harappa, namely obtaining a Dholavira accession-to-CISI concordance (predicting that at least 85% of the 147 remaining Dholavira candidate rows match local unregistered objects, failing below 60%), inspecting the ASI negative register for Lothal Plate CLXI F (1864 vs. 1830), and inspecting HARP's 1996 object cards for H-2587 (H96/7158-01 vs. H96/7458-0001).
