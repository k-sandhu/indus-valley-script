# Line-trained segmentation, graphic families, and cross-city circulation of Indus seal legends

Complete working manuscript, 22 November 2026 (synthesizing packages recorded 12–24 October 2026), prepared with AI research and writing assistance. Author names, affiliations and a submission venue have not been assigned. Draft status: awaiting independent human epigraphic and archaeological review before submission (Section 7).

## Abstract

Indus Valley seals carry short sign sequences whose internal boundaries, graphic structure and administrative circulation remain open empirical questions. We bring together seven dated experimental packages on the pinned Lipi export of the Indus corpus, comparative readable seal corpora, and primary plate audits to measure how seal legends are built and where they recur. First, grouping Wells sign codes into graphic base families improves held-out sign prediction across 8,535 tokens in five object-grouped folds by 0.0219 bits per sign over a Kneser–Ney bigram baseline (p = 0.005 against 200 size-matched random partitions), lowering unseen-sign loss from 14.60 to 14.19 bits (13.93 under published Parpola and Mahadevan classes), whereas grouping signs by added modifiers fails (0.0035 bits, net 0.0006, p = 0.114); random contiguous cuts of the Wells catalogue order already recover about three quarters of the family gain (mean 0.0164 bits). Second, catalogued line divisions separate recurring units: lines recur as contiguous stretches elsewhere more often than break-crossing windows (23 against 4 faces under the conservative line order, 42 ties, p = 0.0003). Auditing 75 photographed multi-line faces confirms 65 divisions with zero contradictions and reveals that the Lipi visual code reverses catalogue reading order as a whole. A linear model trained only on physical line divisions finds held-out divisions on 33 plate-ordered faces with Youden's J = 0.718 (95% confidence interval 0.586 to 0.838; 27 of 34 hits, 7.7% cut rate elsewhere), beating the Dirichlet-process segmenter (0.348), greedy n-gram matching (0.340) and branching entropy (0.117). Calibrated on 309 Ur III Sumerian seal legends with 75 single-line labels, the same model reaches word-boundary F1 = 0.618 (precision 0.908, recall 0.468) against 0.557 for cutting everywhere and 0.692 for branching entropy, showing that line-division training isolates the strongest structural breaks with high precision. Third, a frozen 49-formula closing stock and 225-unit inventory achieve complete inter-implementation agreement across 171 held legends (776 positions, Cohen's κ = 1.00); 76.0% of held legends close with a stock formula against 20.7% shuffled, and four hashed prospective predictions await unread CISI 3.1 and post-catalogue seals. Fourth, Indus stroke values bind more tightly to following signs than Ur III counts bind to commodities (3.61 against 4.53 to 5.15 distinct values per item; top-value share 0.606 against 0.407 to 0.450), and comparing five readable seal corpora (Indo-Aryan, Tamil-Brahmi, Ur III, Old Akkadian and Sargonic Sumerian) shows that final-sign concentration tracks closing formulae rather than head direction. Finally, across 1,430 seals from 29 sites, 65 recurring texts span 174 seals (41 at multiple sites) while zero of 445 seals of six or more signs repeat; the frozen locality test gives p = 0.31, motif concordance is unicorn-driven (88 of 89 matches), 13 of 16 matching sealings link only to other cities, plate measurements show Lothal sealings L-161 to L-170 were carved separately from Mohenjo-daro seal M-895, and Rao (1985) assigns Plate CLXI F conflictingly to both L-164 (1864) and L-161 (1830).

## 1. Introduction

Most inscribed artifacts from the Indus Valley Civilization are stamp seals bearing short sequences of two to eight signs above an animal or scene. Because the script remains undeciphered, structural studies have long asked how these short legends are composed: whether graphically similar signs share distributional contexts, where internal unit boundaries fall, whether stroke signs count commodities or enter fixed expressions, why text endings are far more constrained than text beginnings, and whether identical legends stay in one city or circulate across settlements.

In practice, these questions have usually been tested in isolation, often without holding related artifacts out of training folds, without calibrating segmentation methods on readable seal corpora of comparable length, and without checking catalogue transcriptions against printed plate photographs. When multiple faces of one object, duplicate impressions or catalogue aliases cross between training and test splits, statistical models overstate predictability. When unsupervised segmenters are applied directly to an unread script, there is no ground truth to show whether a high recall on recurring strings reflects genuine boundaries or over-segmentation.

This manuscript brings together seven dated experimental packages carried out between 12 and 24 October 2026 under a shared computational-epigraphy framework. Rather than proposing phonetic values or lexical translations, we test what physical and distributional regularities survive object-grouped validation, plate inspection and readable-script calibration:

1. **Graphic families and modifiers.** We test whether grouping Wells sign codes by visual base shape or by added graphic modifiers predicts rare and unseen signs in held-out inscriptions better than size-matched random partitions and contiguous cuts of the Wells catalogue order.
2. **Physical line breaks and line-trained segmentation.** We test whether lines separated by a catalogue line division recur more often than stretches crossing that division, audit the divisions and reading order directly on printed CISI plates, train a linear boundary model on physical line breaks alone, and calibrate that exact procedure on Ur III Sumerian seal legends where both line and word divisions are known.
3. **Frozen closing stock and prospective tests.** From the line-trained model we freeze a 49-formula closing stock and explicit segmentation rules, verify independent implementation agreement on held-out legends, and lock four prospective predictions with cryptographic hashes for unread CISI 3.1 and post-catalogue seals.
4. **Numeral fixity and cross-script seal comparisons.** We measure how tightly Indus stroke values bind to neighbouring signs compared with Ur III accounting tablets and Linear B records, and we test whether the strong final-sign concentration of Indus legends reflects head-final syntax by building comparative Indo-Aryan, Tamil-Brahmi, Ur III and Old Akkadian seal-legend corpora.
5. **Cross-city circulation and Lothal plate verification.** We quantify duplicate seal texts, motif concordance and cross-city sealings across 29 South Asian sites, measure the carved proportions of Lothal sealings L-161 to L-170 against Mohenjo-daro seal M-895 on printed plates, and audit archival conflicts in the primary Lothal excavation report.

Together, these measurements narrow what a mature Harappan seal legend looks like structurally: a largely unique opening built from graphically organized sign families and locally constrained stroke combinations, joined to a closing formula drawn from a small shared stock that scribes tended to keep intact when breaking a legend across two lines, with short recurring legends and stamped clay closures linking distant cities.

## 2. Related work

Structural and distributional studies of the Indus script have a fifty-year history, and several broad patterns measured here build directly on published observations. We state prior work explicitly so that our empirical additions are clearly separated from established ideas.

**Sign catalogues, graphic decomposition and allographs.** Iravatham Mahadevan (1977) established the modern concordance, positional frequency tables and baseline sign list, followed by Asko Parpola's sign list and structural syntheses (1994) and Bryan Wells's epigraphic catalogue and allographic classification (2015), later expanded in the Interactive Corpus of Indus Texts (ICIT; Fuls 2023). Nisha Yadav and Mayank Vahia (2011) systematically decomposed Indus signs into basic signs, provisional basic signs and modifiers, arguing that sign modification follows standardized design rules across the civilization. Daggumati and Revesz (2021) proposed computational allograph groupings without a size-matched null comparator, and a public repository started on 15 October 2026 (Kumar 2026) reported held-out perplexity gains from merging allograph pairs. Our step 1 experiment adds a held-out, object-grouped measurement predicting fine Wells codes from base families, modifier classes, published Mahadevan and Parpola classes, and contiguous cuts of the Wells number line.

**Segmentation, terminal classes and line divisions.** Kimmo Koskenniemi (1981) combined segmentation with syntactic classification and pairwise sign constraints. Mahadevan (1982) identified the "jar" sign and its functional equivalents as a distinct class of terminal ideograms. Rao and colleagues (2009) applied conditional entropy and modified Kneser–Ney Markov models to missing-sign prediction and corpus structure, while Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) and Sproat (2014) argued that short inscription length and low sign repetition inside single texts align with nonlinguistic emblem systems. Yadav and colleagues (2010) used n-grams to extract recurring combinations and showed that text enders are far more tightly constrained than text beginners. Khanna and Merriam (2025) recently proposed distributional sign and pair classes. On physical layout, Bahata Ansumali Mukhopadhyay (2019) treated each inscribed line as a complete phrase by assumption, and M. V. Bhaskar (2024, Section 3) explicitly examined when physical line breaks affect sign adjacency and how Mahadevan's 1977 corpus handled them. Building on these precedents, we test line-break recurrence statistically against break-crossing windows, verify line order against plate photographs, train a cross-validated boundary predictor strictly on physical line divisions, calibrate that predictor on Ur III seal legends, and freeze a closing-formula stock with prospective tests for unread seals.

**Numerals, seal functions and cross-city circulation.** Mahadevan (1977) tabulated numeral-sign collocations, Parpola (1994) interpreted specific stroke-and-fish combinations as fixed expressions, Wells (2015, Tables 5.1–5.3) printed right-adjacent stroke collocations, and Mukhopadhyay (2023) observed four sign classes almost invariably preceded by three short strokes. For seal function, Parpola (2008) interpreted short seal legends in terms of personal names and offices, drawing qualitative parallels with Mesopotamian seals, while Mukhopadhyay (2019, Fig. 4; 2023, Figs. 14 and 22c) argued for taxation, licensing and commodity control by citing identical legends across distant sites and matching Lothal sealings to Mohenjo-daro seals by example. Parpola (1986) and Dennys Frenez and Maurizio Tosi (2005) analysed the Lothal clay sealings excavated by S. R. Rao (1985), with Frenez and Tosi grouping L-161 to L-172 as impressions of a single elephant seal. What our packages add are corpus-wide permutation tests of duplicate locality, motif concordance and bigram coincidence rates; comparative statistical baselines for stroke fixity against Ur III tablets and Linear B; quantitative edge-asymmetry and title-order measurements across five readable seal corpora; and ruled plate measurements separating the Lothal elephant sealings from Mohenjo-daro seal M-895 alongside an audit of Rao's plate references.

**Contemporaneous public record.** Before our 12–24 October 2026 packages were run, Daniel Bourdeau's public Cyphersolver repository (inspected at commit `1c73645947deba694cb82d42dba967319e8df5a5`, dated 1 October 2026) already documented exploratory analyses of Indus line breaks, seal individuality compared against Ur III and Linear B, stratigraphic variation, and chance baselines for Dravidian compounds. That snapshot is an unreviewed project record and we neither adopt its conclusions nor treat its alternative transcriptions as independent physical observations, but it establishes that those general analytical directions were publicly documented prior to our October runs.

## 3. Corpora, shared assumptions, and methods overview

All Indus analyses draw from one pinned digital transcription, the Lipi export (`yajnadevam/lipi`, commit `fc8813a5`), normalized locally into `data/normalized/lipi_observed_faces.jsonl` (5,680 inscribed faces) and `data/normalized/lipi_clean_rtl_singleline.jsonl` (complete, single-line faces read right to left with no damaged or unknown signs). Signs are coded in the Wells and ICIT numbering scheme. Deduplicating complete clean faces of at least two signs gives the main structural frame of 1,859 distinct texts (8,535 sign tokens across 577 attested sign types). Seal-specific subsets filter to object types beginning with `SEAL`, yielding 1,312 distinct seal legends in the clean structural frame and 1,430 complete single-line seals from 29 named South Asian sites (one face per object family) in the circulation study.

Five methodological conventions apply across every study in this paper and are collected here rather than repeated in each result section:

1. **Object-level grouping.** Inscribed faces that share a catalogue alias, belong to the same physical object, or carry identical text sequences are linked into connected components. Every cross-validation split, held-out calibration set and block bootstrap resamples or holds out entire components so that copies and multi-face objects never leak across training and evaluation folds.
2. **Protocol freezing versus post-outcome checks.** Primary hypotheses, scoring metrics and pass rules were written and SHA-256 hashed before endpoints were computed. Sensitivity analyses, review reruns and exploratory decompositions computed after seeing a primary endpoint are labelled explicitly as post-outcome or exploratory and never overwrite a frozen result.
3. **Reading direction and line order.** The clean frame follows the Lipi catalogue's right-to-left reading-order field (`signs_reading_rtl`), which places the opening sign first and the closing sign last. For multi-line faces, we test both line orders and verify physical line placement directly on printed photographs from *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions* Volumes 1 and 2 (Joshi and Parpola 1987; Shah and Parpola 1991).
4. **AI visual readings of printed halftones.** Both the step 1 sign-family codings and the plate audits of multi-line faces and Lothal sealings were conducted by AI models inspecting rendered glyph sheets and halftone scans (~100 dpi for CISI 1, ~300-pixel crops for Lipi). Those readings document reproducible visual observations under explicit blinding rules, but they require confirmation by human epigraphists and archaeologists before final publication (Section 7).
5. **Structural scope.** All measurements describe graphic distributions, segmentation boundaries, comparative corpus statistics and material circulation. No sound value, lexical gloss or language affiliation is assigned to any Indus sign.

Detailed feature formulas, fold counts, benchmark commands (`python -m bench segmentation`) and cryptographic provenance hashes are documented in [methods.md](methods.md), and every reported number is mapped to its exact output file and JSON key in [claim-evidence-map.md](claim-evidence-map.md).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Graphic base families predict unseen signs, while modifier classes fail

Grouping fine Wells sign codes by their graphic base shape improves held-out sign prediction across 8,535 tokens in five component-grouped folds, whereas grouping signs by added modifiers does not. Two coders (both instances of Claude Opus 5.5 working from code-ordered glyph sheets showing 48 signs per sheet with token counts) decomposed all 722 Lipi font codes into a base family and a 14-term modifier vocabulary. Across the 577 attested codes, the two family partitions agree with an unweighted adjusted Rand index of 0.884 and a token-weighted adjusted Rand index of 0.975, agreeing on cut points between adjacent Wells codes with Cohen's κ = 0.79. Modifier agreement ranged from κ = 0.93 for enclosure and 0.88 for dots down to 0.54 for top elements and 0.38 for stylistic variants.

To test whether these graphic classes carry distributional information, we interpolated a class-based bigram model with an interpolated Kneser–Ney bigram baseline over original Wells codes (baseline cross-entropy 5.893 bits per sign; unigram 7.058 bits). Every model predicts the exact original Wells code, so gains are directly comparable across granularities. Because interpolating with any partition gives a small mixture gain of 0.0029 bits even when every sign sits in its own class, Table 1 reports both raw gains and gains net of that identity partition, compared against 200 random partitions of matching group sizes.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Base family, coder 1 | 85 | 0.0219 | 0.0190 | 0.0061 | 0.005 | 14.19 |
| Base family, coder 2 | 63 | 0.0222 | 0.0193 | 0.0069 | 0.005 | 14.22 |

Base families pass comfortably for both coders (0.0219 and 0.0222 bits per sign, p = 0.005), as do the finer V, P and M allograph inventories. The improvement is concentrated almost entirely on rare and unseen signs: for the 267 held-out tokens of codes never seen in training, loss drops from 14.60 bits to 14.19 bits under coder 1's base families and further to 13.93 bits under the published Parpola-level and Mahadevan-level groupings. For signs seen once or twice (348 tokens), base families reduce loss from 11.44 to 11.29 bits; for frequent signs seen ten or more times (7,112 tokens), loss is unchanged (4.847 to 4.859 bits).

A post-outcome review check shows how much of this signal is already encoded in the Wells catalogue ordering. Because 479 of 576 adjacent attested Wells codes share a coder 1 base family, placing 85 random contiguous cuts along the Wells number line with no visual judgement achieves a mean gain of 0.0164 bits per sign across 100 trials (5th–95th percentile 0.0124 to 0.0197, maximum 0.0212). All 100 contiguous partitions beat the frozen random-partition null, and none reaches coder 1's 0.0219 bits. Thus roughly three quarters of the base-family gain comes from the graphic ordering already built into the Wells catalogue, while visual family grouping adds about 0.005 bits per sign. The base-family gain persists when numeral families are split into singletons (0.0231 bits), when one-substitution near-copies are merged into the same fold (0.0265 bits) and when an end-of-text symbol is included (0.0222 bits).

By contrast, added graphic modifiers fail the distributional test despite being visually productive across bases (for example, inner marks appear on 24 bases across 121 codes and 1,199 tokens, side elements on 25 bases, doubling on 24 and hatching on 23). Grouping signs across bases by their modifier signature yields 0.0035 bits (net 0.0006 bits), below the 95th percentile of random partitions (0.0044, p = 0.114). Tested individually after the outcome, none of nine modifier categories passes (p from 0.14 to 0.93). At this corpus size and coding granularity, contextual distribution tracks the base sign rather than the added mark.

### 4.2 Catalogue line divisions mark recurring units, and plate inspection fixes reading order

Catalogue line breaks supply boundary evidence internal to the Indus corpus (`F-0018`). Of 118 faces in the Lipi export carrying a `/` division marker in the visual code, 40 contain damaged or unknown signs, 6 lack a line or break-crossing window of at least two signs, and 3 are boustrophedon, leaving 69 clean multi-line faces (61 square seals). We asked how often each separated line recurs as a contiguous stretch inside a clean single-line text from another object, compared with windows of the exact same lengths straddling the line division.

Pooling both possible orders of the lines, lines recur at a per-face mean rate of 23.7% against 8.4% for break-crossing windows (pooled over windows, 25.2% against 6.0%): lines score higher on 25 faces, crossing windows on 3, with 41 ties (two-sided sign test p = 0.00003). Under the conservative rule that picks whichever line order gives crossing windows their best chance on each face, crossing recurrence rises to 12.8%, yet lines still win on 23 faces against 4 with 42 ties (p = 0.0003). Post-outcome review controls confirm that the advantage survives deduplicating identical faces (22 against 4, p = 0.0005), dropping faces where one line holds only stroke numerals (17 against 2, p = 0.0007), and shifting the division to every other internal position of the joined text, which drops the mean line-minus-crossing difference from 0.186 to 0.029 (p = 0.0001). By contrast, a parallel Kober-style search for alternating stems (`F-0019`) produces only 2 alternating stems out of 68 (rate 0.029), lying inside the subsampled ranges of all four readable comparison corpora (Ur III seal legends 0.015–0.095, Sumerian lines 0–0.045, Akkadian letters 0–0.061, Egyptian sentences 0–0.062), and a step 5 rehearsal (`F-0020`, `F-0021`) showed that alternation fails to separate readable scripts even on their true words (0.056–0.069 across all four systems).

Because line-order ambiguity affects any supervised segmenter, we audited all 118 divided faces against CISI 1 and 2 photographs before training. Forty-three faces have no held crop (32 CISI 3 numbers and 11 lacking CISI numbers). Three AI readers, each assigned 25 faces with a blind brief separated from segmentation scoring, inspected the 75 photographed faces. Fifty-one faces were legible in full, 21 partly legible and 3 illegible; 65 line divisions were confirmed (visible sign groups matching catalogue counts within one sign), 10 were unclear and 0 were contradicted. Confidence was rated high on 17 faces, medium on 33 and low on 25.

The audit exposed a systematic encoding rule in the Lipi export. On 55 of the 75 photographed faces, the first segment of the Lipi visual code sits on the lower line, and on only 8 does it sit on the upper line (4 left, right or picture field; 8 undetermined). Because the export's `signs_reading_rtl` field reverses the entire visual code string from end to end, the visual code places the line read first at the end, and reading the visual code in reverse places the upper line first on 43 of 46 faces adjudicated with high or medium confidence. Only three high- or medium-confidence faces (H-60, H-97 and K-16) place the lower line first in catalogue reading order; on H-60 that choice produces a canonical opening-to-closing sequence (820-002 … 740-621), indicating that catalogue editors occasionally ordered lines by textual convention. Removing damaged and boustrophedon faces left 75 segmenter-usable faces (79 divisions), of which 33 received a verified plate order (30 reversed visual-code order, 3 forward).

A follow-up visual review (`2026-10-22-face-adjudication`) re-examined the 29 photographed faces that remained unresolved after the initial audit, inspecting 29 primary images and 8 alternate views under a SHA-256-sealed first pass before consulting rendered catalogue fonts. All 29 received a disposition: 21 yielded accepted physical-layout observations (6 high, 15 medium, 8 low confidence) and 12 yielded provisional maps from catalogue segments to visible graphic groups, endorsed by a second independent pass, while 0 historical reading traversals were assigned. That review also uncovered concrete geometry hazards that a binary reversal flag cannot capture, including a three-segment face with two differently rotated lower groups, a six-stroke sign stacked in two tiers that image tier-counting alone would split, a composite photograph bundling two front views and a reverse view, and two byte-identical impression files.

### 4.3 Line-trained segmentation and readable-script calibration on Ur III seal legends

We trained a linear boundary classifier solely on physical line divisions, treating each scribe's break as a choice among the internal positions of a joined legend. Every candidate position receives a score from eleven corpus statistics (forward and backward branching-entropy changes, pointwise mutual information, how often the preceding sign or pair ends single-line texts, how often the following sign or pair starts them, crossing triple frequency and window recurrence drops), each computed with the scored face's entire object-family component removed from the corpus. Two layout features absorb where lines tend to wrap during training and are dropped at test time so the segmenter relies purely on textual cues. Five-fold cross-validation grouped by object components ensures that no face or its relatives appear in both training and evaluation.

Because a physical line division marks where a unit boundary must fall without guaranteeing that other positions are non-boundaries, precision cannot be measured directly on Indus faces. Every method is therefore scored with `python -m bench segmentation` by Youden's $J = \text{recall}_{\text{div}} - \text{rate}_{\text{other}}$, where cutting everywhere, cutting nowhere and cutting at random all score zero.

| Plate order (primary) | 33 | **0.718** (0.586–0.838) | −0.006 | 0.117 | 0.340 | 0.348 |
| Catalogue reading order | 75 | 0.627 (0.523–0.724) | 0.021 | 0.106 | 0.321 | 0.361 |
| Visual code order | 75 | 0.267 (0.158–0.381) | 0.005 | −0.122 | 0.390 | 0.265 |
| Worse order per method | 75 | 0.128 (0.038–0.226) | −0.103 | −0.212 | 0.293 | 0.230 |

*Table 2. Five-fold component-grouped cross-validated Youden's J on Indus multi-line divisions (95% component-bootstrap confidence intervals in parentheses for the line-trained model).*

On the primary set of 33 plate-ordered faces (34 required divisions), the line-trained segmenter finds 27 divisions (recall 79.4%) while cutting only 7.7% of other internal positions, yielding J = 0.718 (95% CI 0.586 to 0.838) and a within-face ranking AUC of 0.877. Both pre-registered decision rules pass: the margin exceeds 0.25 with a lower bound above 0.10, and the paired improvement over the Dirichlet-process segmenter is +0.370 (95% CI 0.227 to 0.512, 100% of bootstrap draws positive). Greedy n-gram matching and the Dirichlet process achieve high recall (94.1% and 97.1%) only by cutting 60.1% and 62.3% of non-division positions. Moving each face's line breaks to random positions and refitting across 200 permutations gives a mean J of 0.010 and a maximum of 0.222 (p = 0.005). Performance depends strongly on reading order (dropping from 0.627 in catalogue reading order to 0.267 in visual-code order) and on layout: on the 30 wrapped two-line faces the model scores J = 0.790 (recall 87.1%, cut rate elsewhere 8.1%), whereas on the 3 faces where a second group stands apart in the picture field it scores 0.000. An independent replay in the field novelty audit reproduced the primary bench endpoint to full precision (27 of 34 hits, recall 0.7941, cut rate elsewhere 0.0765, precision and F1 explicitly null).

To understand what J ≈ 0.72 on line divisions means for true lexical word boundaries, we ran the exact same training and feature pipeline on 309 distinct Ur III Sumerian seal legends (1,187 word divisions, of which 718 are line divisions, and every line division coincides with a word division). In each cross-validation fold, the primary calibration trained the model on exactly one line division from each of 75 legends (matching the Indus training scale) and evaluated its cuts against all held-out word boundaries.

*Table 3. Five-fold cross-validated calibration on 309 Ur III Sumerian seal legends (1,187 word divisions, 718 line divisions).*

The 75-label line-trained model passes its pre-stated calibration rule, exceeding cutting everywhere by 0.061 F1 (0.618 against 0.557, threshold 0.05). More importantly, Table 3 explains why the method rankings invert between line divisions and complete word boundaries. On Ur III seal legends, the 75-label line-trained model achieves J = 0.683 at line divisions and makes word cuts that are correct nine times in ten (precision 0.908), but it finds fewer than half of all word boundaries (recall 0.468). Branching entropy scores poorly on line divisions (J = 0.332) because it cuts freely across internal word junctions, yet that higher cut rate lifts its word-boundary recall to 0.761 and gives it the highest overall word-boundary F1 (0.692, precision 0.635). Training on one physical line break per legend teaches a conservative threshold that isolates the strongest structural joint of a legend (such as between personal name, patronymic and title) with very few false positives, rather than segmenting every constituent morpheme or word.

### 4.4 Frozen closing stock, inter-implementation agreement, and prospective predictions

Applying the conservative threshold of the trained model directly to the 1,687-text build corpus (version 1) cut only 4.5% of positions and yielded a closing stock of just one formula closing 9 of 994 seal legends of three or more signs. Amendment 1, written before the held-out calibration set was opened, kept the trained scoring function unchanged and added one rule: each legend is also cut at its single highest-scoring internal position (the point where the model would break the line), and any stretch of one to four signs following that junction that closes seal legends in at least three unrelated object components enters the closing stock.

Version 2 cuts 29.1% of internal positions in the build corpus (mean unit length 2.23 signs), defines an inventory of 225 recurring multi-sign units (134 of two signs, 73 of three, 15 of four, 2 of five, 1 of six) and freezes a closing stock of 49 formulae: 13 one-sign, 27 two-sign and 9 three-sign sequences. Across the 994 build-corpus seal legends of three or more signs, 719 (72.3%) end in one of these 49 formulae. The commonest closing formulae by unrelated object components are `400` (22), `090` (16), `176-740` (10), `621` (9), `679` (8), `220-520` and `923-740` (7 each), and `151`, `760-740`, `820` and `840-032-740` (6 each).

Explicit human-readable rules (`RULES-v2.md`) apply the frozen lists deterministically: Rule 1 selects the longest final stretch of one to four signs belonging to the 49-formula closing stock, and Rule 2 partitions the preceding opening into the fewest units from the 225-unit inventory. To verify that the rules are unambiguous, an independent agent received only `RULES-v2.md`, the two lists and the held-out calibration set H (171 seal legends of three or more signs from 15% of object components, set aside by hash before inventory construction) and wrote its own parser. Its output matched our reference implementation on every single boundary across all 776 internal positions and all 171 closings (Cohen's κ = 1.00, 100% identical segmentations and closings), passing the operational standard (κ ≥ 0.80, ≥ 90% identical closings). The independent coder also surfaced an explicit consequence of the rule order: because Rule 1 fixes the closing before Rule 2 cuts the opening, the closing splits an inventory unit on 47 of the 171 held legends. Because the deterministic rules use the stock directly rather than the model's per-legend peak score, the rules and the model's direct junction agree on the closing of 35.1% of held legends (rules cut 74.4% of positions because most openings contain no multi-sign inventory unit).

Scored once on the 171 held-out legends in H, the frozen stock reveals a sharp opening–closing asymmetry: 130 of 171 legends (76.0%) close with a stock formula, against 20.7% when each legend's signs are shuffled (97.5th quantile 26.3%). Final sign pairs occur as text endings in the build corpus for 78.9% of held legends against 60.2% for initial sign pairs as text beginnings (54 legends with only the ending known against 22 with only the beginning, one-sided exact McNemar p = 0.00016). Only 21 of 123 scored openings of two or more signs (17.1%) occur anywhere in the build corpus. On the 58 distinct divided seal legends in catalogue reading order, the longer line read alone ends in a stock formula 36 times (62.1%, or 62.7% on the 59-legend calibration count), against 22.2% when the break is shifted to every other position; on the 32 two-line plate-ordered faces, the joined legend ends in a stock formula on only 6 faces whereas the top line alone ends in one on 21, showing that scribes typically ended the main line at the closing formula and set a short one- to three-sign remainder below (28 of 32 faces). Because those divided seals trained the model, the 62% figure is in-sample, and Amendment 2 moved divided legends to their own prospective test.

### 4.5 Stroke-value fixity compared with Ur III administrative accounts

If Indus stroke signs functioned primarily as open-ended commodity counts like Ur III accounting numerals, each counted sign would take a wide range of values. Across the 1,859 distinct clean texts, 1,265 numeral tokens from the short-stroke and tall-stroke families precede a non-numeral sign (or text end), yielding 56 qualifying following signs with at least five tokens (996 tokens; `F-0024`). Comparing this corpus against 200 size-matched subsamples of Ur III administrative tablets (3,421 pairs) and full Linear B commodity–number records (201 pairs across 12 qualifying items) shows that Indus stroke values are consistently more fixed to their neighbours than administrative counts.

In the frozen Indus analysis, stroke value and following sign share 1.074 bits of mutual information against a permutation mean of 0.611 bits (p = 0.0001). Because the frozen Ur III value definition kept only the numeric digit *n* of `n(unit)` (dropping unit differences on 865 of 3,421 pairs and retaining only the last token of 780 compound numbers), post-outcome review checks tested four stricter definitions. Keeping full stroke families for Indus and full digit-plus-unit values for Ur III preserves the gap (4.50 values per item against 6.09 to 7.05; top-value share 0.551 against 0.357 to 0.405; MI ratio 0.524 against 0.447 to 0.484). Rarefying every item to five tokens gives 2.73 values against 3.26 to 3.56 (top share 0.608 against 0.453 to 0.510), removing all value-2 tokens leaves 691 tokens at 3.46 values per item and 0.562 top share, and 16 of 56 Indus items have a single value accounting for at least 80% of tokens against 2 to 8 of ~43 items in Ur III.

Two directional findings refine how these combinations work. First, short strokes (787 tokens) and tall strokes (478 tokens) precede different signs (Jensen–Shannon divergence 0.420 bits, p = 0.0001): short strokes concentrate before `390`, `220`, `240`, `235` and `803`, whereas tall strokes concentrate before `220`, text end, `740`, `520` and `700`. Value frequencies are non-monotonic: two strokes dominate (574 tokens, versus 196 for one and 248 for three), seven strokes (59) outnumber six (33), and the twelve-stroke grid (28) outnumbers each of eight through eleven (6, 4, 1). Second, testing which side carries more information about the stroke value (with unit-aware Ur III values, which correctly detect Ur III's number-before-commodity order at 0.735 bits following against 0.514 preceding) shows the opposite polarity for Indus: the preceding sign carries more excess mutual information than the following sign (0.819 against 0.691 bits with family-aware values; 0.480 against 0.407 in the frozen run), yielding a net MI ratio of 0.221 when the preceding sign is treated as the item.

Accordingly, near-fixed stroke pairs emerge in both directions (`F-0025`). Treating the following sign as the item, sign `520` follows three tall strokes in 38 of 41 occurrences (92.7%, across 38 distinct texts from five named sites) and sign `585` follows seven strokes in 16 of 17 occurrences (94.1%, across 16 texts from four sites), while signs such as fish `220`, jar `740`, `390`, `405` and U-shaped `700` each accept five to eight different values. Treating the preceding sign as the item, the text-opening signs `861`, `817` and `820` precede two short strokes in 98%, 92% and 77% of cases. Because this comparison sets seal and tablet inscriptions against administrative accounts, standard allotments or ranks inscribed on seals could also show high value fixity; the contrast specifically separates Indus stroke usage from open-ended commodity counting.

### 4.6 Edge asymmetry and head-final seal-legend controls

Indus texts begin with a wide variety of signs and end with a small set (`F-0022`). Measuring edge asymmetry as the normalized Shannon entropy of initial signs minus that of final signs gives +0.260 across 1,859 clean distinct texts (initial 0.768, final 0.508; within-text shuffle 95% range −0.017 to +0.018) and +0.270 across 1,312 distinct seal texts (0.755 minus 0.486). Although 35.2% of clean texts end in the jar sign `740` (and the top three finals cover 51.8%), removing every text ending in `740` still leaves an edge asymmetry of +0.149, confirming that final concentration spans a broader closing class.

At first glance, Indus sign-level asymmetry (+0.260) looks like the mirror image of Ur III Sumerian seal legends (−0.204 at sign level on 309 legends). However, step 6 (`F-0023`) showed that edge asymmetry flips sign with the choice of unit and genre in readable head-initial languages: Ur III seal legends reverse to +0.079 at word level, Egyptian hieroglyphic sentences score +0.044 at sign level and −0.107 at word level, and Ur III administrative tablets sit near zero (+0.005 sign, −0.004 word). On Ur III seals, the negative sign-level score reflects personal-name spelling: 78 of 309 legends open with *ur* and 58 with *lu₂*, while removing the 30 opening with the divine determinative shifts the value only from −0.204 to −0.182.

To test directly whether edge asymmetry separates head-final from head-initial seal traditions, we compiled four comparative object-linked seal-legend corpora (`2026-10-21-head-direction-corpora`): Indo-Aryan seals and sealings from Basarh and Bhita (Bloch 1903–04, Spooner 1913–14, Marshall 1911–12; 245 eligible sign-level legends from 495 objects), Tamil-Brahmi seals, rings and coins (Mahadevan 2003, Tables 1.6–1.8; 23 eligible legends), the full ORACC Ur III Sumerian seal archive (3,044 eligible legends from 31,220 objects), and Old Akkadian seals and sealings from ORACC and CDLI (11 eligible Akkadian legends and 42 Sargonic Sumerian legends).

Because the frozen discrimination protocol requires at least two corpora of 25 eligible legends in each typological group (and at least 20 in the pre-specified second panel), the shortfall of provenienced, complete Old Akkadian legends (11, reflecting museum-acquired cylinder seals without findspots and broken impressions) and Tamil-Brahmi legends (23) left every frozen statistic untestable (`testable: false`, zero statistics kept). Placing Indus legends on head direction therefore remains formally closed.

Nevertheless, the comparative values settle why sign-level edge asymmetry cannot diagnose head direction. At matched size (n = 196), Indo-Aryan legends separate completely from Ur III legends across all 2,000 replicate draws (+0.250 against −0.219; top-3 final concentration 0.628 against 0.204; stock closure beyond shuffle +0.264 against −0.025; word-internal asymmetry +0.204 against −0.111), and in the n = 18 panel Tamil-Brahmi aligns with Indo-Aryan (+0.363, 0.889, +0.527, +0.411). Yet the 11 Old Akkadian legends, a head-initial Semitic language, also show strong positive edge asymmetry (+0.134, outside their within-legend shuffle range of −0.073 to +0.092), whereas contemporary Sargonic Sumerian legends sit inside chance (+0.020). Post-run counts explain the pattern: final-sign concentration tracks whether the local sealing convention ends in a fixed grammatical suffix or formula (genitive *-sya* or *-sa* in 147 of 245 Indo-Aryan legends, *-ṉ* in 15 of 23 Tamil-Brahmi legends, and possessive *-šu* in 5 of 11 Akkadian legends), whereas Ur III seals typically close with an open-ended personal name (the father's name after *dumu* in 1,701 of 3,044 legends). Conversely, an exploratory count over all lexical members (including single-word Sanskrit compounds) points to the relative order of title and personal name as the feature that tracks head direction: Indo-Aryan legends put the title before the name in 57 of 61 adjacent pairs (+0.87), whereas Ur III puts the title second in 2,393 of 2,475 pairs (−0.93), Akkadian in 10 of 15 (−0.33), and Sargonic Sumerian in 34 of 46 (−0.48). For Indus seals, high edge asymmetry confirms the presence of closing formulae already isolated by our segmenter rather than determining language family or head direction.

### 4.7 Seal-text circulation, motif concordance, and cross-city sealings

Across 1,430 complete single-line seals from 29 named South Asian sites (led by Mohenjo-daro with 917 seals, Harappa with 257, Dholavira with 64, Lothal with 51, Kalibangan with 36 and Chanhu-daro with 33), seal legends are overwhelmingly individual compositions at long lengths, while short recurring texts circulate across cities (`F-0026`–`F-0030`).

**Duplicate locality and length structure.** Sixty-five distinct texts occur on two or more seals, covering 174 seals in total (183 same-text seal pairs), and 41 of those 65 texts occur at more than one site (`F-0026`). No two of the 445 seals with six or more signs share an identical text (`F-0027`). Under the frozen primary locality test (`F-0028`), the text-weighted mean share of same-text seal pairs recovered from the same city is 0.473, compared with 0.448 when site labels are shuffled globally across all 1,430 seals (95% range 0.345 to 0.552, ratio 1.06, one-sided p = 0.313; pair-weighted 0.415 against 0.448, p = 0.747). Under the frozen protocol, duplicate seal texts therefore do not show overall single-site concentration.

Two exploratory sensitivities clarify what sits beneath that pooled null result. First, short texts come from a wider spread of sites, so holding text length fixed while shuffling sites lowers the null expectation from 0.448 to 0.382, placing the observed text-weighted share (0.473) modestly above chance (ratio 1.24, p = 0.032; merging Mayig graphic variants gives 0.479 against 0.394, p = 0.023 across 81 shared texts). Second, decomposing same-site pairs by city shows a split between Mohenjo-daro and smaller settlements: Mohenjo-daro matches its length-stratified expectation (59 observed against 60.2 expected, p = 0.57), Harappa is slightly above expectation (10 against 6.0, p = 0.11), and all smaller sites combined show local clustering (7 against 1.5 expected, p = 0.0025: three Banawali seals sharing one text and a second Banawali pair, two Dholavira pairs, one of which may be a single seal catalogued twice, and one Lothal pair). Restricting to the 513 seals outside Mohenjo-daro gives a same-site share of 0.413 against 0.286 expected (p = 0.073). Fitting a mixture model in which a fraction *f* of shared texts are strictly local and the rest follow the length-stratified null gives a point estimate of *f* = 0.15 (upper 95% bound 0.25). At Mohenjo-daro, pairs of duplicate seals with period labels share the same period slightly more often than chance (13 of 19 pairs, 68.4% against 45.8%, p = 0.051).

To test whether recurring four- and five-sign texts reflect deliberate whole-text reuse rather than independent composition from common sign transitions, we computed expected coincidence pairs under an interpolated Kneser–Ney bigram model. Observed duplicate pairs exceed the full-corpus bigram expectation modestly at short lengths (122 against 97.2 for two signs; 34 against 17.7 for three signs) and by 5.3-fold for four or more signs (27 observed against 5.13 expected: 24 against 4.05 at length four, 3 against 1.03 at length five). Refitting the bigram model only on non-seal texts gives a 4.2-fold excess, and only on seal texts a 3.1-fold excess; all three ratios sit below the protocol's 10-fold criterion for routine whole-text reuse. Exploratory tests of regionality in opening versus closing two-sign segments across 957 seals of four or more signs find both mildly local (openings 0.598 against 0.482, p = 0.004; closings 0.534 against 0.481, p = 0.041), while the difference between their ratios (0.135) falls comfortably inside its permutation range (−0.214 to +0.217, p = 0.110).

**Motif concordance is unicorn-driven.** Among 130 pairs of seals carrying identical texts where both seals preserve a field motif, 89 share their motif (68.5% against 61.6% under within-site shuffling, p = 0.149; text-weighted 72.6% against 62.0%, p = 0.033). However, 88 of those 89 matching pairs are unicorn-to-unicorn pairs (unicorns account for 913 of the 1,430 seals), and among the 42 pairs involving any other animal, only 1 shares its motif (`F-0029`). Including seals coded with no motif (mainly rectangular text-only seals) as a category raises pair-weighted concordance to 54.1% against 43.0% (p = 0.016). That increase comes from the separation between square animal seals and rectangular text-only seals, not from tight pairing of recurring legends to non-unicorn emblems.

**Cross-city sealings and the Lothal L-161–L-170 / M-895 plate audit.** Sixteen clay sealings or tags in the export carry a complete text found on an eligible seal, representing six distinct texts (`F-0030`). Thirteen of those 16 sealings (four distinct texts) match only seals recovered at other cities: ten Lothal bale sealings (L-161 to L-170) carry the four-sign legend of Mohenjo-daro seal fragment M-895; Kalibangan tag K-82 matches Mohenjo-daro seals M-1697 and M-712; Lothal tag L-146 matches two Mohenjo-daro seals; and a Rupar tag matches one Mohenjo-daro seal. The remaining three Mohenjo-daro tags match seals found at both Mohenjo-daro and other cities.

Because L-161 to L-170 are coded with an elephant while M-895 is catalogued as a unicorn, we ran a blinded two-reader plate audit (`2026-10-13-plate-checks`) on CISI 1 (pp. 276–278) and CISI 2 (p. 85) renders and ruled enlargements to test whether M-895 itself stamped the Lothal sealings or whether a second seal carried the same text:

1. **Legend match (P1: Pass).** Both AI readers independently confirmed the same four signs in the same order on M-895 (impression and mirrored seal) and on every Lothal sealing with a surviving text band (L-161 through L-167 complete or nearly complete; L-169 and L-170 retaining the right end; L-168 worn smooth).
2. **Animal motifs (P2: Unresolved).** Both readers identified an elephant on the Lothal sealings (heavy body, columnar legs, hanging trunk crossed by a tusk, clearest on L-161 and L-162; low-to-moderate confidence). On M-895, the first reader saw a single forward-curving horn and neck folds (unicorn), whereas the blinded second reader called the animal unidentifiable, possibly a horned animal facing right. Neither reader saw a trunk, tusk or ear on M-895, leaving P2 unresolved under the protocol's agreement rule until human specialist inspection.
3. **Carved proportions (P3: Pass).** Although the impressed face width on L-161 (23.4 mm, scale calibration range 21.1 to 24.2 mm) matches M-895's width (23.3 mm, catalogue 23.4 mm), the carving differs consistently across every measured sealing. The second sign (brackets enclosing strokes) occupies 38% to 45% of the text line on L-161 through L-167 (second reader: 37% to 41%) against 54% on M-895 (second reader: 53% to 54% on both views). The gap before the final sign spans 16% to 19% of the line on the sealings against 8% on M-895, the bracket interior uses short dashes rather than long bars, and M-895's final oval carries an inner outline absent on the sealings. The Lothal sealings were therefore stamped by a single seal (agreeing with Frenez and Tosi 2005) that was not M-895.

Finally, a bounded archival audit of the primary Lothal sources (`2026-10-24-office-seal-evidence`, `2026-10-24-office-seal-pass3`) established two unresolved bibliographic and material conflicts that constrain functional interpretations of the elephant sealings (`F-0042`, `F-0043`). First, while CISI 1 (p. 373) links L-164 to excavation object 1864 (museum number LTH SRG 1232), Rao (1985, Vol. II, p. 319) describes overlapping cords on 1864's flat underside and interprets it as a flat-stoppered jar closure, whereas Frenez and Tosi (2005, pp. 78–79) place L-161 to L-172 under Type 5 ("Wooden Surfaces (Boxes?)"), explicitly noting that box shape and size remain conjectural. Second, Rao's own text contains an internal plate conflict: printed p. 319 attaches Plate CLXI F to excavation object 1864 (L-164), whereas the plate entry on printed p. 325 attaches Plate CLXI F to excavation object 1830 (L-161 = LTH SRG 1195), alongside phase II–III, SRG 3, GX 10, warehouse layer 2 and a depth of two feet. Because neither inspected source resolves which specimen is photographed in Plate CLXI F, those warehouse layer fields cannot be assigned securely to L-164 through that plate, and the deposit total itself varies between 65 sealings (Rao 1979, Vol. I, p. 113) and 69 of 90 sealings (Rao 1985, Vol. II, p. 319).

## 5. Discussion

Taken together, these seven experimental packages sketch a coherent quantitative anatomy of Indus seal legends while identifying exactly where current evidence stops.

At the sign level, graphic base families capture distributional context for rare and unseen signs beyond random partitions, confirming statistically what Mahadevan (1977), Parpola (1994) and Wells (2015) encoded in their sign lists, while revealing that three quarters of that predictive gain already sits in the Wells catalogue order and that coarse modifier classes carry no detectable cross-base signal at current sample sizes. At the phrase level, physical line breaks prove to be a genuine structural cue: catalogued lines recur far more often than break-crossing stretches, and a linear classifier trained on just 75 line-break faces locates held-out divisions on plate-ordered faces with J = 0.718, more than doubling the Dirichlet-process baseline.

The Ur III calibration keeps that segmentation result properly scaled. A line-trained model operating at J ≈ 0.7 on physical breaks achieves 90.8% word-boundary precision on readable Sumerian seal legends while finding only 46.8% of word boundaries. In other words, physical line divisions expose the primary structural joint of a legend rather than its complete word segmentation. In Indus seals, that joint repeatedly separates a variable, rarely repeated opening (only 17.1% of held openings recur anywhere in the build corpus) from a closing formula drawn from a compact stock of 49 sequences that closes 76.0% of held legends, and on divided seals scribes frequently ended the main line right after that closing formula.

The numeral and comparative seal studies place guardrails on how far purely distributional statistics can be pushed toward semantic or typological claims. Indus stroke groups are demonstrably more tightly bound to adjacent signs than Ur III administrative counts are to commodities, with multi-site near-fixed expressions in both directions (`3 tall + 520`, `7 + 585`, and openers `861`, `817` and `820` before two short strokes), pointing away from open-ended accounting tallies on seals without distinguishing fixed lexical phrases from standardized allotments or ranks. Similarly, our five-corpus head-direction comparison shows that strong final-sign concentration, long noted on Indus seals, arises whenever a seal tradition closes with a regular formula or suffix, whether in head-final Indo-Aryan and Tamil-Brahmi or in head-initial Old Akkadian. Testing whether Indus legends are head-final or head-initial will depend on enlarging the Akkadian and Tamil-Brahmi seal corpora and testing title-before-name ordering once an independent structural map of openings and closings is established.

The circulation results reinforce a composite picture of seal use. Long legends (six signs or more) never repeat across 445 seals, consistent with individual holders or specific multi-part identifications. Short recurring legends, however, cross city boundaries freely at Mohenjo-daro (frozen locality test p = 0.31), show modest local clustering at smaller settlements (Banawali, Dholavira, Lothal), rarely preserve non-unicorn animal motifs when texts repeat, and appear on clay sealings whose matching seals were excavated at other cities. The plate audit of L-161 to L-170 against M-895 shows that identical four-sign legends could be carved on at least two distinct seals of matching size (one stamping clay closures found at Lothal, the other excavated at Mohenjo-daro), while the Rao Plate CLXI F audit reminds us that archaeological context and container reconstructions must be verified against primary registers before anchoring administrative narratives to individual objects.

**Boldest supported reading and the tests that decide it.** An Indus seal legend combines a variable, holder-specific opening with a closing formula drawn from a shared stock of about fifty office, rank or relational formulae, occasionally followed by a short one- to three-sign terminal addition placed on a second line when the top band fills. Short recurring legends, especially four-sign sequences such as the M-895 and L-161 text, named shared institutional roles or standard designations carved on multiple distinct seals across cities rather than single personal seals carried from one town to another. Two concrete empirical tests decide this reading. First, scoring our hashed prospective predictions P1 to P3 on unread CISI 3.1 seals (~50 single-line legends) and post-catalogue finds will test whether the 49-formula closing stock generalizes out of corpus; a stock closing rate at or below 32% at n = 50 (the 97.5th quantile of shuffled legends) kills the structural model. Second, specialist confirmation of M-895's field motif and calibrated underside views of the Lothal L-161 to L-172 sealings (paired with resolution of Rao's Plate CLXI F specimen link) will test whether the same four-sign legend crossed both a change in seal die and a change in animal emblem across a verifiable urban packaging context.

## 6. Limitations

Six limitations bound the claims in this manuscript:

1. **Upstream corpus provenance and gaps.** All Indus counts rest on the pinned Lipi export (`yajnadevam/lipi`, commit `fc8813a5`), whose redistribution rights remain unresolved and which omits parts of CISI 1, CISI 3 and post-catalogue excavations. All derived sign sequences and inventories stay local; only aggregates, hashes and code are tracked.
2. **Small primary segmentation sample.** Only 33 clean multi-line faces had verified plate orders (25 of them from Mohenjo-daro), producing a wide confidence interval on J (0.586 to 0.838) and zero recall on the 3 faces whose second group sits separately in the picture field. Enlarging the labelled set from CISI 3 photographs and multi-line tablets is needed to test geographic generality.
3. **AI visual readings and coder dependence.** The sign-family codings (step 1), the 75-face plate audit, the 29-face follow-up adjudication and the Lothal/M-895 measurements were all performed by AI models inspecting digital glyph sheets or halftone scans (~100 dpi for CISI 1). Because both step 1 coders were instances of the same model reading code-ordered sheets with frequency counts, their high agreement (ARI 0.884 / 0.975) measures procedural consistency rather than independent human judgement.
4. **Post-outcome inventory amendment and exploratory splits.** Version 2 of the closing stock (Amendment 1) was defined after seeing version 1's low cut rate on the build corpus, although before the held set H was opened. The length-stratified locality sensitivity (p = 0.032), the Mohenjo-daro versus small-site breakdown, the preceding-item numeral sensitivities and the title–name order counts were computed after frozen primary endpoints and must be treated as exploratory until confirmed on fresh corpora.
5. **Untestable head-direction thresholds.** Because provenienced, complete Old Akkadian (11) and Tamil-Brahmi (23) seal legends fell below the pre-registered 25-legend threshold, no head-direction statistic passed the frozen discrimination rule.
6. **Genre differences in numeral comparison.** Comparing Indus seal and tablet texts against Ur III administrative accounts and Linear B commodity records spans different functional genres; high stroke-value fixity separates Indus usage from open-ended accounting tallies without identifying the underlying semantics.

## 7. Pre-submission epigraphic and archaeological review checklist

None of the source packages integrated here has yet passed human specialist verification. Before submitting this manuscript for peer review, an independent epigraphist or South Asian archaeologist should complete five bounded checks:

1. **Primary 33 plate-ordered multi-line faces and 12 provisional face-adjudication maps.** Inspect the CISI 1 and 2 photographs (and higher-resolution plates where held) for the 33 primary segmentation faces (paying special attention to the three forward-order exceptions H-60, H-97 and K-16, the 3 separate-field faces, and the doubtful within-line sign orders noted on L-5 and H-98), and review the 12 provisional segment-to-group maps from `2026-10-22-face-adjudication` to confirm whether they warrant promotion to stable corpus annotations.
2. **M-895 animal field and L-161 to L-170 legend and motif.** Confirm on the CISI 2 plate (p. 85) or original object whether M-895 preserves a unicorn horn and neck folds or an unidentifiable horned animal, verify the four-sign reading and elephant trunk-and-tusk identification across L-161 to L-170 on CISI 1 (pp. 276–278), and check L-166's compressed left margin on the original sealing.
3. **Rao Plate CLXI F specimen binding and Lothal reverse geometry.** Consult excavation registers, specimen cards or calibrated underside and edge photographs for L-161 (1830 = LTH SRG 1195) and L-164 (1864 = LTH SRG 1232) to resolve whether Plate CLXI F depicts 1864 or 1830, establish whether the warehouse layer 2 context applies to L-164, and test Rao's flat-stoppered jar closure against Frenez and Tosi's tentative wooden-box interpretation.
4. **Human sign-family recoding.** Have an independent human coder classify the Lipi and Wells glyph inventory from shuffled sheets with code numbers and token counts stripped, checking low-agreement families (hatched creatures, bent bands, outline chevrons) and the twelve-stroke grid directly on CISI photographs.
5. **Prospective evaluation on unread seals.** Acquire CISI 3.1 and curated post-catalogue seal transcriptions and run the unmodified, hash-checked `score_prospective.py` script once to score predictions P1 through P4 before publishing final confirmation outcomes.
