# Background brief: the Indus (Harappan) civilization

Status: background compilation for the slide deck, 8 October 2026. No new analysis was run. The brief is written for readers meeting the subject for the first time and gives a short citation after each fact.

**How the citations were checked.** Sources opened in this pass are cited with a page or URL: local copies under `research/literature/sources/` and `data/raw/`, the project's own reports, and web pages reached on 8 October 2026. A dagger (†) marks a citation to a standard synthesis or primary report that was not reopened in this pass; the fact is widely repeated in the literature, but its page should be checked in print before it is quoted on a slide. ## 1. Where

The civilization grew along the Indus River and its five Punjab tributaries (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej), and along a second, now mostly dry river system to the east called the Ghaggar in India and the Hakra in Pakistan (Possehl 2002, *The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective*, ch. 1†; Wright 2010, *The Ancient Indus*, ch. 1†). Its sites lie mainly in modern Pakistan and northwest India, with an outlying trading settlement at Shortughai on the Amu Darya in northeast Afghanistan (Francfort 1989, *Fouilles de Shortughaï*†). At its widest the distribution runs from the Makran coast of Baluchistan to western Uttar Pradesh, and from northeast Afghanistan to Gujarat and, in Late Harappan times, Daimabad in Maharashtra (Wikipedia, "Indus Valley Civilisation", read 8 Oct 2026, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation).

Published estimates of the area differ because authors draw the boundary differently. V. N. Prabhakar's teaching module gives nearly 700,000 km² (e-PG Pathshala, "Protohistory of India and Pakistan IV", https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp02/?p=111); Khan and Lemmen give about 1 million km² (Khan and Lemmen 2013, "Bricks and urbanism in the Indus Civilization", arXiv:1303.1426, p. 2); encyclopedic summaries give 1.25–1.26 million km² (Wikipedia for Schools copy of "Indus Valley Civilization", via web search, 8 Oct 2026). A slide should give the range and avoid a single figure.

Site counts also vary with the date of the survey and the definition of a site. Possehl's 2002 figures, as reproduced in a University of Lucknow teaching note, are at least 1,022 Mature Harappan sites, 406 in Pakistan and 616 in India, of which about 96 had been excavated, 41 in Pakistan and 55 in India (Possehl 2002 and Settar and Korisettar 2002, as quoted in Anil Kumar, "Harappan Civilization", https://www.lkouniv.ac.in/site/writereaddata/siteContent/202003241550006972anil_kumar_HARAPPAN_CIVILIZATION%201.pdf). Wright gives "over a thousand" Mature Harappan sites with nearly a hundred excavated (Wright 2010, as quoted in Wikipedia, "Indus Valley Civilisation"). Prabhakar gives over 1,500 settlements of all sizes (e-PG Pathshala module, above). Possehl's gazetteer of all periods lists 3,173 sites (Khan and Lemmen 2013, p. 3, citing Possehl). Using the Indus Google Earth gazetteer, Khan and Lemmen count about 300 more sites in the Early than in the pre-Harappan phase, another 300 more in the Mature phase, and a gradual fall to about 800 sites in the Late phase (Khan and Lemmen 2013, p. 5). Possehl's separate per-phase tables in *Indus Age: The Beginnings* (1999) and the 2002 synthesis could not be opened in this pass and should be checked in print before quoting phase totals.

The inscribed objects that the project studies come from far fewer places: the project's site census resolves 115 site entities, including 36 find records outside South Asia (`presentations/2026-10-08-site-map-and-overview/README.md`; `data/sites-census-notes.md`).

Five sites are usually named as the largest cities. Their sizes and populations are estimates from surface extent and excavated house density, and they differ by author:

## 2. When

Two dating frameworks are in common use. Shaffer and Kenoyer group the sequence into eras: a Regionalization Era (in Shaffer's scheme c. 4000–2500/2300 BCE, ending at 2600 BCE in Kenoyer's usage, and containing the Early Harappan phase), an Integration Era (2600–1900 BCE, the Mature Harappan cities) and a Localization Era (1900–1300 BCE, the Late Harappan) (Kenoyer 1998, *Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization*†; Wikipedia, "Periodisation of the Indus Valley Civilisation", read 8 Oct 2026). Possehl uses Early Harappan, Mature Harappan and Posturban, and dates the Mature phase c. 2500–1900 BCE (Possehl 2006, "Shu-ilishu's Cylinder Seal", *Expedition* 48(1), p. 42, local copy `possehl_2006_shu_ilishu.txt`). The project's map applies the convention Early c. 3200–2600, Mature 2600–1900 and Late 1900–1300 BCE where a site has no dated phases of its own (`presentations/2026-10-08-site-map-and-overview/README.md`).

Harappa has the best-dated single sequence (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, "Inscribed Objects from Harappa Excavations 1986–2007", Table 1, author PDF p. 2, local copy `data/raw/2026-10-06-backward-chronology/kenoyer-meadow2010.pdf`):

| 2, Kot Diji (Early Harappa) phase | c. 2800–2600 BCE | town; first seals, weights and multi-sign marks |

The Mature Harappan cities were therefore contemporary with the Old Kingdom pyramids, the Akkadian empire and Ur III, and their decline was under way by the time of Hammurabi. About 1,500 years separate the last Indus inscriptions from Ashoka's Brahmi.

## 3. Discovery

The first European description of Harappa comes from Charles Masson, who visited the mound in 1829 on his way to Ranjit Singh's court at Lahore and published the account in 1842 in *Narrative of Various Journeys in Balochistan, Afghanistan and the Panjab* (harappa.com, "First Explorers: Charles Masson, 1829", via web search 8 Oct 2026). Some accounts give 1826, the year that appears in another harappa.com page title ("charles masson 1826", https://www.harappa.com/content/charles-masson-1826, not opened).

In the 1850s, contractors building the Lahore–Multan railway, under the engineers John and William Brunton, took bricks from the Harappa mounds for track ballast; the bricks supported roughly 160 km of line (Wikipedia, "Indus Valley Civilisation"; Possehl 2002†). Alexander Cunningham, later the first Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India (founded 1861), visited Harappa in 1853 and 1856, dug there briefly in 1872–73 and published the first Indus seal in his survey report for 1872–73, issued in 1875 (Cunningham 1875, *Archaeological Survey of India Report* 5, pl. XXXIII†; Wikipedia, as above). He took it to be a foreign object.

Systematic excavation began in the 1920–21 season, when Daya Ram Sahni dug at Harappa (Vats 1940†; Marshall 1931†; Parpola's harappa.com essay "Deciphering the Indus Script", sec. 1, local `parpola_1986_deciphering.txt`, dates the start of excavation at Harappa to 1920). R. D. Banerji had recognized the antiquity of Mohenjo-daro in 1919–20 and excavated there in 1922–23 (Wikipedia, as above). John Marshall, Director General of the Survey, announced the discovery of a previously unknown civilization in the *Illustrated London News* of 20 September 1924 (Royal Asiatic Society blog and harappa.com, via search; Gadd 1932, p. 191, local `gadd1932_ocr.txt`, dates the announcement to the autumn of 1924). Marshall's three-volume Mohenjo-daro report appeared in 1931 and M. S. Vats's Harappa report in 1940 (Kenoyer 2020, "The Origin and Development of the Indus Script", p. 217, local `kenoyer2020_origin.txt`).

Mortimer Wheeler excavated the defences and Cemetery R37 at Harappa in 1946 and worked at Mohenjo-daro in 1950 (Wheeler 1947, "Harappa 1946", *Ancient India* 3†; Wheeler 1953, *The Indus Civilization*†).

After the 1947 partition most of the known large sites lay in Pakistan, and survey in India intensified. In India, S. R. Rao found Lothal in Gujarat in 1954 and excavated it from 1955 to 1960 (Rao 1979–85, *Lothal*, ASI Memoir 78†); B. B. Lal and B. K. Thapar excavated Kalibangan in Rajasthan from 1960–61 to 1969 (Lal et al. 2003, *Excavations at Kalibangan*†); J. P. Joshi located Dholavira in Kutch in 1967–68† and R. S. Bisht excavated it from 1990 (Wikipedia, "Dholavira", via search); Rakhigarhi in Haryana was excavated in 1997–2000 by Amarendra Nath† and from 2011 by Vasant Shinde (Wikipedia, "Rakhigarhi", via search). In Pakistan, F. A. Khan excavated Kot Diji in 1955–57†, M. R. Mughal's Cholistan survey of the 1970s recorded Ganweriwala and hundreds of Hakra-valley sites (Mughal 1997, *Ancient Cholistan*†), the French mission under Jean-François Jarrige began work at Mehrgarh in 1974†, and the Harappa Archaeological Research Project has excavated at Harappa since 1986 (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 1). Mohenjo-daro was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1980 and Dholavira on 27 July 2021 (UNESCO WHC list nos. 138 and 1645; Dholavira date via search).

## 4. Cities and daily life

Mature Harappan cities were laid out on raised platforms with streets running roughly north–south and east–west, and many were divided into a walled high mound (often called the "citadel") and a lower residential town (Kenoyer 1998†; Possehl 2002†). Mohenjo-daro has a western high mound and an eastern lower town. Harappa has four major mounds (F, AB, E and ET) and a fifth under the modern town (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 2). Dholavira's fortified area is divided into a citadel, a middle town and a lower town (Wikipedia "Dholavira" and UNESCO-derived summaries, via search).

Bricks, whether sun-dried or baked, were moulded in the proportions 1:2:4 for thickness, width and length; a common house brick measures about 7 × 14 × 28 cm and a larger wall brick about 10 × 20 × 40 cm (Kenoyer 1998†; Kenoyer 2010, "Measuring the Harappan world"†). Houses were built around courtyards, many had bathing floors and latrines, and these drained into covered brick drains running along the streets (Kenoyer 1998†). Michael Jansen estimated from the wells found in the excavated areas that Mohenjo-daro had more than 700 wells, and their positions appear to have been fixed when the city platforms were first built (harappa.com, "Mohenjo-daro city wells", via search; Jansen 1993†).

The Great Bath on Mohenjo-daro's high mound is a sunken tank about 12 m by 7 m and 2.4 m deep, reached by steps at both ends; its floor and walls are made of fitted baked brick set in gypsum mortar, with a layer of bitumen behind the brick to keep the water in (harappa.com "Great Bath, Mohenjo-daro", via search; Kenoyer 1998†). Its purpose is inferred, most often as ritual bathing, and no text identifies it.

Dholavira, in a dry region of Kutch, depended on stored water. Its builders cut a series of large stone-lined reservoirs within and around the fortified area and diverted water from seasonal streams into them (Wikipedia "Dholavira" and UNESCO-derived summaries, via search; Bisht 2015†). At Lothal, Rao excavated a brick-lined basin about 217–219 m long and 36–37 m wide and identified it as a tidal dockyard connected to an estuary (harappa.com Lothal pages and Rao 1979†). That interpretation is disputed: Leshnik (1968, "The Harappan 'port' at Lothal: another view", *American Anthropologist* 70†) and later writers, including Possehl, read it as a reservoir or irrigation tank, while recent sediment studies by Gupta, Prabhakar and Jain have been presented as support for the dockyard reading (news summaries via search, September 2024).

Craft production was specialized and widely standardized (Kenoyer 1998, ch. 7†). Craftsmen drilled long carnelian beads with specially made stone drills (Kenoyer and Vidale 1992†), cut bangles from the conch shell *Turbinella pyrum*†, made glazed faience ornaments, and carved seals from steatite, which they then fired to harden the surface (Kenoyer 1998†). Copper and bronze were cast and hammered into tools, vessels and ornaments (Kenoyer 1998†). The inscribed objects themselves include terracotta, stoneware, faience, shell, bone, ivory, sandstone, steatite, gypsum, copper, silver and gold (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 5).

Farmers grew wheat and barley in the winter season and millets, rice and pulses in the summer, and many settlements used both seasons (Petrie and Bates 2017, "'Multi-cropping', intercropping and adaptation to variable environments in Indus South Asia", *Journal of World Prehistory* 30†). Cotton was cultivated; the earliest cotton fibres in the region come from Neolithic Mehrgarh (Moulherat et al. 2002, *Journal of Archaeological Science* 29†). The main livestock was humped zebu cattle, with water buffalo, sheep and goats (Meadow 1996†; Kenoyer 1998†). Terracotta figurines of people and animals, model carts with solid wheels, whistles and gaming pieces are common finds in houses and streets (Kenoyer 1998†).

## 5. Weights, measures and trade

Harappan cubical stone weights, most often of banded chert, follow a shared series in which the smaller weights double (ratios 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) and the larger ones continue in decimal steps; the weight at ratio 16 is about 13.7 g (Kenoyer 2010, "Measuring the Harappan world", pp. 116–117, as checked in `experiments/2026-10-06-weight-metrology/REPORT.md`; Hemmy 1937–38†). Hemmy's 1931 and 1938 calculations correspond to about 13.70 g at ratio 16, and his 1943 modal analysis gives 13.63 g (weight-metrology REPORT, citing Hemmy 1938, pp. 601–602, 674, and Hemmy and Hall 1943, pp. 236–237). The project's own fit of 90 Dholavira weights of 5–100 g gives a unit of 13.716 g with a 1.6% coefficient of variation; the 147 smaller weights of 1.3–5 g fit 14.280 g instead (weight-metrology REPORT, opening paragraph and mass-band table). The same weights appear in Harappa's Kot Diji phase, before the cities (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 5).

Three marked rulers are often cited for length: a shell fragment from Mohenjo-daro with divisions of about 6.7 mm, an ivory scale from Lothal with divisions of about 1.7 mm, and a bronze rod from Harappa with divisions of about 9.34 mm (Kenoyer 2010†; Mackay 1938†; Rao 1979†; Vats 1940†). Whether they share a single unit is debated.

Mesopotamian cuneiform texts name a distant land called Meluhha, which most scholars identify with the Indus region (Possehl 2006, p. 42, local). Sargon of Akkad boasted that ships from Meluhha, Magan and Dilmun moored at the quay of Akkad; in Kramer's translation the line begins "The ships from Meluhha / the ships from Magan" (Possehl 2006, p. 42). Magan and Dilmun are identified with modern Oman and Bahrain (Possehl 2006, p. 42). Texts also mention a Meluhhan village in Akkad whose residents were called "Son of Meluhha" (Possehl 2006, p. 42). A cylinder seal now in the Louvre (AO 22310) belonged to Shu-ilishu, whose inscription names him an interpreter of the Meluhhan language; Possehl dates it to the Late Akkadian period, c. 2020 BCE on the low chronology, and Porada placed its style in the Late Akkadian period, possibly Ur III (Possehl 2006, pp. 42–43; Penn Museum, https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/shu-ilishus-cylinder-seal/).

Indus-style seals were found at Ur and other Mesopotamian sites from the 1920s onward (Gadd 1932, "Seals of ancient Indian style found at Ur", *Proceedings of the British Academy* 18, local `gadd1932_ocr.txt`). The project's census of finds abroad lists 36 records in Iraq, Iran, the Gulf states, Central Asia and Afghanistan, including Ur, Kish, Nippur, Susa, Tepe Yahya, Failaka, Qala'at al-Bahrain, Altyn Depe and Gonur Depe (`data/abroad-sites-notes.md`; `data/sites-census-notes.md`). Etched carnelian beads of Indus manufacture occur in the Royal Cemetery at Ur (Kenoyer 1998†). In the other direction, Shortughai on the Amu Darya lies near the Badakhshan lapis lazuli sources and is generally read as an Indus trading outpost (Francfort 1989†). The project found no bilingual inscription among the Indus-related objects abroad and no Indus weight standard among Mesopotamian and Iranian weights (`slides/01-background.html`, summary slide; weight-metrology REPORT, opening paragraph).

## 6. Society and belief

Excavators have not identified a palace, a royal tomb or a large temple at any Indus city, and no surviving image or text names a ruler (Possehl 2002†; Kenoyer 1998†). The limestone "Priest-King" bust from Mohenjo-daro is often used as a symbol of the civilization, but its name is a modern label (Possehl 2006, p. 43, mentions its loan to the Metropolitan Museum's 2004 exhibition under that name).

How the cities were governed is disputed. Possehl argued that the Indus civilization achieved urban complexity without a state in the usual sense (Possehl 1998, "Sociocultural complexity without the state", in *Archaic States*†). Kenoyer proposed a set of competing city-states, each ruled by elites such as merchants, landowners and ritual specialists (Kenoyer 1997, "Early city-states in South Asia"†). Jacobson (1986)† and Ratnagar (1991, *Enquiries into the Political Organization of Harappan Society*)† argued for state-level or centralized organization. The available evidence (uniform weights, bricks and seal styles over a large area, and the absence of royal display) is compatible with several of these models.

The most common motif on square seals is a one-horned bovine, called the "unicorn" in the literature. In the project's corpus export, 1,246 of 1,609 square seals with an identified motif show it (77.4%) (`experiments/2026-10-06-sign-context-and-seals/REPORT.md`, line 3). Square seals with script and the unicorn or other animals appear late in Harappa Period 3A and are common through Periods 3B and 3C (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 6).

A Mohenjo-daro seal (M-304 in the Helsinki *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions*) shows a horned figure seated with legs bent, surrounded by animals. Marshall called it a "proto-Śiva", relating it to the later Hindu god Shiva as lord of animals (Marshall 1931, vol. 1†). The identification is disputed: Srinivasan (1975–76, "The so-called Proto-Śiva seal from Mohenjo-Daro", *Archives of Asian Art* 29†) reads the figure as buffalo-horned and links it to buffalo symbolism, and Hiltebeitel (1978, *Anthropos* 73†) relates it to the buffalo and the goddess cult; Parpola accepts a link to later Shiva and Rudra through a different route (Parpola 1994†). The difficulty is that the comparison rests on resemblance to images made some two thousand years later, with no text to identify the figure.

At Kalibangan, Lal and Thapar's excavations exposed rows of clay-lined pits containing ash and charcoal, some with a central clay stele, on the high mound and in houses; the excavators interpreted them as fire altars for ritual (Lal 1997, *The Earliest Civilization of South Asia*†). Others treat them as hearths or cooking pits, so the ritual reading is disputed (Possehl 2002†).

Most Mature Harappan burials are extended inhumations in cemeteries outside the settlement, with pottery and personal ornaments, as at Harappa's Cemetery R37 (Wheeler 1947†; Kenoyer 1998†). Late Harappan Cemetery H at Harappa contains pot burials and painted urns of a different style (Vats 1940, *Excavations at Harappa*†). The number of known burials is small compared with estimated city populations (Possehl 2002†).

## 7. The script

Indus signs survive on small, durable objects: stamp seals, the clay sealings they produced, moulded and incised tablets, copper tablets, pottery, bangles, rods and tools (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 5; `slides/01-background.html`). No long text on perishable material has been found.

Counts of inscribed objects depend on the corpus and the unit counted. Mahadevan's 1977 concordance selected 2,906 inscribed objects from 3,455 photographed (`experiments/2026-10-06-research-program/REPORT.md`, line 26) and recorded 13,372 sign occurrences (Ganesan, Subramanian, Suresh Babu and Mahadevan, "The Indus Script: Text and Context", sec. 3.1, local `mahadevan_2010_context.txt`). Andreas Fuls's 2023 report on the Interactive Corpus of Indus Texts (ICIT) gives 4,665 objects, 5,650 texts, 17,966 legible sign occurrences and 16,015 photographs and drawings (CAA 2023 abstract book, p. 281, https://2023.caaconference.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2023/04/abstract_book_v13.pdf#page=281, as recorded in `experiments/2026-10-06-evidence-census/sites-and-publications/REPORT.md`). The project's working corpus holds 5,680 inscribed faces (`slides/01-background.html`).

Estimates of the number of distinct signs range from about 400 to about 700, because sign lists differ in whether variants and compounds count as separate signs. Mahadevan listed 417 signs (Mahadevan 1977; confirmed in `mahadevan_2010_context.txt`, sec. 3.1); Parpola about 386 (Parpola 1994†); Wells 676 (Wells 2015, *The Archaeology and Epigraphy of Indus Writing*†, figure as given in the 2016 *Nature* correction to Robinson 2015, via search; other summaries give 694); and Fuls's 2023 catalogue more than 700, of which fewer than 100 form a high-frequency core (harappa.com description of Fuls's *Catalog of Indus Signs*, via search). The project's corpus uses 714 sign codes, 219 of them occurring once (`slides/01-background.html`).

The texts are short. The average length of the objects in Mahadevan's concordance is 4.6 signs, and the longest text on a single surface has 17 signs (Farmer, Sproat and Witzel 2004, *EJVS* 11(2), p. 22, local `farmer_sproat_witzel_2004_author.txt`); Parpola gives an average of no more than five (Parpola, "Deciphering the Indus Script", harappa.com essay, local `parpola_1986_deciphering.txt`). In the project's corpus the average is 3.5 sign positions per face, half the faces carry three or fewer, and the longest single face is M-314 with 17 positions (`slides/01-background.html`).

The usual direction of reading is right to left on impressions, so seals were carved in mirror image. The evidence is internal: short texts begin at the right edge and leave space at the left, and long texts push their leftmost signs onto a second line (Mahadevan 1997, review of Parpola 1994, local `mahadevan_1997_review_full.txt`; Parpola's harappa.com essay, local). Farmer, Sproat and Witzel accept a usual carving direction but question whether the signs were "read" at all (Farmer et al. 2004, p. 21 n. 5, local).

Marks scratched on pottery appear at Harappa in the Ravi phase (Period 1, from c. 3700 BCE), and multi-sign sequences that Kenoyer and Meadow regard as an early script appear in the Kot Diji phase, c. 2800–2600 BCE (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 5); Frenez dates the introduction of the script to 2800–2600 BCE (Frenez 2018, p. 170, as cited in `slides/01-background.html`). The standardized script was used through the Mature phase, c. 2600–1900 BCE (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 5). After about 1900 BCE inscribed seals become rare. Kenoyer and Meadow state that the post-firing graffiti on Late Harappan pottery at Harappa do not continue the script system, against Rao's argument for continuity at Lothal (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 8; Rao 1991†); B. B. Lal argued that Indus signs continued on Late Harappan and later Megalithic pottery (Lal 1975, as summarized in Kenoyer 2020, p. 217, local).

## 8. History of decipherment attempts

None of the proposals below has been accepted by specialists. By 1985 Mahadevan counted over forty published claims of decipherment, none generally accepted (Mahadevan 1985, "The Indus Script: Some Claims of Decipherment and Objective Methods to Test their Validity", p. 1, local `mahadevan_1985_claims.txt`). The obstacles have been stated many times: there is no bilingual text, the inscriptions are very short, the language family of the Indus population is unknown, and no proposed reading has been checked against an independent fact such as a counted quantity or a named person (Parpola 1994†; Robinson 2015, *Nature* 526†; `slides/01-background.html`, summary slide).

| Year | Proposal |
|---|---|
| 1931 | In the first Mohenjo-daro report, C. J. Gadd found no connection between the Indus script and Sumerian or Egyptian writing (Kenoyer 2020, p. 217, local, citing Gadd 1931, p. 411). |
| 1932, 1934 | G. R. Hunter published a structural study and compared Indus signs with Egyptian, Sumerian and Brahmi (Hunter 1932, *JRAS*†; Hunter 1934, *The Script of Harappa and Mohenjodaro*†). |
| 1939–41 | Bedřich Hrozný, decipherer of Hittite, proposed Hittite-related readings (Hrozný 1939†). |
| 1953 onward | Henry Heras proposed proto-Dravidian readings (Heras 1953, *Studies in Proto-Indo-Mediterranean Culture*†). |
| 1965 | Yuri Knorozov's Soviet team published a computer-assisted positional analysis and argued for a Dravidian language (Knorozov et al. 1965, *Predvaritel'noe soobshchenie*†). |

Proposals continue to appear in print and online. The ones listed here are the attempts most often cited in the scholarly literature; their authors disagree on the language (Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, Munda or unknown) and on whether the signs record language at all.

## 9. Decline and legacy

Between about 1900 and 1700 BCE the large cities shrank or were abandoned, seals, weights and the script went out of use, and settlement shifted toward smaller villages, with site numbers holding up or rising in the east (Haryana, the upper Ganga–Yamuna area) and in Gujarat while the Hakra valley in Cholistan emptied (Possehl 2002†; Khan and Lemmen 2013, pp. 5–6; Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, author PDF p. 8). Possehl described the change as a transformation rather than a collapse (Possehl 1997, "The transformation of the Indus civilization", *Journal of World Prehistory* 11†).

The proposed causes fall into three groups, and most current authors combine them.

**Rivers.** The Ghaggar-Hakra was once identified with the Vedic Sarasvati and its drying was blamed for the abandonment of Cholistan. Giosan et al. argued from sediment and dating work that during Harappan times the Ghaggar-Hakra was fed by monsoon rain, not by Himalayan glaciers, and that declining monsoon rainfall reduced its floods (Giosan et al. 2012, "Fluvial landscapes of the Harappan civilization", *PNAS* 109(26)†). Clift et al. dated the loss of glacier-fed water to well before the Harappan period (Clift et al. 2012, *Geology* 40†), and Singh et al. found that the Sutlej had abandoned the channel by about 8,000 years ago, so that Harappan towns stood along a former river course (Singh et al. 2017, *Nature Communications* 8: 1617†).

**Climate.** Lake and cave records show a weakening of the summer monsoon around 4,200–4,100 years ago, the "4.2 ka event". At Kotla Dahar in Haryana, Dixit, Hodell and Petrie dated an abrupt drop in lake level to about 4,100 years ago (Dixit et al. 2014, *Geology* 42†); Staubwasser et al. reported a drought signal in the Indus delta at the same time (Staubwasser et al. 2003, *Geophysical Research Letters* 30†). Sarkar et al. measured oxygen isotopes in animal bones and teeth at Bhirrana and argued that monsoon weakening was gradual and that farmers adapted by shifting to drought-tolerant crops (Sarkar et al. 2016, *Scientific Reports* 6†); their early dates for Bhirrana are far older than the regional convention (`presentations/2026-10-08-site-map-and-overview/README.md`). Giosan et al. later proposed that winter rainfall partly offset the summer decline in the northeast and drew people there (Giosan et al. 2018, *Climate of the Past* 14†). Petrie et al. argue that Indus farmers already managed highly variable rainfall and that the climatic effect differed by region (Petrie et al. 2017, *Current Anthropology* 58†).

**Trade and organization.** References to Meluhha in Mesopotamian texts fall away after the Old Babylonian period, and Gulf trade shifted toward Dilmun as intermediary (Wright 2010†; Possehl 2002†). Reduced long-distance trade is proposed as one reason the urban institutions that used seals and weights lost their purpose.

**The invasion hypothesis.** Wheeler proposed that Indo-Aryan invaders destroyed the cities, citing unburied skeletons in the upper levels of Mohenjo-daro and writing that "Indra stands accused" (Wheeler 1947, *Ancient India* 3†). George Dales showed that the skeletons came from different levels and periods and lay in the city's latest occupation, with no layer of destruction or battle (Dales 1964, "The Mythical Massacre at Mohenjo-daro", *Expedition* 6(3)†). The invasion explanation is now abandoned, although the date and manner of Indo-Aryan language arrival in South Asia is a separate, continuing debate (Wright 2010†).

**Proposed continuities.** Several features of later South Asian culture have been proposed as Indus inheritances. Kenoyer lists shell bangles, cart designs, house plans, craft techniques and weight standards (Kenoyer 1998, final chapter†). Parpola traces elements of Hindu religion, including aspects of Shiva and goddess worship, to the Indus period (Parpola 2015, *The Roots of Hinduism*†), and Mahadevan argued for Dravidian continuity, including links to early Tamil (Mahadevan's later papers†). These are proposals based on resemblance across one to three thousand years; they suggest where to look for evidence but are not themselves archaeological findings.

## 10. Key facts for a first-time reader

1. The Indus or Harappan civilization flourished as an urban society c. 2600–1900 BCE in what is now Pakistan and northwest India (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, Table 1).
2. Estimates of its extent range from about 0.7 to 1.25 million km²; Prabhakar describes it as more than twice the size of Egypt and Mesopotamia (Prabhakar, e-PG Pathshala; Khan and Lemmen 2013, p. 2).
3. More than 1,000 Mature Harappan sites are known, about 100 of them excavated (Possehl 2002, via Kumar; Wright 2010).
4. Mohenjo-daro and Harappa each covered 100–250 ha and may have housed 30,000–60,000 people (Kenoyer and Meadow 2010, p. 4; McIntosh 2008).
5. Cities had planned streets, covered drains, standardized 1:2:4 bricks and, at Mohenjo-daro, an estimated 700 wells (Kenoyer 1998†; Jansen via harappa.com).
6. Traders used a shared weight standard of about 13.7 g at ratio 16 and reached Mesopotamia, where the region was called Meluhha (Kenoyer 2010, p. 116; Possehl 2006, p. 42).
7. No palace, royal tomb or large temple has been identified (Possehl 2002†).
8. Catalogues list from 2,906 (1977) to 4,665 (2023) inscribed objects; the average text has 4–5 signs and the longest single-surface text 17 (Mahadevan 1977; Fuls 2023; Farmer et al. 2004, p. 22).
9. The script is undeciphered: no bilingual exists and the language is unknown (Parpola 1994†).
10. The cities were largely abandoned after 1900 BCE; river change, monsoon weakening and trade decline are the main proposed causes, and the older invasion theory has been abandoned (Giosan et al. 2012†; Dixit et al. 2014†; Dales 1964†).
