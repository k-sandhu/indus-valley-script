Claim

The animal on an Indus seal marks the holder's affiliation, and the unicorn affiliation was a single body present in every city. Rarer animals mark smaller affiliations, each based in fewer places. The affiliation is shown by the animal and is not written in the legend, so holders of the same title in different affiliations carry the same text with different animals.

Why it might be true

Unicorns are 77% of square seals with a known motif, and they are the majority at each of six adequately sampled sites (sign context and seals). Seals with identical texts share their animal mainly when it is the unicorn: of 89 identical-text pairs that share an animal, 88 are unicorn pairs, and of 42 pairs involving another animal, 1 shares it (seal-text identity). Two results constrain the claim. Unicorn seals are no more uniform in size than others (the coefficient of variation differs by 0.013, p = 0.65), and a seal's text does not predict whether its animal is a unicorn (gain −0.063 nats per object, p = 0.81). The first rules out a single workshop template; the second fits an affiliation that is not written in the legend.

Kill condition

P1 fails in both parts: rarer animals are no more site-concentrated than chance and unicorns depart from site totals. That pattern would make the animals regional styles rather than affiliations.
