Claim

The interlocking Dravidian readings that Asko Parpola presents for the Indus script record the density of the Old Tamil compound vocabulary and the choice of the sign sequences he examined, not the language of the inscriptions. Applied to sign sequences chosen before any Tamil compound is looked up, his procedure finds attested interlocks for his word assignments no more often than for random Tamil words of the same compounding productivity and frequency.

Why it might be true

Each of Parpola's rebus interlocks that holds in the Tamil Lexicon is something the Lexicon supplies for random words. The compounds W1-X and X-W2 share a member for 30.5% of random replacements of muruku and mīṉ; 43% of random picturable concepts have a word that is the first member of some X-mīṉ compound; 8–11% complete the spindle link with kaṭavuḷ (interlocks.json, fields /null/I1_T0s/K50, /null/pictures_T0s). Parpola's own muruku and mīṉ even meet in a second, irrelevant member, puḷi "sour". Single homophones are also cheaper in Dravidian than in Indo-Aryan or Sumerian: 2.31% of picture–name pairs against 1.50% and 0.45% (homophones.json, field /prediction_single_homophone).

The four interlocks that hold for his words occur together in only 0.44% of random replacements (/null/network_T0s). That rarity is a property of the conjunction, and a conjunction of cheap links is rare only when few were tried. With per-link chance rates near 0.21, four successes become more likely than not once about 17 sign sequences have been examined (exploratory.json, field /E3_template_multiplicity). Parpola has worked on the script since 1964 and the 2010 paper presents his best cases; one of its five interlocks, Muruka-p-piḷḷai, comes from modern Jaffna names rather than from a dictionary. The numeral compounds aṟu-mīṉ and eḻu-mīṉ are not chance, but they show only that mīṉ means "star"; the stroke count that most often precedes the fish on seals, two, has no star compound in the Lexicon.
