# Calibrating a proposed sign key

12 October 2026. Derived from [Study A of the published-claim tests](../../experiments/2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/sanskrit-key/REPORT.md), which applied these checks to Yajnadevam's Sanskrit key.

A phonetic or semantic key assigns a sound or a meaning to each Indus sign. It earns support only if the corpus prefers it over keys with the same freedom, meaning the same free choices. Readings that look like words are not that support. Random consonant strings drawn at Sanskrit letter frequencies divide into dictionary stems in 100% of cases once one-consonant stems are allowed. With two-consonant stems, strings of four or more consonants divide in 92% to 96% of cases from lemmas attested in the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS) and about 99% from Monier-Williams. If each inscribed face (one side of an object) is one word, words that repeat units where the face repeats signs (equality-pattern matches) fit 91–97% of Indus faces for Sumerian, Akkadian, Egyptian and Greek inventories alike ([Bronze Age comparison](../../experiments/2026-10-12-bronze-age-comparison/REPORT.md)). Using a reconstruction of the search behind the Sanskrit key, Raghavendra's [August 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02999) reached 68–97% dictionary coverage in English, Sanskrit and Tamil on a constructed emblem corpus that encodes no sounds. Criteria of this kind have almost no redundancy, so arguments from unicity distance (the text length beyond which only one key should give acceptable readings) built on them give no bound.

A likelihood criterion does separate keys. Held-out text in the claimed language, reduced to the classes the key distinguishes, sets the target. The measure is order gain, the bits per consonant an order-5 model saves over letter frequencies: Sanskrit spans of one to four words gain 0.29 to 0.42 bits per consonant, while random strings lose about 0.44. The checks below compare a key with that target and with keys that share its freedom.

1. Reproduce the key exactly from its published table and reading rules, and measure how often published readings depart from it. Departures are additional freedom.
2. Measure the redundancy of the representation the key fixes. Consonant skeletons, with vowels removed and similar consonants pooled into classes, have far lower redundancy than written text (0.167 against 0.573 for Sanskrit under the same model). Recompute any unicity distance with the measured value.
3. Calibrate the acceptance criterion on random strings at the language's letter frequencies, length by length.
4. Place the key among rearrangements of its own values, swapping values only among signs of similar frequency (within sign-frequency strata). A key fitted to the corpus should sit far outside that distribution under an independent model of the claimed language. Report the position under more than one training text and projection, and separate composition from order by subtracting the score of the same texts with signs shuffled within each text.
5. Find the optimum in the same space by simulated annealing over within-stratum swaps, and report consonant counts beside every optimum: unconstrained swaps can raise the score by turning frequent signs into vowels. If the optima far exceed the key, the corpus does not single it out. Repeat on texts with scrambled sign order to see how much of the fit needs the attested order.
6. Check transfer by fitting on half the texts and scoring the other half, and again after removing texts that lie within one edit of, or are contained in, a text of the fitting half. On synthetic corpora of this size the language identified varies with the seed, so use several.
7. Rank each sign's value locally among the alternatives with all other signs fixed, by both log-likelihood and order gain, since the first favours frequent classes and the second rare ones. Compare the key's mean rank with that of its rearrangements.
8. Compare languages only under matched freedom, with token-level letter frequencies held fixed. Unconstrained assignment lets an optimizer exploit rare letters and inflates the statistic: in the first, failed version of the language comparison (Stage 3e), Latin reached 8.5 bits per consonant.

The scripts accept another key in the same `sign,xlit,canonical,regex` format through the `IVS_KEY_CSV` environment variable. Run them from a copy of the directory so that the frozen outputs stay unchanged. Semantic meaning maps, such as the project's anchor catalogues of proposed sign meanings, need the same rearrangement and achievable-fit comparisons with a criterion suited to meanings.
