# Taking Parpola's readings further

Research direction, 20 October 2026. This note sets out Asko Parpola's model of the Indus script, records where the project's tests leave it, and proposes eleven studies that extend it with methods and evidence from fields his own work did not combine. Each proposal states its claim at full strength, the test that would decide it and the data it needs. None has been run; a claim becomes a conjecture card when a study adopts it.

We worked from Parpola's own summary of the model, [*A Dravidian solution to the Indus script problem*](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Parpola-2010-Coimbatore.pdf) (2010), held locally by the fish-context study; from Wanzke's paper on the orientation of Mohenjo-daro in *Interim Reports* vol. 2 (1987), held locally as `research/literature/sources/jansen_urban1987_interim2_govscan_77275.pdf`; and from the registry, the working model and the fish-context outputs of 20 October.

## Parpola's model

Asko Parpola, born in 1941 and professor emeritus of South Asian studies at the University of Helsinki, has worked on the script since 1964, first with Seppo Koskenniemi, Simo Parpola and Pentti Aalto, who announced a Proto-Dravidian decipherment in 1969. His most durable contributions are corpora. The Helsinki concordances of 1973 and 1979–1982 were among the first computer analyses of an undeciphered script, and the *Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions* (CISI; volumes of 1987, 1991, 2010, 2019 and 2022) photographs nearly every known inscribed object. Our object record is built on CISI numbers. The argument is set out in *Deciphering the Indus Script* (Cambridge University Press, 1994) and placed in a history of South Asian religion in *The Roots of Hinduism: The Early Aryans and the Indus Civilization* (Oxford University Press, 2015). The model has six connected parts.

**Language.** The Harappans spoke a Dravidian language. Brahui, a Dravidian language, is still spoken in Baluchistan; the Rigveda already contains Dravidian loanwords and retroflex consonants; and speakers of Indo-Aryan arrived later. In Parpola's reconstruction they came through the Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC) from about 1900 BCE, after an earlier wave of Indo-Iranian speakers whom the Rigveda calls Dāsas.

**Script type.** The script is logo-syllabic, like archaic Sumerian and Egyptian. About 400 signs is too many for a syllabary, and many texts have one to three signs, a typical word length for such scripts. Word signs (logograms) stand beside signs used for their sound by the rebus principle. The script formed between 2800 and 2500 BCE, partly from earlier pot marks, probably after the idea of writing reached the Early Harappans from Proto-Elamite Iran.

**Method.** A sign can be read when four conditions hold together: its picture is recognisable, it is used as a rebus, the intended meaning follows from context, and a historically plausible language has homophones for both the picture and the meaning. For Proto-Dravidian Parpola allows variation in the length of vowels and consonants and little else, and he requires that a word be attested in more than one subgroup of the family. Readings must interlock with one another like the entries of a crossword, and Old Tamil literature, the Dravidian source least affected by Indo-Aryan, is the main external check.

**Function.** Seal legends are mainly proper names of persons, with titles and descent, as on contemporary Mesopotamian seals. Names in Mesopotamia and later India often contain a god's name, so a large and evenly spread group of signs should hold theophoric elements. Parpola also proposed that seal size marks rank, with royal titles on the largest seals (1986).

**Readings.** The fish signs are that group. Fish is never mentioned in Mesopotamian seal legends, yet nearly one sign in ten on Indus seals belongs to the fish family, plain or with added marks. The common Dravidian word for fish, *mīn*, is homophonous with *mīn* "star", and in cuneiform the star sign marks the names of gods. The other readings build on this one.

**Astral religion.** Around these readings Parpola reconstructs a Harappan religion centred on the sky. The Vedic calendar of 27 or 28 asterisms (*nakṣatra*) begins with the Pleiades. Their heliacal rising at the vernal equinox dates the list to about the 23rd century BCE, and because the Vedic people did not bring it from the Indo-Iranian tradition, they adopted it in India. Myth preserves the system: the Pleiades are the divorced wives of the Seven Sages and the mothers of Skanda, whom later Tamil texts call *aṟu-mīn kātalaṉ*, "beloved of the Pleiades". The seven figures on the fig-deity seal M-1186 are the Seven Mothers, the Pleiades. Stoneware bangles, often inscribed with nothing but the intersecting circles, were votive gifts for a son. The trefoil robe of the "priest-king" is a garment of stars (*The Sky-Garment*, 1985). Indian names taken from the asterism of birth go back to Harappan practice (*Astral proper names in India*, 1990). Parpola answered the claim of Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) that the signs are not writing in *Is the Indus script indeed not a writing system?* (*Airavati*, 2008).

## Where our tests leave it

The working model already adopts part of Parpola's view of numerals. On seals most groups of strokes are fixed parts of names or titles: a sign that follows strokes takes 3.61 stroke values on average, against 4.53–5.15 for commodities in matched Ur III samples ([working model](../WORKING-MODEL.md), section 6, credence 0.5).

The fish-context study read its confirmation half on 20 October. Its report and review are not yet written, so these numbers are preliminary ([results](../../experiments/2026-10-20-fish-context/confirmation-v1/results.json)). Fish variants combine in a fixed order: 43 of 51 untied groups of related seals follow the order frozen on the discovery half (220, 235, 240, 226, 231, 233, 241; one-sided p = 3×10⁻⁷). The stroke group before a fish behaves as a fixed expression tied to the fish variant: a model with fish-specific counts predicted the stroke value better than a model of counted quantities in 81 of 95 untied groups (p = 6×10⁻¹³). Parpola's narrow asterism reading did not pass. Before the plain fish, 11 of 17 groups had three or six short strokes; the lower 95% bound of that share, 0.41, is far below the frozen threshold of 0.8, and seven strokes before the plain fish did not occur in the confirmation half. Fish signs were no commoner on seals than on other objects after controls for site and text length (p = 0.49). The study lowered the narrow astronomical reading to 0.05 and raised the reading of fish signs as ordered compound labels to 0.55.

Two strokes precede a fish in 77 of 102 cases on the confirmation seals. Parpola treats two long strokes as a separate word sign, *veḷ*, distinct from the numeral two; whether these pairs are long or short strokes decides whether the commonest fish compound is his Venus or a count.

Three earlier results set the standard for any reading. Dictionary fits carry little weight: random consonant strings divide into Sanskrit dictionary words in 92–100% of cases, and keys fitted to half the corpus fit the other half equally well under unrelated languages ([key calibration](key-calibration-2026-10-12.md)). Texts begin with many different signs and end with few (an entropy difference of +0.260 against a shuffle range of ±0.018), which fits a closing stock of suffixes or titles; the statistic cannot choose a language because we hold no head-final seal-legend corpus to compare (STRUCTURAL-TYPOLOGY-2026-10-12). Only 2 of 68 segment stems alternate their final sign, a rate inside the range of readable scripts at this scale ([F-0019](../findings/F-0019-indus-alternation-has-few-stems.md)).

## What a single scholar could not measure

Parpola writes that his readings interlock to an extent that "excludes chance coincidences". That is a probability judgement made by eye, over a lexicon of tens of thousands of entries and a language family whose age was not then estimated. The studies below replace such judgements with measured base rates, test the calendrical and astronomical claims against physical evidence he did not use, and add comparison corpora, dated phylogenies and settlement layouts. Studies 1 and 2 measure the method, 3 to 6 test the astral model, 7 and 8 test the language, 9 and 10 test continuity in time, and 11 combines the evidence. Risk classes follow the [conjecture rules](../conjectures/README.md).

### 1. The base rate of a rebus

**Claim.** Parpola's interlocking readings would rarely arise by chance, and Dravidian supplies them more readily than the other candidate languages.

**Test.** We encode his procedure as a program. Given a picture word and a target meaning, it searches a lexicon for homophones under his tolerance: vowel and consonant length free, attestation in two subgroups, compounds attested in Old Tamil. We run it on random pairs of a picturable noun and a meaning suitable for names (deity, star, colour, direction, kinship, quality) drawn from Concepticon lists, in the *Dravidian Etymological Dictionary* (Burrow and Emeneau, 2nd edition 1984), Turner's *Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages*, a Munda lexicon, the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary and a Burushaski dictionary, with the tolerance scaled to each phoneme inventory. We then repeat his interlock searches. For the *veḷ* reading he collected the attested names *Muruku-X* and the star compounds *X-mīn* and found a shared member X. We rerun that search with the god's name and the head noun replaced by random words of the same frequency and compounding productivity, and record how often some shared member appears.

**Prediction and kill condition.** A single homophone pair is about as easy to find in Dravidian as elsewhere, while Parpola's whole network of interlocks arises in fewer than 1% of random replacements. The reading is dead as evidence if random replacements produce interlocks of the same depth in more than 20% of draws.

**Data.** The Digital Dictionaries of South Asia publish DEDR and Turner's dictionary; their reuse rights need review, so derived tables stay local. The study uses no Indus texts and spends none of the corpus. Risk: stretch.

### 2. A synthetic Dravidian script with an answer key

**Claim.** Parpola's method, applied blind to a logo-syllabic script the size of the Indus corpus, recovers true readings more often than it produces false readings that look equally convincing.

**Test.** We build about 4,000 short legends from attested early Tamil personal names, titles and donor formulae (Mahadevan, *Early Tamil Epigraphy*, 2003) and write them with about 400 signs: logograms for picturable words and rebus signs for their homophones, at rates taken from archaic Sumerian. Sign shapes are drawn so that coders recognise the depicted object only part of the time, as with the Indus signs. A team that has not seen the key receives the sign images and the corpus and applies the four conditions and interlock checks. We score true and false readings and the team's confidence in each, and repeat with a scrambled key as a control.

**Prediction and kill condition.** Among readings the team rates as confident, at least half are true. If confident readings are no more often true under the real key than under the scrambled key, the method cannot certify a reading at this corpus size. The study extends the [alien-artifact design](cross-disciplinary-and-alien.md) to decipherment. Risk: safe.

### 3. Birth-star names form a closed class

**Claim.** The fish compounds on seals are personal names given from the asterism the moon occupied at birth. They therefore form a closed set of about 27 or 28 asterism names, with a few planets, used at nearly equal frequency.

**Reasoning.** The domestic ritual manuals of the Vedic schools prescribe a name taken from the birth asterism, Pāṇini's grammar (4.3.34) gives rules for such names, and many donors in early Brāhmī inscriptions bear names formed from asterisms. The moon passes through every asterism once a sidereal month, so births spread such names almost evenly over the set, changed only by fashion and auspicious choice. Ordinary name vocabularies are open and follow a steep Zipf distribution. A closed and flat class is a signature that names drawn from ordinary words rarely produce, and Parpola's 1990 study of astral names argued for the custom without turning it into a frequency prediction.

**Test.** We first measure the signature where the answer is known. From Lüders's *A List of Brahmi Inscriptions from the Earliest Times to about A.D. 400* (appendix to *Epigraphia Indica* vol. 10, 1912, out of copyright) we extract donor names, mark those formed from asterisms, and measure their share of names, the number of distinct asterisms used and the evenness of their frequencies as normalised entropy. We then freeze three predictions for Indus seals on the discovery half. Because the fish texts have been examined repeatedly, the score is read only on texts no analysis has seen: the 244 absent numbers of the CISI 3.1 range and inscriptions published after the catalogues ([Q-1026](../questions/Q-1026-post-catalogue-finds.md)).

- N1. A Chao1 estimate of the number of distinct fish compounds in name position lies between 24 and 45, while other opening vocabulary of the same total frequency keeps growing.
- N2. The normalised entropy of fish-compound frequencies exceeds that of frequency-matched opening vocabulary in at least 95% of draws and falls within the range measured on the Brāhmī asterism names.
- N3. Three asterisms are named in former and latter pairs (Pūrva- and Uttara-Phalgunī, -Āṣāḍhā and -Bhadrapadā), with dual names in the Vedic lists. One modifier contrast in the fish family applies to exactly three bases, and both members of each pair occur at similar frequency.

**Kill condition.** Fish compounds follow the same accumulation curve and Zipf slope as other opening vocabulary. The preliminary fish-context output already shows the two-stroke prefix on nine fish codes, so the stroke pair is not a dual confined to three asterisms; that post-outcome observation removes only the simplest version of N3. Risk: long shot.

### 4. The towns were laid out on the stars

**Claim.** Harappan builders aligned the main axes of new towns on the setting point of a bright star or star group, so a town's deviation from the cardinal directions records when it was laid out.

**Evidence so far.** Wanzke measured the architectural axes of Mohenjo-daro at 1–2° clockwise from the cardinal directions and proposed that they point to the setting of a star on the western horizon (*Interim Reports* vol. 2, pp. 33–44; the argument is on p. 37). His table gives the setting points north of west in 2500 and 1500 BCE: Aldebaran −2.7° and +3.4°, the Pleiades +2.2° and +7.2°, Procyon +4.9° and +7.5°. He chose Aldebaran, which places the alignment in 2000–1600 BCE, centuries after the city was founded, and set the Pleiades aside as too faint to observe at setting. His own table puts the Pleiades at +2.2° in 2500 BCE, close to the measured offset at the time the city was laid out, and the Pleiades are the asterism Parpola places at the head of the Harappan calendar. Wanzke ended by noting that the theory could be verified only by calculating exact orientations at other Harappan sites; we have not found a study that did so.

**Test.** Two measurers working independently take the axis bearings of every Harappan town with an orthogonal layout (Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira, Surkotada, Banawali, Rakhigarhi, Chanhu-daro, Kanmer, Shikarpur and others) from georeferenced satellite images and published plans. For each town we model the horizon profile and atmospheric extinction and compute the rising and setting azimuths of the candidate stars at the dated founding phase, using radiocarbon phase models where they are published. The star is chosen on Mohenjo-daro, Harappa and Kalibangan and frozen before the other towns are scored.

**Predictions and kill condition.** If the Pleiades set the axes, every town deviates clockwise, by about 0.5° more for each century of later founding. If Aldebaran did, the deviation changes sign around 2050 BCE, anticlockwise in early towns and clockwise in late ones. A solar or topographic layout predicts no relation to date. The claim is dead if deviation and founding date are uncorrelated across at least eight towns.

**Data.** A new table of settlements with axis bearings and their measurement error, horizon profiles and founding dates. It extends the object record to the places objects came from and fits a data-acquisition question card. Risk: long shot.

### 5. The Vedic asterism list is a translated Harappan list

**Claim.** The asterism list headed by the Pleiades is a Harappan calendar carried into Vedic tradition, and some Sanskrit asterism names translate Dravidian names.

**Test.** For each asterism we align the earliest Sanskrit name (Taittirīya Saṃhitā 4.4.10, Atharvaveda 19.7) with its Old Tamil names and check the Iranian record, which has no lunar-mansion calendar. A name translated from a source language is transparent in that language and often opaque in the borrowing one, so the claim predicts Dravidian names that are transparent where the Sanskrit is opaque more often than the reverse. We also recompute the dates implied by the astronomical statements, the Kṛttikās that "do not swerve from the east" (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 2.1.2.3) and the Pleiades at the equinox, with modern precession, extinction and the range of directions "east" could have meant, and check whether they fall within 2600–1900 BCE.

**Kill condition.** Old Tamil asterism names turn out to be translations from Sanskrit at least as often as the reverse. Most attested Tamil asterism names are Sanskrit loans; the test turns on native names such as *aṟu-mīn*. Risk: stretch.

### 6. "Star" at the root of the Dravidian tree

**Claim.** Proto-Dravidian *mīn* meant both "fish" and "star", so the pun was available to speakers in the third millennium BCE.

**Reasoning.** Parpola checks that a word occurs in more than one subgroup. A dated tree allows a sharper question. Kolipakam and colleagues estimate the age of the Dravidian family at about 4,500 years ([Royal Society Open Science 5: 171504, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.171504)), which puts the breakup of the proto-language within the Mature Harappan period. A reading that needs a meaning found only in South Dravidian would make the Indus language an early South Dravidian dialect, although the northern branch (Brahui, Kurukh, Malto) is the one nearest the Indus.

**Test.** We code the meanings of every word in Parpola's readings across the languages of the published tree using DEDR, and estimate the probability that each meaning was present at the root and at the North Dravidian node.

**Predictions and kill condition.** The meaning "star" for *mīn* has a root probability above 0.5, and across the readings the needed meanings reach the root more often than meanings drawn at random from the same dictionary entries. The reading of the fish sign as "star" in a third-millennium language is dead if that meaning is confined to South Dravidian with a root probability below 0.2. Risk: stretch.

### 7. Seal legends beside head-final seal legends

**Claim.** Indus seal legends have the structure of seal legends written in a language that puts modifiers before their heads and marks grammar with suffixes, as Parpola argues from the numerals that precede the fish.

**Test.** This meets the retry condition of STRUCTURAL-TYPOLOGY-2026-10-12. We build at least two head-final and two head-initial seal-legend corpora and measure the same statistics at sign and word level: early Tamil-Brāhmī seals and rings (Mahadevan 2003) and the Prakrit seals and sealings from Basarh and Bhita published in the Archaeological Survey's annual reports of 1903–1914, against Ur III and Old Akkadian seal legends from the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative and West Semitic stamp seals (Avigad and Sass, *Corpus of West Semitic Stamp Seals*, 1997). We keep the statistics that separate the two groups consistently and then place the Indus legends, segmented with the units of the [seal-legend units package](../../experiments/2026-10-20-seal-legend-units/PROTOCOL.md).

**Prediction and kill condition.** The Indus legends fall with the head-final group. The claim is dead if they fall with the head-initial group on every separating statistic. All South Asian candidates are head-final, so a pass excludes Sumerian, Akkadian and Elamite as the language of the legends without choosing among Dravidian, Munda and Indo-Aryan. Risk: stretch.

### 8. Meluhhan words in cuneiform

**Claim.** The words and names that Mesopotamian scribes recorded from Meluhha, their name for the Indus region, are Dravidian.

**Test.** We collect every term recorded as Meluhhan or for Meluhhan goods, and every personal name of a person identified as Meluhhan, from the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary, Oracc, CDLI and the Ur III database BDTNS. The study builds on Parpola and Parpola (1975) on Meluhha and Sanskrit *mleccha*, Parpola, Parpola and Brunswig (1977) on the Meluhha village at Lagash, and Mukhopadhyay's derivation of Mesopotamian words for elephant and ivory from Dravidian *pīlu* ([Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 8: 193, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-021-00868-w)). Each item is scored against the candidate languages with the base rates of study 1.

**Prediction and kill condition.** The Dravidian likelihood ratio exceeds that of each alternative; the claim is dead if another candidate scores as well or better. These are the only words of the Indus region written down by contemporaries in a script we can read, and the list may hold only a few dozen items. Risk: long shot.

### 9. The star mark leaves the repertoire

**Claim.** When the fish sign took on the meaning "star", the separate star mark dropped out of use.

**Reasoning.** Parpola explains the absence of a star sign from the script, although a star motif is painted and incised on Early Harappan pottery, as economy: the fish sign already covered "star". At Harappa, marks scratched on pottery run from about 3300 BCE through the Ravi, Kot Diji and Harappan phases, and some became signs of the script.

**Test and kill condition.** From the Harappa Archaeological Research Project's pot-mark records by phase, we measure the shares of star marks and fish marks against the other early marks. The claim predicts that the star mark declines as the fish mark rises across the transition from the Kot Diji to the Harappan phase, while other early marks keep their rates; it is dead if the star mark keeps its earlier rate in Harappan-phase pot marks. Phase labels are partly held from CHRONOLOGY-REGION-01; the mark tables need acquisition. Risk: stretch.

### 10. Fish compounds in the graffiti of Tamil Nadu

**Claim.** The fish compounds of the Indus seals survive, in their order, in marks scratched on Iron Age and Early Historic pottery in Tamil Nadu.

**Test.** Rajan and Sivanantham's *Indus Signs and Graffiti Marks of Tamil Nadu* (Tamil Nadu Department of Archaeology, 2025) digitises marks on more than 15,000 sherds from 140 sites and reports Indus parallels for 60% of 42 base signs ([review at Harappa.com](https://www.harappa.com/content/indus-signs-and-graffiti-marks-tamil-nadu)). Simple marks arise independently in many traditions, so resemblance of single shapes cannot show descent, while a shared order of compound parts can. Coders who have not seen Indus texts map the fish-like marks and their attachments; we then test whether the fish compounds follow the order frozen by the fish-context study more often than chance, with Egyptian and Aegean potters' marks as unconnected comparisons.

**Kill condition.** The graffiti contain no fish compounds, or their compounds do not follow the frozen order. The study also meets part of the retry condition of BACKWARD-CHRONOLOGY-01, which asks for dated intermediate repertoires. Risk: long shot.

### 11. An evidence ledger for the language question

Parpola's case for Dravidian combines independent lines: Brahui, Rigvedic loanwords and retroflex consonants, the later distribution of languages and the structure of the script. Ancient DNA has since added another. The genome from Rakhigarhi and the individuals of the "Indus Periphery Cline" carry no steppe ancestry, and that ancestry contributes to present-day South Indian populations (Shinde and colleagues, *Cell* 179: 729–735, 2019; Narasimhan and colleagues, *Science* 365: eaat7487, 2019). Witzel's analysis of the Rigvedic substrate points instead to a "Para-Munda" language in the Punjab (*Electronic Journal of Vedic Studies* 5(1), 1999).

We propose a ledger that gives, for each line of evidence, a likelihood ratio among the candidate languages (Dravidian, Munda or Para-Munda, Indo-Aryan, a relative of Burushaski, an isolate), states the assumptions behind it and computes which single new observation would move the result most. Ancestry does not determine language, so the genetic ratio has to be estimated from documented cases of language shift. The ledger is for choosing acquisitions and tests nothing by itself. Risk: tooling.

## The integrated model

The boldest reading these studies could support is this. The Harappans named people after the asterism of their birth, and the fish compounds on their seals are those names, built by a small grammar of modifiers on the word *mīn*. The same sky set the axes of their towns, and their asterism list, headed by the Pleiades, passed into the Vedic calendar. We put the whole model at a credence of about 0.05. It makes four predictions that can each fail on material nobody has examined: a closed and flat class of fish compounds in the fresh CISI 3.1 pool (study 3), axis deviations that grow with founding date across Harappan towns (study 4), a root-level meaning "star" for *mīn* (study 6) and an interlock network that random lexicons rarely reproduce (study 1). A failure of study 3 or study 4 kills the model as stated.

## Order of work

Studies 1, 2 and 6 use dictionaries and trees, not Indus texts, so they spend none of the corpus and can start now. Study 3 should freeze its predictions on the discovery half now and wait for the fresh CISI 3.1 pool. Study 4 is a data-acquisition task that produces a new settlement table. Studies 7, 8, 9 and 10 each need a comparison corpus to be acquired first. Each study runs in its own dated package under the start-study and test-conjecture skills, and its records of new sources and fields go into that package for the maintainers to fold into [DATA.md](../DATA.md).
