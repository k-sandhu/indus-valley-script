# What the 12 October tests established

Written 12 October 2026, after the published-claim tests, the seal-text identity study, the structure-first program and the Bronze Age comparison had reported. It records what these studies show when read together, for use before choosing the next study. Each statement links to the study that measured it.

## Fitting values to the corpus is cheap

Every flexible fit we tried on the Indus corpus succeeds, for any language. Random consonant strings can be read as dictionary words in 92% to 100% of cases when the reader may supply vowels and word divisions ([published-claim tests](../../experiments/2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/sanskrit-key/REPORT.md)). When each inscribed face (one side of an object) is taken as a word, words that repeat units where the face repeats signs cover 91% to 97% of faces for Sumerian, Akkadian, Egyptian and Greek inventories ([Bronze Age comparison](../../experiments/2026-10-12-bronze-age-comparison/REPORT.md)). Rearranging a published key's own values raises its Sanskrit order gain, the bits per consonant a Sanskrit language model saves over letter frequencies, from −0.39 to about +0.2. Keys fitted on half the inscriptions transfer to the other half equally well under Sanskrit, Latin, Old Tamil and Sumerian models, also after near-duplicate texts are removed. A proposed key or meaning map is informative only when it stands far above rearrangements of itself: in artificial Indus-shaped corpora encoded from known languages with a homophonic key, in which several signs write one sound, the correct key stands 27 to 72 standard deviations clear. The [key calibration memo](key-calibration-2026-10-12.md) gives the procedure.

## What the corpus does have

The inscriptions repeat exact sign sequences: 37% of the three-sign sequences (trigrams) in one half of the distinct texts occur in the other half, against 8% to 10% for homophonic encodings of running text in four languages. Texts end with signs from a small closing class and begin with many different signs ([structure-first step 6](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step6-language/REPORT.md)). Where the catalogue divides a text into lines, the divisions fall between units that recur elsewhere ([step 3](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step3-segmentation/REPORT.md)). Stroke groups are partly fixed to the neighbouring sign ([step 4](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step4-numerals/REPORT.md)), and on tablets a recurring text stays with one class of image ([step 2](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step2-image-text/REPORT.md)).

On seals, long texts are almost all unique. None of 445 seals with six or more signs shares its text with another seal, and identical texts of four or more signs are only about five times as common as a sequence model of the corpus predicts. The 65 texts that do recur are mostly short. Duplicate pairs within Mohenjo-daro occur as often as chance predicts, while some duplicates at smaller sites stay in one place ([seal-text identity](../../experiments/2026-10-12-seal-text-identity/REPORT.md)). Identical texts share the animal carved on the seal only on unicorn seals. Most sealings (clay impressions of seals) carrying a seal text match seals from other cities, and ten Lothal sealings with an elephant carry the text of a Mohenjo-daro seal fragment recorded with a unicorn. Round seals of the Gulf type, found abroad and in South Asia, put common Indus signs in unusual orders ([foreign finds](../../experiments/2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/foreign-finds/REPORT.md)).

## How the descriptions fare

Read together, the seal texts look like individually composed legends built from parts used in every city, with a small stock of short texts that recur across cities and appear with different animals except on unicorn seals. This fits legends of names with titles or roles, and role formulae. It fits less well a corpus whose duplicates are mostly owned by single local holders, although some duplicates at smaller sites may be of that kind. It does not fit a script that spells sounds with many freely interchangeable signs: signs given the same value in the published Sanskrit key do not substitute for one another, and exact sequences recur far more than free homophony would allow. No measurement chooses a language.

## Where effort now pays

1. Physical checks of specific objects: the Lothal L-161 sealings against seal M-895, the Gulf-type seal transcriptions, and the circular seals found in South Asia. Each can change a stated result directly.
2. Deposits that contain both seals and sealings, where the same issuer can appear on both, to tell an issuer's identity from a shared formula.
3. Segmentation trained on the catalogued line divisions, so that recurring units can be counted as units rather than as sign sequences.
4. A calibrated comparison of seal legends with traditions in head-final languages, in which the main word of a phrase comes last (Prakrit or Tamil-Brahmi seals and sealings), before any argument about language type.
5. Fitting keys or meanings only with the calibration procedure, and only after boundaries and units are fixed.
