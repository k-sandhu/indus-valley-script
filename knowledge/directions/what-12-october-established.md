# What the 12 October tests established

Written 12 October 2026 after the published-claim tests, the seal-text identity study, the structure-first program and the Bronze Age comparison had reported. It records what these packages show when read together, for use before choosing the next study. Each statement links to the package that measured it.

## Fitting values to the corpus is cheap

Every flexible fit tried on the Indus corpus succeeds, for any language. Random consonant strings pass a lexical readability test with free vowels and word division in 92% to 100% of cases ([published-claim tests](../../experiments/2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/sanskrit-key/REPORT.md)); equality-pattern word matches cover 91% to 97% of faces for Sumerian, Akkadian, Egyptian and Greek inventories ([Bronze Age comparison](../../experiments/2026-10-12-bronze-age-comparison/REPORT.md)); rearranging a published key's own values raises its Sanskrit order gain from −0.39 to about +0.2 bits per consonant; and keys fitted on half the inscriptions transfer to the other half equally under Sanskrit, Latin, Old Tamil and Sumerian models, also after near-duplicate texts are removed. A proposed key or meaning map is informative only when it stands far above rearrangements of itself; on synthetic Indus-shaped encodings a correct homophonic key stands 27 to 72 standard deviations clear. The [key calibration memo](key-calibration-2026-10-12.md) gives the procedure.

## What the corpus does have

The inscriptions repeat exact sign sequences: 37% of the sign trigrams in one half of the distinct texts occur in the other, against 8% to 10% for homophonic encodings of running text in four languages. They end in a small closing class and begin with many different signs ([structure-first step 6](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step6-language/REPORT.md)). Catalogued line divisions coincide with recurring units ([step 3](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step3-segmentation/REPORT.md)), stroke groups are partly fixed to neighbouring signs ([step 4](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step4-numerals/REPORT.md)), and image classes on tablets carry recurring texts ([step 2](../../experiments/2026-10-12-structure-first/step2-image-text/REPORT.md)).

On seals, long texts are almost all unique: none of 445 seals with six or more signs shares its text with another seal, and identical texts of four or more signs exceed a sequence model of the corpus only about fivefold. The 65 texts that do recur are mostly short. At Mohenjo-daro they are placed as freely as chance would place them, while some duplicates at smaller sites stay in one place ([seal-text identity](../../experiments/2026-10-12-seal-text-identity/REPORT.md)). Identical texts share the field animal only on unicorn seals. Most sealings carrying a seal text match seals from other cities, and ten Lothal sealings with an elephant carry the text of a Mohenjo-daro seal fragment recorded with a unicorn. Round Gulf-type seals, abroad and in South Asia, put common Indus signs in unusual orders ([foreign finds](../../experiments/2026-10-12-published-claim-tests/foreign-finds/REPORT.md)).

## How the descriptions fare

Read together, the seal texts look like individually composed legends built from parts used in every city, with a small stock of short texts that recur across cities and appear with different animals except on unicorn seals. That fits legends of names with titles or roles, and role formulae, better than duplicates that are mostly owned by single local holders, although some duplicates at smaller sites may be of that kind, and it does not fit a script that spells sounds with many freely interchangeable signs: same-value signs in the published Sanskrit key do not substitute for one another, and exact sequences recur far more than free homophony would allow. No measurement chooses a language.

## Where effort now pays

1. Physical checks of specific objects: the Lothal L-161 sealings against seal M-895, the Gulf-type seal transcriptions, and the circular seals found in South Asia. Each can change a stated result directly.
2. Deposits that contain both seals and sealings, where the same issuer can appear on both, to tell an issuer's identity from a shared formula.
3. Segmentation trained on catalogued line divisions, so that recurring units can be counted as units rather than sign sequences.
4. A calibrated seal-legend comparison with head-final traditions (Prakrit or Tamil-Brahmi seals and sealings) before any typological language argument.
5. Fitting keys or meanings only with the calibration procedure, and only after boundaries and units are fixed.
