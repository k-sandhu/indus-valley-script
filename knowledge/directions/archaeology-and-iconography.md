# What the objects can tell us

Research notes, October 2, 2026.

A seal was something a person carried, pressed, stored, lost or discarded. A tablet could have passed between people; a pot could have held goods; a sealing could have recorded access to a container. Starting with those actions gives us useful questions about the inscriptions. Who needed the mark? What did the recipient need to recognize? Which details stayed constant when the owner, goods or destination changed?

We can approach the writers through the practical problems their objects helped them solve. The most promising first targets are object use, production history and repeated transactions. These offer observations we can collect alongside the text.

## Sealing archives and transactions

Frenez's 2024 study re-examines the Lothal sealings and proposes an administrative archive. This creates a tractable research target: a bounded collection whose object relationships and deposition history can be studied together. The publisher and institutional abstracts are available; the specimen-level argument still needs to be read. [Study](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02666030.2024.2401206), [institutional record](https://repository.iitgn.ac.in/entities/publication/8b5a98f0-16f1-4a53-b2f9-dd19c8bb4986).

Imagine an official checking a shipment. The front might identify a person or institution; the reverse could preserve the container or fastening to which the seal was attached. Repeated fronts on different attachments could reveal the reach of one authority. Different fronts on the same kind of attachment could reveal a shared activity.

The first experiment would pair each inscription with its reverse impression, material, dimensions and find context. Two readers would classify the attachment traces with the inscription covered. Categories might include cord, fabric, container surface and a peg-like fixture. We would then test whether text modules predict those categories after accounting for site, period and material. Holding out complete seal or die families would show whether the relationship extends to another set of objects.

A gain on that test would give us a functional association to investigate. A gain confined to one die would direct attention toward the production group. Both outcomes would help choose the next objects to inspect.

## Small graphic details may carry the distinction we need

Feet on a figure, the height of a stroke, the position of a fish modifier and the internal structure of a compound deserve close attention. They could reflect an engraver's habits, the demands of a material, or a distinction important to the user.

Jamison, Uesugi and Yamaguchi's 2023 multivariate study reports spatial and temporal variation in unicorn-seal attributes. It gives us a starting method for separating production style from other associations. The institutional abstract has been inspected; access to its full data would help define a replication. [Study record](https://okayama.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/unicorns-and-seals-new-multivariate-approaches-to-exploring-patte/), DOI 10.1016/j.ara.2023.100486.

We could select one feature, such as fish modifiers, and photograph examples under consistent scale and lighting. Record the feature, tool marks and carving style before consulting sequence contexts. Then compare two explanations: the variants follow a production group, or they follow an independently recorded object use across production groups. A new carving group would be the test set.

This fits a problem encountered in our first investigation: coarse sign classes sometimes conceal feet, stroke height and compound structure. Better feature records could change both the crosswalk and the statistical analysis. The next step is a small, carefully reviewed feature set with an epigrapher, followed by a frozen test on additional objects.

## Pictures and text as parts of one object

The animal or scene may be closely connected to the inscription. Candidate relationships include an animal label, an institution's emblem, a workshop device, a ritual association or a category of transaction. Paired surfaces and objects without the picture can help distinguish these possibilities.

For example, an institution might keep its text while varying the scene; a workshop might keep a carving style across several texts; an animal label might recur across different production groups depicting the same animal. Each explanation predicts a different pattern of repetition.

We would build a graph of verified paired surfaces, repeated devices, text modules and production relationships. Define the predicted equalities and differences on one part of the collection, then inspect withheld objects. Yadav's paired-module analysis supplies an existing starting point for this work. [Yadav 2013, pp. 93–95](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/201311/Nisha%20Yadav_Scripta%202013.pdf).

The unresolved fine-photo joins in our copper and tablet cases are a useful first batch. Resolving them would improve the observations before a larger model is fitted.

## Follow change through time

The Harappa record offers an opportunity to examine changing habits of inscription and use. Kenoyer and Meadow's HARP introduction distinguishes Ravi, Kot Diji and urban subperiods, documents production methods, and records excavation and registration identifiers. It also describes copied tablets and mould duplicates. [Primary introduction](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer-Meadow-2010-HARP.pdf).

A chronological study could ask when a graphic feature appears, which materials carry it, and whether its neighboring signs change. We would begin with securely dated cohorts and keep deposition, manufacture and use dates separate in the records. Matching samples for length, material, preservation and recovery method would make the comparison easier to interpret.

A useful first test would learn a change pattern from early and late cohorts, predict an intermediate cohort, and repeat at a second site. That would help distinguish a broad transition from a local production history.

For later Indian material, the interesting question is the path of transmission. Rare compound shapes, repeated combinations and intervening dated examples offer better targets than isolated simple shapes. A comparison could blind dates and regions during graphic coding, then test whether the resulting sequence fits a transmission model. Readable later scripts would provide controls for a known lineage. The February 2007 symposium volume on Indus and Tamil preserves proposals worth cataloguing for this purpose. [Volume](https://www.tamildigitallibrary.in/admin/assets/book/TVA_BOK_0010667_Indus_civilization_and_tamil_language.pdf).

## Quantities, capacities and physical contents

Containers and counts offer a route to narrower meanings. A convincing target would combine a legible numerical-looking group, an independently measured capacity or counted contents, and a clear object relationship.

Our first numerical audit found a scale ambiguity in the published vessel examples. A new test should therefore fix the candidate unit and sign counts before the target measurement is examined. Compare a proportional-count model with fixed capacity classes and administrative categories. Include similar vessels bearing different signs and use blinded shape reconstruction where the vessel is incomplete.

The unresolved M-1383 context and H904 historical correspondence are practical source tasks for this branch. Once those are settled, a separately measured object would be more informative than another fit to the same examples. [Saved numerical source audit](../../research/literature/metrology/numerical_source_audit.md).

## Wear, repairs and changes of ownership

An inscription altered during an object's life could be especially informative. A repaired sign, re-cut device or changed slot might show which information was durable and which could be updated.

Raking-light photography, reflectance transformation imaging or 3D surface records could establish the order of cuts. We would record intersections and wear before proposing a reading, then look for recurring patterns of replacement. Experimental carvings and damaged surfaces would help estimate how confidently cut order can be recognized.

This direction needs access to suitable objects and good surface documentation. A pilot on a few clear cases would establish whether a larger study is worthwhile.

## A practical starting sequence

1. Review a small set of original photographs to settle feet, stroke-height and compound distinctions in the current crosswalk.
2. Obtain specimen-level records for the Lothal sealing study and assess the available reverse impressions.
3. Build an object-use table with blind attachment labels, physical aliases and find-context confidence.
4. Freeze one prediction task and its baselines, then test on withheld object families.

The [context data plan](context-data-plan.md) specifies the proposed fields. The [roadmap](research-roadmap.md) places these tasks alongside numerical, cryptographic and comparative experiments.

## Working assumptions

The experiments above are proposals. The first investigation's results and source joins are recorded in the [literature retrospective](../retrospectives/literature.md). For each new test, we need a stable object identity, a source-backed context and independently recorded outcome labels. Chronological uncertainty, shared moulds and uncertain graphic features belong in the data, so their effect can be examined directly.
