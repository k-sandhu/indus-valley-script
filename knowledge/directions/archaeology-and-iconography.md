# What the objects can tell us

Research notes, October 2, 2026.

A seal was something a person carried, pressed, stored, lost or discarded. A tablet could have passed between people; a pot could have held goods; a sealing could have recorded access to a container. Starting with those actions gives us useful questions about the inscriptions. Who needed the mark? What did the recipient need to recognize? Which details stayed constant when the owner, goods or destination changed?

We can approach the writers through the practical problems their objects helped them solve. The most promising first targets are object use, production history and repeated transactions, because each offers observations we can collect alongside the text.

## Sealing archives and transactions

Frenez's 2024 study re-examines the Lothal sealings and proposes an administrative archive. This gives us a tractable research target: a bounded collection whose object relationships and deposition history can be studied together. The publisher and institutional abstracts are available; the specimen-level argument still needs to be read. [Frenez 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02666030.2024.2401206), [institutional record](https://repository.iitgn.ac.in/entities/publication/8b5a98f0-16f1-4a53-b2f9-dd19c8bb4986).

Imagine an official checking a shipment. The front of a sealing might identify a person or institution; the reverse could preserve the impression of the container or fastening to which it was attached. Repeated fronts on different attachments could reveal the reach of one authority. Different fronts on the same kind of attachment could reveal a shared activity.

The first experiment would pair each inscription with its reverse impression, material, dimensions and find context. Two readers would classify the attachment traces with the inscription covered. Categories might include cord, fabric, container surface and a peg-like fixture. We would then test whether text modules, recurring groups of signs, predict those categories after accounting for site, period and material. Holding out every impression of a seal or die together would show whether the relationship extends to another set of objects.

A gain on that test would give us a functional association to investigate. A gain confined to one die would direct attention toward the production group. Both outcomes would help choose the next objects to inspect.

## Small graphic details may carry the distinction we need

Feet on a figure, the height of a stroke, the position of a fish modifier and the internal structure of a compound deserve close attention. They could reflect an engraver's habits, the demands of a material, or a distinction important to the user.

Jamison, Uesugi and Yamaguchi's 2023 multivariate study reports spatial and temporal variation in the attributes of unicorn seals. It gives us a starting method for separating production style from other associations. We have inspected the institutional abstract; access to the full data would help define a replication. [Jamison, Uesugi and Yamaguchi 2023](https://okayama.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/unicorns-and-seals-new-multivariate-approaches-to-exploring-patte/), DOI 10.1016/j.ara.2023.100486.

We could select one feature, such as fish modifiers, and photograph examples under consistent scale and lighting. The feature, tool marks and carving style would be recorded before anyone looks at the texts in which the sign occurs. We would then compare two explanations: the variants follow a production group, or they follow an independently recorded object use across production groups. A new carving group would be the test set.

This addresses a problem from our first investigation: coarse sign classes sometimes conceal feet, stroke height and compound structure. Better feature records could change both the crosswalk that matches sign codes between catalogues and the statistical analysis. The next step is a small feature set reviewed carefully with an epigrapher, followed by a frozen test on additional objects.

## Pictures and text as parts of one object

The animal or scene may be closely connected to the inscription. Candidate relationships include an animal label, an institution's emblem, a workshop device, a ritual association or a category of transaction. Paired surfaces, and objects that carry the text without the picture, can help distinguish these possibilities.

Each explanation predicts a different pattern of repetition. An institution might keep its text while varying the scene; a workshop might keep a carving style across several texts; an animal label might recur across different production groups that depict the same animal.

We would build a graph of verified paired surfaces, repeated devices, text modules and production relationships. The predicted equalities and differences would be defined on one part of the collection, then checked on withheld objects. Yadav's paired-module analysis supplies an existing starting point for this work. [Yadav 2013, pp. 93–95](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/201311/Nisha%20Yadav_Scripta%202013.pdf).

Our copper and tablet cases whose joins still await confirmation from detailed photographs are a useful first batch. Resolving them would improve the observations before a larger model is fitted.

## Follow change through time

The Harappa record offers an opportunity to examine changing habits of inscription and use. Kenoyer and Meadow's HARP introduction distinguishes Ravi, Kot Diji and urban subperiods, documents production methods, and records excavation and registration identifiers. It also describes copied tablets and mould duplicates. [Kenoyer and Meadow 2010](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/Kenoyer-Meadow-2010-HARP.pdf).

A chronological study could ask when a graphic feature appears, which materials carry it, and whether its neighbouring signs change. We would begin with securely dated cohorts and keep the dates of deposition, manufacture and use separate in the records. Matching samples for length, material, preservation and recovery method would make the comparison easier to interpret.

A useful first test would learn a pattern of change from early and late cohorts, predict an intermediate cohort, and repeat the test at a second site. That would help distinguish a broad transition from a local production history.

For later Indian material, the question is the path of transmission. Rare compound shapes, repeated combinations and dated examples from the intervening period offer better targets than isolated simple shapes. A comparison could hide dates and regions from the coders of the graphics, then test whether the resulting sequence fits a model of transmission. Readable later scripts would provide controls with a known lineage. The February 2007 symposium volume on Indus and Tamil preserves proposals worth cataloguing for this purpose. [Indus civilization and Tamil language](https://www.tamildigitallibrary.in/admin/assets/book/TVA_BOK_0010667_Indus_civilization_and_tamil_language.pdf).

## Quantities, capacities and physical contents

Containers and counts offer a route to narrower meanings. A convincing target would combine a legible group of signs that looks numerical, an independently measured capacity or counted contents, and a clear relationship between the inscription and the object.

Our first numerical audit found that the published vessel examples leave the scale ambiguous. A new test should therefore fix the candidate unit and the sign counts before the target measurement is examined. It would compare a model in which the count is proportional to the contents with fixed capacity classes and with administrative categories. Similar vessels bearing different signs belong in the test, and shape reconstruction should be done blind where the vessel is incomplete.

Two source tasks come first in this branch: settling the find context of M-1383, and establishing whether H904 is the tablet cited under an older number. Once those are settled, a separately measured object would be more informative than another fit to the same examples. [Saved numerical source audit](../../research/literature/metrology/numerical_source_audit.md).

## Wear, repairs and changes of ownership

An inscription altered during an object's life could be especially informative. A repaired sign, a re-cut device or a changed position might show which information was durable and which could be updated.

Raking-light photography, reflectance transformation imaging or 3D surface records could establish the order of cuts. We would record intersections and wear before proposing a reading, then look for recurring patterns of replacement. Experimental carvings and damaged surfaces would show how reliably the order of cuts can be recognized.

This direction needs access to suitable objects and good surface documentation. A pilot on a few clear cases would establish whether a larger study is worthwhile.

## A practical starting sequence

1. Review a small set of original photographs to settle how the current crosswalk treats feet, stroke height and compounds.
2. Obtain specimen-level records for the Lothal sealing study and assess the available reverse impressions.
3. Build an object-use table with attachment labels assigned without sight of the inscription, the other catalogue numbers under which each object appears, and the confidence of each find context.
4. Freeze one prediction task and its baselines, then test on withheld groups of related objects.

The [context data plan](context-data-plan.md) specifies the proposed fields. The [roadmap](research-roadmap.md) places these tasks alongside numerical, cryptographic and comparative experiments.

## Working assumptions

The experiments above are proposals. The first investigation's results and source joins are recorded in the [literature retrospective](../retrospectives/literature.md). Each new test needs a stable object identity, a context backed by a source, and outcome labels recorded independently of the text. Chronological uncertainty, shared moulds and uncertain graphic features belong in the data, so that their effect can be examined directly.

A simulation of a flat stamp pressed into curved clay gives a concrete hypothesis about what survives. When the stamp meets the clay centrally, or when opposite tilts are equally common, the graphics in the first and last positions have the same distribution, because the model is symmetric under reversal. When the stamp always tilts the same way, the two distributions differ by a total variation distance (half the summed absolute difference) of .03085 among impressions with at least three visible graphics, the declared minimum length. Damage can also change which graphics are retained. The model supplies dimensions, visibility rules and a planned clay experiment; measurements of actual object surfaces can replace its invented graphic classes and contact assumptions.
